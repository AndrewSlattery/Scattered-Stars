# Landwick

At 13°S, 72°E: the coastal cultural centre of Arrhenos, sprawling along the shore where land gives way to brackish marshland and then the sea. The climate hits immediately—hot and humid, with sudden rain that steams off within the hour.

## Character

Landwick is where the planet's taste gets decided. Its fashion is what provincial teenagers measure their own cities against, its media houses make most of what Arrhenos watches, and its publishing sets the arguments the rest of the planet has secondhand, a season later. Hundreds of theatres and museums, art districts in every medium.

The standing tension is the old one everywhere art meets money. Landwick is at once the place where the new thing happens and the place where the new thing is packaged, priced and shipped—often within the same year and often by the same men—and the fights about which districts have gone over are the city's most reliable entertainment after the theatre itself.

## Neighbourhoods

The Corso is the promenade—the fashion houses, the media studios, and the long seafront colonnade where being seen is the activity. What the planet means by Landwick fashion is decided here, two seasons before it reaches Brovdingonai, a lag the Corso finds correct. The great media houses keep their production lofts in the streets behind, making the serials, the music, and the criticism-of-the-criticism the planet consumes. A thin prestige export of it travels the Fleet circuits.

The Folio is the publishing quarter: presses, review houses, the long arcaded book-lanes, and the cafés where the planet's arguments are staged before they are printed. Every school of thought keeps an organ here, and the feuds between journals run for decades and are conducted with a courtesy that fools nobody. The cafés seat by allegiance, the waiters know the factions, and a critic changing his usual table is information that reaches Campottonì by evening.

The Brackens is where the city gives out into the marsh: stilt-streets, houseboats, and tide-halls that flood on schedule and stage their theatre around the water's timetable. The experimental end of everything happens here, among companies too new or too strange for the Corso's houses and composers writing for spaces that are dry twice a day. The district's poverty is partly real and partly a rent the ambitious pay on purpose. The Corso raids it for talent annually.

Saltglass is the old harbour quarter and the seat of the wedding houses. Landwick stages weddings as theatre—days long, scored, costumed—and Saltglass is where the trade lives: the costumers and composers, the vow-writers, who are a profession with a guild, and the barge-stages that carry the ceremonies out onto the water at dusk. Families save for years and hire a house the way one hires an opera company, the great houses book seasons ahead, and the quarter's calendar of premieres is reviewed, seriously, in the Folio press.

## The Tep

Formally the Tepidarium Quarter: a neighbourhood in the south-eastern reach, where the old geothermal works used to run. It never quite cooled down, and old thermal pipes still leak enough warmth to keep the streets perpetually misty.

When the heating infrastructure was decommissioned eighty years ago, squatters and artists moved into the old pump stations and have been building upward ever since: crystalline composite additions grafted onto industrial brick, smart-fabric awnings strung between buildings never meant to touch, bioluminescent moss encouraged because it looks beautiful in the permanent fog.

The Baths are not separate from the neighbourhood but the neighbourhood itself. What used to be condensation chambers is now a three-dimensional city-within-a-city: interconnected pools on seven levels, with waterfalls cascading through floor openings. The sound of falling water is constant and becomes a kind of silence you stop noticing until you leave.

Men move through in all stages of undress. Someone waist-deep negotiates a contract with someone fully dressed on a walkway overhead. A noodle vendor parks his floating cart at the pool's edge. Residential alcoves are carved into the walls, some curtained and some completely open, with men sleeping, reading, and working at holographic interfaces while steam drifts past. People conduct entire lives in what other neighbourhoods would consider semi-public space.

Sorian's sits up on the third floor, where two mismatched buildings lean against each other. Its keeper, Thales Copper-Hand, lost three fingers to a fabricator accident and had them replaced with obvious bronze prosthetics he refuses to upgrade; his torc is remarkable for all the wrong reasons, its design reminiscent of the First Republic. The business is grey-market electronics—not illegal exactly, just unregistered. Torcs with the tracking stripped out, neural interfaces that do not report to the Ministry.

## Figures

Matthias Orren, 40, writes the Folio's most-read and most-resented column, and is one of Elio Webb's network of old university partners. His trade is the planet's taste, and his private conviction, aired only in the network's long threads, is that Landwick's genius and Landwick's racket are the same organ and he has made his living failing to tell them apart. He keeps a standing table at a café whose faction he refuses to confirm, which is itself a faction.

Berro and Sun Blackwell, 68 and 71, keep a Tep-edge household—a fabricator and a baths-keeper—whose youngest son Fenno tends bar on a freighter somewhere on the Hesperine Circuit and sends money home every pay cycle. They have stopped asking when he is coming back, which is not the same as having stopped wondering. The remittances have quietly made them the lenders of first resort for half their lane: a small, warm, unlicensed bank run on a spacer's wages, which Fenno does not know about.

Cadano Ruz, 55, is master of the Saltglass wedding house Ruz & Tide, three generations in the trade, composer of forty-one wedding-cycles, and the man the great families call when the occasion must be unforgettable and the taste beyond reproach. He has never married. "The cobbler's sons go barefoot. And I have seen what it costs done properly."
