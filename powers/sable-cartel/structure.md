# The Sable Cartel: Structure

The Cartel's structure is not a chain of command. It is an architecture of mutual distrust, and much of what outsiders mistake for cunning is the shape that distrust forces on it.

## The Thirteen

The Directors communicate through the Veil Protocol, which conceals their identities and locations from one another as much as from the galaxy. A body whose members may never have met, cannot freely confer, and cannot verify whom they are speaking to does not move like a single mind. When no Director can be overruled and none can be certain of the others, the default outcome is that nothing happens, and nothing happening is easily mistaken afterwards for patience.

Analysts who have given up naming individuals try instead to name tendencies. The Bureau's working model speaks of a lending tendency—conservative, protective of the Cartel's legitimate businesses and of the respectability they run on—pulling against an operational tendency that favours aggressive, high-margin plays and tolerates exposure. Most decisions, on this reading, are the residue of those two grinding against each other, with a large undecided middle settling wherever the last concession left it.

## The Veil Protocol

Each level of the organisation knows only its own operations, its immediate superior, and enough context to function. Lateral contact between equals is restricted. Nobody below the Directors can map more than a fragment, and the design assumes that anyone at any level may be compromised, including the people who built it. The Veil does not wrap a secret core; it protects everyone from everyone.

Two consequences follow. The Veil makes the Cartel slow and partly blind to itself, since the information that would let it coordinate is the same information that would let an investigator unravel it. And subsidiaries enjoy genuine autonomy not as a gift but as a side effect, because nobody above them can micromanage what nobody above them is permitted to see. A great deal of what the Cartel decides is thousands of local managers acting on local incentives.

## The hierarchy

| Directors             | strategic authority, such as it is       | most, or so it is assumed                       |
| Division Heads        | run a major line of business             | their division, and that the Director level exists |
| Regional Controllers  | coordinate a division across a region    | their region, and that Division Heads exist      |
| Subsidiary Directors  | run an individual company                | their company; may not know the parent exists    |
| Operational staff     | do the actual work                       | their job                                        |

The base of that pyramid is not a cadre but a population: tens of billions who load freight, underwrite policies, audit accounts, drive harvesters and answer service requests, most of whom will live and die without the word Cartel ever applying, in their own minds, to themselves.

The cost of the design shows at the seams. A Regional Controller who cannot see across the Veil cannot easily tell an honest subsidiary from one quietly looting itself, and a Division Head cannot be sure whether a rival division is competing with him by accident or by instruction. Local fiefdoms form and drift accumulates.

## The divisions

Each Division is a line of business with its own Head, its own subsidiaries, and its own interests, which do not align.

| Capital            | banking, lending, finance                    | wants stability, repayment, and counterparties that will still clear its paper |
| Transit            | shipping, logistics, infrastructure          | wants volume, open routes, and to stay indispensable  |
| Assurance          | insurance and risk                           | wants predictable risk and quiet conflicts            |
| Security           | private military and protection              | wants demand for protection, which means conflict     |
| Commodities        | resources, agriculture, fez-adjacent trade   | wants scarcity in what it holds, glut in what it buys |
| Intelligence       | information gathering and brokerage          | wants to know first, and to sell to all comers        |
| Special Operations | the deniable work                            | wants latitude, and to be feared more than used       |

Assurance prices a war on the assumption that it stays contained; Security profits if it spreads; both answer eventually to the same Directors and neither can order the other to stand down. Capital needs the Cartel to look respectable enough that ten thousand clerks in ten thousand offices go on clearing its paper without asking, and Special Operations needs it feared enough that nobody tests it. Intelligence sells to everyone, including—through enough intermediaries—one division's secrets to another.

The count of divisions is not fixed. Special Operations may be one division or a polite name for several, and at least one more is assumed to exist because some Cartel behaviour fits none of the seven.

## Origins

The Cartel's own account is clean and almost certainly false: a circle of financiers and shippers scattered and ruined by the Cascade, recognising that no single polity could be trusted with their survival, built an institution that belonged to none of them and had no heart for any government to seize.

The corporate archaeology is messier. The oldest subsidiaries carry registrations that predate the Cascade, a few traceable on paper to pre-collapse concerns like the Trans-Orion Mercantile. Others were plainly assembled later, in the disorder of the late 2800s, when orphaned companies could be had for nothing and folded into holding structures whose paperwork has long since rotted past reading. The likeliest history is not a founding at all but an accretion: a league of trading and banking firms that discovered, gradually, that acting as one when it suited them and disclaiming one another when it did not was worth more than any of them alone.

## Recruitment

The Cartel does not recruit so much as acquire. It buys companies, and their people keep working, frequently never told the ownership has changed.

Movement up the Veil is rarer. Individuals who show talent and discretion are offered a little more—access, money, responsibility—and with each step a clearer view of how far the structure runs. Most stop at the rung where the compensation still outweighs the unease. There is usually a moment when an employee grasps what they have been working for: an oddly specific instruction, a payment routed through a company with no business being involved, a colleague who knows something they should not. The Cartel pays close attention to how people take that moment. Curiosity is not punished; it is noted. Equanimity is promoted.

## Arbitration and betrayal

Disputes between divisions and subsidiaries—over territory, licences, poached staff, a contract two subsidiaries each thought was theirs—go to anonymous panels of senior personnel who hear the claims and impose binding outcomes. Penalties run from financial adjustment through forced restructuring to the quiet dissolution of an offending subsidiary. Subsidiary directors comply because the system is broadly fair within its narrow remit, and because open conflict between Cartel assets conducted in public is worse for every faction at once. It is closer to old merchant law than to anything in a corporate handbook.

Betrayal is the one matter arbitration does not touch. Passing information about the Cartel's structure to outsiders is handled by elements of Special Operations, and most leaks are handled with lawyers, money and quietly ruined careers, because that is cheaper and surer than anything dramatic. The dramatic cases exist—defectors who surfaced in another polity's protective custody and did not last, accidents a shade too convenient—and they are rare.
