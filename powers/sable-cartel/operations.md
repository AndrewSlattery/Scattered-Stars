# The Sable Cartel: Operations

Most of what the Cartel does is legal, and most of what it does is dull: freight moved, loans serviced, policies underwritten, crops brought in, warehouses kept. The illicit business is real and lucrative and lives in the margins and seams of the legitimate business rather than beneath it.

The line between the two is hard to find because it is not a line. It is a judgement made ten thousand times a day, by ten thousand managers, about particular cargoes and particular clients, and many of them get it wrong, or look away, or never see the question at all.

## Lending

The Capital Division, through Nexus Financial and its kind, lends where respectable finance will not go: to minor polities, independent stations and frontier ventures too small or too risky for the corridor houses. The terms are fair and often generous. The catch is the gravity that follows: a polity that banks with Nexus finds that Cartel shippers quote the best rates, Cartel insurers the best cover and Cartel security the best protection, until its economy is a Cartel economy and nobody can name the morning it changed.

That is the version that works, and it works often. Some borrowers default and drift into the network as intended; others default and simply repudiate, banking on the plain truth that the Cartel has no fleet to send and no court to appeal to. A frontier government that decides it would rather be poor and free than solvent and owned can walk away, and a handful have. The Cartel writes off the loss with a discipline that surprises people who expect vengeance.

## Logistics

The Transit Division, and Wayline Logistics above all, moves a meaningful fraction of everything that moves: thousands of freighters, hundreds of transit stations, fuel depots and warehouses across more systems than any rival services. On the frontier, where other carriers will not go, Wayline is often the only carrier there is.

A Wayline hub clears millions of legitimate containers for every one that hides something. The network is not a smuggling operation with a freight cover; it is a freight operation with a smuggling problem it has learned not to solve too thoroughly.

## Insurance

The Assurance Division writes the policies nobody else will: conflict-zone assets, political-risk cover, cargo through contested space. It occasionally insures both sides of the same war, which is endlessly cited as proof of some grand design.

Assurance insures both sides because it sells to anyone with a premium, and because the desk pricing the war is not told what the Security Division might be doing to start it. Far from orchestrating conflicts to collect on them, Assurance lives in dread of them, a war that escalates the wrong way being a column of claims. Its interests run directly against Security's, and the two cannot coordinate even where coordination would pay.

## Security

The Security Division fields private military through Aegis and others: convoy escort, facility protection, executive details, and where the client pays and the optics allow, line combat. The work is professional and the contractors are good, recruited heavily from Mandate veterans and Freehold militia. It is also, structurally, an intelligence operation, since a security contract is a key to a client's facilities, schedules and weaknesses.

It does not always go cleanly. A protection contract that turns into a massacre, a guard force that backs the losing side of a local war, a client who proves to be exactly the sort a respectable firm should have refused—when these happen, the Cartel's instinct is to disown the subsidiary, fast and completely.

## Smuggling

The same network that makes Transit indispensable makes smuggling nearly free. Routes are mapped, schedules are known, and inspection patterns are studied. Restricted technology, controlled substances, embargoed goods and occasionally people move inside the vast legitimate flow, timed to arrive where and when inspection is lightest. A customs officer boarding a Wayline freighter is hunting one wrong container among ten thousand right ones.

Customs services do win, through a manifest that will not reconcile, an informant, or a run of bad luck. The exposed subsidiary is cut loose, the route goes cold, the loss is absorbed, and the connection upward dies at the desk of a mid-ranking manager who knew better than to keep notes.

## Information

The Intelligence Division's product is knowledge—gathered from every loan file, security contract, shipping manifest and harvest report, then analysed, packaged and sold. Buyers include governments, corporations, militaries and private parties, and the Cartel sells to all of them without preference. A Mandate officer and a Freehold commander may buy reports drawn from the same source, each cut to their own appetite.

The product is imperfect. Sell contradictory intelligence to opposed buyers often enough and some of it is necessarily wrong, and the reputation for omniscience papers over a great deal of guesswork priced as certainty. Buyers know what they are buying from, and discount accordingly.

## Markets

The Commodities Division trades across many sectors, and its signature is fez. Its futures desk is the most aggressive participant in the market and its positions are uncannily well timed: a harvest known to be failing before the market prices it, a route known to be closing before the premiums rise. Rivals are certain this is manipulation, and proving it would mean tracing advance knowledge through thousands of subsidiaries to a single trade.

Much of it is not manipulation but luck wearing the Cartel's reputation. A desk that places a thousand bets will have a prophetic-looking run, and the firm is delighted to be credited with foresight it did not have. The fez desk has also been wrong, expensively, more than once, and the years it lost money are not the years it talks about.

## Special Operations

Assassination through deniable intermediaries, sabotage, political manipulation, the theft of guarded technology. The external evidence is thin.

Some of it is real. Much of what is laid at the Cartel's door is not: every unexplained death and convenient accident in human space acquires a Cartel rumour sooner or later, and the firm is in no hurry to deny work it never did. Special Operations is most powerful in the cases where it never had to act at all.
