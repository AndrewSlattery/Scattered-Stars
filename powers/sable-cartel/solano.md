# Solano

A farm the size of a world, some 190 million people growing grain, oilseed and engineered orchard crops with some processing. It is owned through Verdant Holdings, through four intermediate companies whose names appear on no local document, and through whatever sits above those, by the Sable Cartel.

Nobody on Solano knows this. The word Cartel occurs there the way it occurs everywhere, as a thing in thrillers, and nobody applies it to the grain company.

## The Company from underneath

The Company is not sinister; it is furniture. It surveyed the world, financed the settlement and built the towns. Its name is on the clinic, the school and the seed-store, and its buying price for grain is the number the whole year orbits.

Three generations have worked its land, mostly on terms the frontier would envy: steady wages, decent housing, pensions that pay out, and a company that—unlike the boom-and-bust operators two systems over—has never once failed to meet a payroll in a hundred and forty years. Solano is what patient money looks like when it is being patient: well fed, well schooled, unhurried, and unable to imagine leaving, since the Company owns the freight line, holds the mortgages and buys the crop.

The valley towns keep their own weather beneath the Company's: harvest festivals older than anyone's employment contract, lamp-shrines and chapel-rows in whatever mix each settlement's founders carried in, marriages that knit the valleys together and feuds that do the same. Solano's culture is farm culture, with a company for a sky.

## The seam

What Solano does for its owners, beyond the modest profit of feeding people, is report. Every weighbridge ticket, yield map, freight manifest and seed order flows upward as routine paperwork, and somewhere far above the local ledgers, aggregated across a hundred worlds like this one, it becomes the live map of supply that feeds the Intelligence Division and the fez desk.

Nobody on Solano spies. The paperwork is honest, filed by honest clerks. The town clerk of Verro Valley, who has filed forty years of impeccable harvest returns, has contributed more to the Cartel's commodity position than any operative, and would be bewildered and rather offended to hear it.

There is exactly one person on the world who has read the watermark: the Company's regional controller, who arrived eleven years ago from off-world, learned in her first year the shape of what she must not ask about, and has settled at the rung where the compensation still outweighs the unease. She runs Solano well. Some seasons she wonders, briefly, who she runs it well for.

## Visitors

Twice in living memory the outside world's idea of the Cartel has arrived on Solano looking for itself.

A Mandate trade attaché spent a season in the 3840s auditing the Company's export structure for the Bureau, and left with four hundred pages proving that Solano Agricultural is owned by a holding company in a jurisdiction where the trail goes dark.

Three years ago a Blackwell Media correspondent came through making a documentary about frontier agriculture, filmed the harvest festival at Verro Valley, interviewed the clerk and the schoolteacher and the controller, and produced an hour of genuinely lovely television about a world nobody had ever filmed. One Cartel arm documenting another, with neither aware of it.

## People

Ama Verro-Callis, 66, is harvest-master of the Verro Valley co-operative, which negotiates the towns' side of the buying price with the Company each year: the nearest thing Solano has to politics, conducted across a table in the seed-store loft, hard and civil, between parties who will both still be here in forty years. She has beaten the Company's opening price thirty-one seasons running. Asked by the Blackwell correspondent whether she ever wondered who owned the owners, she gave the answer that made the final cut. "Somebody owns everything, dear. I own the part I stand on at harvest."

Tomas Ru, 38, teaches in the high valleys, Company-salaried like everyone, his pay routed through four holding companies he has never heard of. He teaches the settlement's history—the survey, the landing, the first hard winters—from a Company-printed textbook he has begun quietly to annotate, because the archive box in the school cellar tells the first winters differently. There is nothing dangerous in the discrepancies. But he keeps annotating, and somewhere very far above him a filing has noted, without alarm and without action, that the Verro Valley school requisitioned access to pre-consolidation records.
