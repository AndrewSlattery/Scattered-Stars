# Marag

Marag is what most of the Mandate actually is, which is why nobody writes about it. A middling agricultural world in a middling coreward province, weeks off the courier trunks, it has been Imperial for seven centuries and would struggle to name the last time the Empire noticed. The throne world's files record it as orderly, productive, and quiet. All three are true, and none of them means what Tiamming thinks it means.

## The Mandate, at this distance

On Tiamming the empire is a system. On Marag it is **four buildings and a schedule**: the prefecture, the registry office, the Sapphire courthouse (local tier; the nearest Regional Court is a sector away), and the Bureau station—one chief, four assistants, and a continent apiece. The [prefect](../government.md) *is* the government here, exactly as the government files admit, and Marag has been lucky in its current one and knows from its grandmothers' stories what unlucky feels like.

The [citizen-subject line](../citizen-subject.md) runs through everything and weighs almost nothing daily—**spotty and personal**, in the official phrase the officials don't use. Half the world is subject; the registration bands are worn, where they are worn, pushed up the forearm like a bracelet; the checkpoint on the delta road has been staffed by the same family of citizens for three generations and waves through everyone it recognises, which is everyone. What the line still does, it does at the hinges of a life—the examination fees, the better postings, the weight of a word in court—and it does it as *custom* administered by neighbours, which is gentler than Parvati's version right up until it isn't, because a grievance here has no crowd to disappear into and no other checkpoint to try.

## Not one Marag

The world sorts into three, and resents being summarised as any of them.

**The delta** is the world the sector files mean: river cities, engineered grain plains, the barge culture that moves the harvest, the examination schools where delta families have bought their sons into the civil service—one or two a generation, celebrated on the town gates—for centuries. Delta Marag is where the Classical is spoken with real polish, where the Mandate is believed in as well as obeyed.

**The highlands** are older, drier, and devout: herding country, terrace villages, the lamp-shrines of the **Long Vigil** on every ridgeline. Highland Marag treats the state as weather—inevitable, seasonal, worth planning around—and pays its quotas punctually so as to be left alone, which it mostly is.

**Port Serat-Marag**, the one licensed spaceport, is a third country of perhaps two million: off-world manners, Trade Tongue signage, the warehouses, the recruiting office, and everything on the world that is less than a decade old. The port thinks the delta is smug and the highlands are a museum. The rest of the world calls port people *the hurried*, and means it as diagnosis.

## The Vigil

The registries record Marag's faith as *regional folk practice, lamp-tending, ancestral*—the Ministry of Rites' polite fiction, extended to a tradition that is in fact a theology seven centuries deep, with its own schools, its scandalous mystics, its long doctrinal quarrel between the delta's easy observance and the highlands' strict one. The **Long Vigil** holds that every settled world is a lamp lit against the dark between the stars, and that the tending of the lamp—the fields, the children, the dead, the flame itself—is one continuous act of keeping faith. Every household keeps a flame; every village keeps a Nightwatch; and once a year at the **Lamp Feast**, the whole world banks its fires down to a single flame per town and sits with the dark it is refusing.

The Ministry counts the Lamp Feast as Misrule, because it falls near the Gap and involves licensed disorder, and is wise enough not to look closer. The Vigil, which predates the Modern Mandate's arrival here and expects to outlast much else, files the paperwork the fiction requires and keeps its voice down, as instructed, and as it would have done anyway. A faith about tending lamps has patience as a sacrament.

## What arrives, and when

The trunk news reaches Marag weeks old and matters accordingly. [Vega serials](../culture.md) arrive by the season-crate, years stale, and are loved without urgency—the delta towns hold viewing nights for episodes Lyra forgot a decade ago, and the local drama tradition (barge-plays, performed on the harvest water) borrows their plots and improves, everyone agrees, on their endings. The [Yansieve news](../yansieve-rebellion.md) arrives the same way, and lands differently: the port reads it like the port reads everything, loudly; the delta discusses it carefully at dinner, in Maragi; and the highlands say nothing at all, which the Bureau's station chief dutifully reports, and cannot interpret, because the highlands have said nothing at all about everything for seven hundred years.

The military road runs through here too. The recruiting circuit takes several thousand of Marag's subject sons each year—twenty years for a legal transformation their children will inherit—and the **veterans' lodges** in the delta towns are where they come home to: citizens now, pensioned, listened to, and quietly the most attentive readers of the frontier news on the planet. The empire taught them its arithmetic. They are doing it.

## People

**Prefect Ren Baiyu** (58) — Xuanese-educated, examination-risen, posted here twenty-one years ago as a rung on a ladder he stopped climbing. He governs well by the only method distance allows: through the delta families, the Vigil's Nightwatches, and the veterans' lodges, spending his tiny budget where the world tells him it hurts. His sector reports are models of the form—orderly, productive, quiet—and are, he would admit after the second cup, works of translation: the true state of Marag, rendered into sentences Tiamming will not misread into intervention. He has been offered promotion twice and pleaded health. His health is excellent.

**Varza Oduyan** (74) — Nightwatch-mother of the high terraces, which makes her, in the state's eyes, a folklore practitioner, and in fact the senior religious authority for forty million people. She has met the prefect eleven times in twenty years, each visit recorded as *cultural liaison*. Between them they have settled water disputes, a famine winter, and one very quiet matter involving a Bureau assistant who overstepped, and their mutual regard is the true constitution of the highlands. She tends the oldest lamp on the world. She has opinions about the dark between the stars, and about the Empire, and considers them related subjects.

**Sero Mkren** (44) — twenty years in the Imperial Army, citizenship earned at the standard price, home now with a pension, a limp acquired at a place he names only when drunk, and the delta's most crowded veterans' lodge table. He is loyal, in the way of a man who paid too much for something to call it worthless—and he follows the Yansieve dispatches with the specific attention of someone checking a sum. Asked by the prefect, whom he likes, what the lodges think of the frontier, he gave the answer that went, lightly edited, into a sector report: *"We think what soldiers think. That it could have been us, both ways."*
