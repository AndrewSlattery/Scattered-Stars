# Yansieve: Voices Through the Blockade

**A compiler's warning.** Everything in the entry on [the Yansieve Rebellion](yansieve-rebellion.md) is what can be established. This appendix is the rest: what comes *out* of the blockaded systems, by the routes such things travel—smugglers' logs, intercepted broadcasts, refugee testimony, the Bureau's own paper. None of it can be verified. The blockade exists precisely so that none of it can be verified. The documents below disagree with one another on nearly every important point, and the disagreement is the most accurate picture available.

---

## I. From the log of a Freehold gun-runner (3861 C, sold at Veritas Station)

*The author ran the blockade at least four times for the arms trade; the log was sold, in the frontier way, once it was stale enough to be safe.*

Third run into Kessa Landing. What you hear in the Anchorage bars is that they're eating their boots in there, and what I can report is that the stevedores who offloaded us were thin and fast and paid on time, in kind, in rare-earth concentrate at a fair weight. Ribs on show, yes. Starving men don't work double shifts and they don't *sing* while they do it — and they sing, some anthem of theirs, new words to an old Mandate harvest tune, which struck me as either very fine or very sad, and I've not decided.

What nobody tells you: the checkpoints are theirs now, and they run them exactly like the Bureau ran the old ones, wave-through for known faces and a cold hour for strangers. I said as much to my broker and she said, *you build with the bricks you were raised in.* No bands, though. First port I've ever worked where nobody's wrist says what they are.

## II. Broadcast fragment, the Free Systems Assembly (intercepted 3860 C; authenticity disputed)

*Rebel addresses are relayed system to system inside the cluster; this fragment reached the outside on a data drive whose chain of custody the Bureau itself calls "improvable." The speaker is a woman; she names herself only as a delegate for Merrow's World.*

…and they will tell you we are starving, and some seasons they are right. I will not stand here in front of the families of this district and pretend the winter was gentle. But I ask you to remember what we paid *before*, every year, in the quiet way that never made anyone's news — the sons who went into the registry queues and came out smaller, the wages that weighed less because our word weighed less, the whole long tax of being a subject people. We have exchanged a tax we paid forever for a hunger that ends. That is the whole of the bargain and I would sign it again…

*[The fragment ends mid-sentence. Three separate versions circulate, with three different endings. This is the shortest.]*

## III. Bureau of Internal Harmony, sector assessment (excerpt, 3861 C, classification lapsed in error)

4.3. The Assembly's cohesion continues to be misread by optimistic parties within [REDACTED]. Factional divisions (negotiationist / separatist / the Merrow tendency) are genuine but are not, at present, load-bearing: external pressure remains the strongest unifying force acting on the rebel population, and the blockade is that pressure. The Directorate is invited to consider, as it has been invited before, that our principal instrument may be manufacturing the cohesion it is deployed to erode.

4.4. Food security in the inner systems is worse than rebel broadcasts admit and better than our own projections require. The projections have been revised four times. The Directorate is referred to the revision history rather than to the current figure.

4.7. On the question of wider sympathy: sampling of subject-population sentiment in [REDACTED] and Parvati remains within tolerances. The tolerances have been widened twice.

## IV. Testimony of a refugee, recorded at Haven Ascendant (Chronicle Service deposit, 3861 C; unsealed by the depositor's instruction)

*A man of about fifty, formerly a water engineer on Kessa. He paid a runner for passage out in the rebellion's second year; his deposit is among the few first-person accounts available to outsiders, and he asked that it be open, "because everything else from home is sealed one way or the other."*

People ask me which side I fled. Write this down exactly: I fled the *war*, not a side. On Kessa I repaired the same pumping station under three flags in two years — the Empire's, then the council's, then the council's again after the faction quarrel, which outsiders have never heard of and which cost forty lives in my town alone. The council men were my neighbours and they were brave and some of them were fools, and the second year they shot a hoarder who was not hoarding, and also they tore the registry office down with their hands and I cried like a child with everyone else. Both things. You want it simple and I cannot help you.

What I miss — and I know how this sounds, an old subject missing anything of that place — is the *morning after the sweep ended*, the first morning, before the blockade, before the shortages. Nobody working. Everybody in the street. A whole town standing in the road looking at each other, thinking: whatever we are now, we are not *filed*. I have been safe on this beautiful ship for a year and I have never once felt that again.

## V. Letter home from an Imperial Navy rating, blockade duty (3862 C; passed by the censor)

*Ordinary post from the cordon, censored and legal; thousands like it reach Tiamming each rotation. This one was shared by the family, which is how such things become documents.*

…mostly it is nothing, love, you must not imagine battles. It is watching a green world through instruments for ten hours and logging traffic that never comes. Sometimes a runner tries the shell and mostly we let the interdiction net do the work and mostly they turn back. Chao says we are the cork in a bottle the Emperor does not want to drink from or pour out, and Chao has been on report twice for saying cleverer things than that, so I only think it.

I will tell you the one thing, and the censor may keep it if he likes because it is only true: on the dark side of the third world you can see their cities. Lit. Four years, and the lights are still on down there. Nobody in this mess hall says anything about it, but everybody looks.

---

## What the documents agree on

Almost nothing—except this. The lights are on; the checkpoints have changed hands and not disappeared; the hunger is real and unevenly told; the factions are real and not yet fatal; and everyone, on every side of the shell, is watching everyone else and doing arithmetic. Beyond that, the reader stands where the whole Mandate stands: outside a sealed volume, choosing which testimony to believe, in the knowledge that the seal itself was built so that the choosing would never end.
