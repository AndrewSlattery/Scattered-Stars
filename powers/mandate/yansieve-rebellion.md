# The Yansieve Rebellion

Twelve to fifteen trailing frontier systems—the count is disputed, including by the rebels—have been in open revolt since 3858 C. They have abolished the citizen-subject distinction in the territory they hold, and demand its abolition throughout the Mandate or, failing that, independence.

## Origins

The Yansieve cluster was settled late, administered lightly, and worked largely by subjects in extractive labour. Its grievances were the ordinary grievances of the far frontier: disproportionate taxation, infrastructure that never arrived, long neglect punctuated by sudden arbitrary attention. Most of the Mandate's frontier could say the same and does not rebel.

In 3858 C the Bureau ran a registration sweep across the cluster, its stated purpose the updating of subject records. In practice it meant mass detention over paperwork irregularities, property seizure, forced relocation, and violence against anyone who resisted being processed. Protest became suppression became uprising, and within months a dozen systems had declared independence.

The organisation behind it—cells, couriers, weapons caches—had assembled beneath the Bureau's notice. Whether that reflects resource limits, intelligence failure, or the old habit of hearing sedition only when it is spoken in Classical has not been settled in public. Heads rolled regardless, and Yansieve operations are now run with an intensity that owes something to an institution's need to prove it still works.

## The stalemate

Imperial blockades ring the rebel systems and the Navy holds the lanes, but isolation is not conquest. A ground campaign to retake the systems would be bloody, ruinously expensive, prolonged and politically poisonous. It has not been authorised.

In the territory they hold, the rebels have replaced imperial administration with elected councils, reorganised the economy around self-sufficiency, and raised militias. How well any of it functions is precisely what the blockade keeps ambiguous. Mandate sources report chaos and incipient collapse; sympathetic accounts describe austere but working self-rule.

The rebellion is not one thing. Its factions divide over whether negotiation is conceivable, how a post-imperial society should be ordered, whether to seek outside support and from whom, and how to fight. Containment is in large part a wager on the rebels falling out before the blockade does.

## The argument at the centre

Marshal Cael Ombros and the war faction hold that four years of blockade is four years of advertising, and that a decisive campaign would settle the frontier whatever the cost.

Containment is the emperor's line: wait for hunger, exhaustion, or the rebels' own factions to do the work that armies would do expensively and visibly.

A thin scattering in Colonial Affairs and Justice, with deputy minister Vasha Toren the nearest thing it has to a face, holds that the registry manufactures rebels faster than the Bureau can detain them. They do not say abolition. The rebels have already said it for them.

## The official line

The Mandate's position is that this is criminal sedition: the citizen-subject system is the legal foundation of the state, to abolish it would be revolution, and the Throne does not negotiate revolution.

Imperial rhetoric prefers a rebellion made elsewhere. The Bureau's own intelligence suggests contacts between rebel leaders and elements of the Frontier Freeholds, and nothing conclusive beyond that. The smuggling is real but commercial, and runs through Sable Cartel subsidiaries that are also lending against the blockade.

## The watching empire

Across the Mandate, subjects follow Yansieve news with a specificity that unsettles the men whose job it is to notice such things. Hiveholm workers discuss the frontier in detail; Parvati's subjects share both legal status and grievance with the rebels; agricultural Osthavn, weeks of travel away, hosts the occasional refugee and a great deal of pointed silence. Few are ready to rise.
