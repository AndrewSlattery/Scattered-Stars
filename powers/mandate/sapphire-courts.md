# The Sapphire Courts

Named for the blue stone worked into their architecture, the Sapphire Courts are the Mandate's judicial system. At the lower levels they mostly work; toward the top they mostly bend.

## Structure

| Local Courts    | Minor civil and criminal matters; registration violations | appointed magistrates                 |
| Regional Courts | Serious crimes, appeals, commercial disputes              | judges selected by legal examination   |
| High Courts     | Major crimes and constitutional questions; one per sector | imperial appointees                    |
| Azure Tribunal  | Final appeals; cases touching the Throne                  | the Chief Justice and twelve Tribunes  |

Local courts are first contact for most people and most matters, and range from the brisk and fair to the notoriously slow and purchasable. High Court appointees are political as much as judicial, chosen for reliability before brilliance. The Azure Tribunal sits in the Chrysanthemum Court; its decisions are final, with no appeal beyond the emperor, who almost never intervenes.

## The Sapphire Steps

The High Court complex in the Chrysanthemum Court, its steps carved from actual sapphire and worn down by nine hundred years of use. Major trials here are spectacles, with galleries seating thousands and proceedings broadcast across the empire.

## Procedure

The model is inquisitorial: the judges investigate. Testimony is weighed by citizenship status, social standing, relationship to the case and corroboration, with documentary and physical evidence preferred where it exists—which advantages men whose lives are well documented over men who live off the books.

Citizens may represent themselves or engage advocates, a professional class running from the local journeyman to the elite specialist. Subjects may engage advocates too, and frequently cannot afford one.

Sentences run from fines through imprisonment to execution for the gravest offences against the state. Subjects receive harsher sentences than citizens for equivalent crimes—not as written policy but as a statistical regularity everyone in the system knows about. Appeals are expensive and slow, and most cases end where they begin.

## The Courts and the Bureau

The Bureau of Internal Harmony runs parallel to the Courts in matters of state security. It may detain a subject administratively without charge, trial or limit, and may hold a citizen for seventy-two hours—or far longer under the heading of national security—with a judicial authorisation that is rarely refused.

The most serious political cases therefore never reach a courtroom at all. The line between an ordinary crime, which the Courts will try in the open, and a threat to the state, which the Bureau will handle elsewhere, is not fixed in law.
