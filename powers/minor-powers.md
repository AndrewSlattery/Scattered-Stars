# Beyond the Seven: Minor Powers and Independents

The seven great powers dominate the news, the corridors, and compilations like this one—and govern, between them, perhaps half of humanity. The other half lives in the three to four thousand polities that never make the digest: regional powers of nine systems and vast self-regard, single worlds with a thousand years of their own history, leagues and communions and combines that are, to their citizens, simply *the world*. No gazetteer could list them. What follows is ten, chosen for range rather than importance, as a standing reminder of the size of everything this document leaves out.

---

## The Khiva Ascendancy

*Major regional power; nine systems on the Golden Circuit's coreward leg.*

Wealthy, ancient, and magnificently unembarrassed, the Ascendancy has spent six centuries describing itself as the eighth great power, and the actual great powers have long since stopped correcting it—Khiva sits on too profitable a stretch of the Circuit to be worth offending. A hereditary Ascendant rules through a council of trade families; the fleet is real, the manners are exquisite, and the diplomatic style is that of a middleweight who has never once been made to feel like one. Perhaps fifty polities in human space occupy Khiva's tier. Every one of them has its own view of the rankings.

## The Ellorine Communion

*A devout dozen worlds, rimward of the Mandate's quiet flank.*

The corridors run secular and assume the galaxy does too; the Communion is one of the many places the assumption dies. Its faith—the **Unbroken Voyage**—grew out of generation-ship liturgy in the early Scattering: the conviction that the diaspora itself is a sacrament, humanity's crossing from one shore to another not yet revealed, and that every settled world is a station of that passage. Twelve worlds hold it as the air; pilgrim-ships work the nearby routes; the theology is old, subtle, argued across a dozen schools, and entirely invisible to corridor compilations except as a line about "insular regional customs." The Communion, for its part, regards the secular corridors with mild pity, as people who have forgotten they are travelling.

## The Reyes Trust

*Neutral banking and arbitration; the rimward deeps.*

A family house—part bank, part arbitration court, part hereditary vocation—that has served a rimward volume of some three hundred settled systems for longer than the Arrhenos Third Republic has existed. The Trust holds stakes, keeps secrets, and rules on disputes for polities that will never in their history route a transaction through the Mas system, and it is the standing proof that the neutral trade is a *niche with many practitioners*, not one planet's patent. "The Arrhenos of the rimward deeps" is a comparison the Trust makes itself, with a straight face; Arrhenos, which holds a modest correspondent relationship with the family and a higher opinion of it than it advertises, has never returned the compliment.

## The Chandrasar Weave

*A lingua franca, and the two hundred systems that speak it.*

Spinward of the Mandate's volume, Trade Tongue is the *third* language. The Weave—a trade pidgin that grew a literature and became a tongue—binds a loose spinward commonwealth of some two hundred systems that trade with one another far more than with anyone else. It has its own courier lines, its own catalogues, its own classics; corridor traders working the volume hire Weave-speaking factors and complain about it; and a child raised in the Weave can live a full and cosmopolitan life without ever needing the language this document is written in. The corridors call the region "the Chandrasar systems." The region does not call itself anything. It is where everything is.

## Ìlú-Oba, the Broadcast Kingdoms

*A regional media culture that beats Vega at home.*

A prosperous cluster trailing-rimward of the Sol–Vega neighbourhood whose serials, music, and festival cycles dominate their own volume so thoroughly that Vega product runs as a niche import—prestige viewing for enthusiasts, the way the Kingdoms' own work runs in Vega. The great houses of Ìlú-Oba entertainment are dynastic, flamboyant, and periodically at open creative war with one another, which is half the content; their stars mean nothing on Lyra and everything across forty systems. Vega's commercial attachés file patient reports about market penetration. The penetration has been imminent for a century.

## Resheph, and the cousins

*Another of the galaxy's gender experiments—the largest of the small ones.*

Arrhenos is the largest and richest society to have rearranged gender, not the only one, and Resheph is the eldest of the cousins: an all-female world of some ninety million, founded a generation before Arrhenos by a movement with different reasons and a quieter temperament, reproducing by ovum fusion and interested in nobody's opinion. It trades little, travels less, and maintains with Arrhenos the famous **cousinly correspondence**—two centuries of letters between scholars comparing notes on lives the rest of the galaxy finds remarkable and they do not. Beyond Resheph the family scatters small: post-gender collectives in the Freehold arc, a five-gender society in the Chandrasar Weave, single-sex colonies that stayed a station's size or failed. None is famous. Together they are the standing answer to the galaxy's fascination with Arrhenos: it is not that the experiment is unthinkable; it is that only one of the experimenters had a bank.

## The Veskarn Combine

*Corporate governance, practised honestly.*

Six systems owned and operated by a corporation that, unlike the Cartel, has a headquarters, publishes accounts, and will tell you exactly what it is. Every citizen holds shares; the franchise is the shareholder register; politics is dividend policy, conducted in general meetings of forty billion voting proxies. It is neither the tyranny outsiders assume nor the efficiency the Combine advertises—share concentration has produced its own quiet aristocracy, and the annual meeting is as theatrical as any coronation—but it works, after its fashion, and its citizens defend it with the particular firmness of people who are tired of being told they live in a business. The Cartel is rumoured to hold a position in the Combine. The Combine publishes its register and dares anyone to find it.

## Cold Harbour

*The system that refuses the quid.*

Burned three centuries ago by a corridor currency collapse that wiped out a generation's savings, the Cold Harbour system trades in kind, in labour-credit, and in its own dour scrip, and honours the quid nowhere—not at the docks, not at the exchange, not for love. Corridor merchants must barter or bank elsewhere, and mostly stop coming, which suits Cold Harbour; the system runs a deliberately modest economy, self-sufficient in essentials, and regards monetary sovereignty as its founding grudge and chief civic virtue. Economists cite it as a cautionary tale. It has outlasted several of the institutions that did the citing.

## The Amaranth League

*The Republic's own diaspora.*

Enhancement without the Lattice: the League's founders were Neo-Solar citizens who wanted the long life, the morphologies, and the medicine, and could not bear the shared mind—or, as League historians prefer, *chose the self*. Three systems trailing of Sol, technically superb, culturally intense, and permanently arguing with the Republic in the way only family can: each regards the other as the experiment that lost its nerve. The League is the largest of many NSR splinters, and the standing proof that even the strangest polity in human space sheds dissenters like any other—and that its ideas travel farther than its Lattice ever will, changing shape as they go.

## Pell's Landing

*Where the long life hasn't arrived.*

A poor world at the far end of a Long Haul spoke: one city, one clinic, a resupply schedule measured in waits. The corridor's two-hundred-year lifespan is a rumour here—the medicine exists, the supply and the specialists do not—and a Pell's native who reaches ninety has done well. The world is not a tragedy to itself; it farms, marries, argues, and buries its dead by lamplight the way settled people always have, and its emigrants' letters home from the corridors read like dispatches from a heaven with an entry fee. It stands here for hundreds of worlds like it: the quiet, unphotographed places where the diaspora's greatest inequality is not money or rank, but *time*.
