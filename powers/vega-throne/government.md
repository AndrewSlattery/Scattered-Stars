# The Vega Commercial Throne: Government

Vega is not a democracy and does not pretend to be one. Its argument for itself is that a market votes every day, with money, and disciplines more ruthlessly than any electorate could: a house that fails its customers loses its sales, then its revenue, then its rank, and at last its name.

The market adjudicates the few thousand families that are in it. The other one and a half trillion Vegans get neither a vote nor a verdict.

## The monarch

The monarch holds sovereign authority over all Vega territory, a controlling interest in the Throne Corporation, final arbitration in disputes between houses, and command of the Royal Fleet. These are not ceremonial powers, but the monarch rules through institutions, precedent and the management of competing noble interests far more than by decree.

Lysandra IV has reigned since 3795 C. The Banking War was the test of her reign, and the reconstruction has been, in her hands, partly an opportunity to reshape the field while rivals were weak.

Her children hold posts across the royal enterprises: Prince Caspian, the heir, over the Entertainment Division; Princess Aurelie over External Relations; Prince Hadrian liaising with the Royal Fleet; Princess Seraphine over Luxury Brands; and Prince Octavian over the News Division, a delicate posting given what Vega's press is meant to be trusted as separate from. Family members are expected to perform, and those who cannot are quietly sidelined.

## The houses

| Royal House      | 1      | the monarch's family                                  |
| Archducal houses | 7      | vast commercial empires; one seat attainted and empty |
| Ducal houses     | ~40    | major players with significant market presence        |
| Comital houses   | ~400   | mid-tier, in specialised niches                       |
| Baronial houses  | ~3,000 | minor nobility; single enterprises or localities      |

Noble status confers territorial jurisdiction, commercial licences, court access, trial by peers, and the right to raise a retinue. It requires taxes, court attendance, military contribution, and commercial performance above a minimum threshold.

## Attainder

A house that fails its obligations can be stripped of everything: rank gone, licences revoked and returned to the Throne for redistribution, jurisdiction lost, court access ended, and often personal assets seized. Former nobles become commoners, and their descendants carry the stigma down.

Of the fourteen houses attainted since the Banking War, seven fell for bankruptcy, four for criminal conduct, two for rebellion, and one for the catch-all of conduct unbecoming. The first archducal attainder in two centuries fell in this period, on House Kraeven.

The door opens inward too. New houses are created by royal grant for extraordinary service, by purchase at baronial level only, by marriage, or by commercial triumph too spectacular for the Throne to ignore. Most new creations are baronial, and climbing higher takes generations.

## Valdorian

House Valdorian has won entertainment so thoroughly—roughly a third of all Vega production, the largest private military in Vega space, chokepoints over the infrastructure its rivals depend on—that it approaches the power to defy the Throne. It has done so legitimately. It has not failed, so it cannot be attainted; it has broken no law, so it cannot be charged; and it has performed correct loyalty for thirty-one years under Duke Caelum.

Queen Lysandra has declined every opportunity to clip Valdorian, calculating that moving against a house that has done nothing wrong would damage the legitimacy the whole system runs on.

## The succession

Prince Caspian is the accepted heir and his eventual accession is expected to be smooth. He is also eighty-nine, childless, and has shown no interest in becoming otherwise.

The Throne can designate an heir, but the choice must in practice be acceptable to the great houses, whose withdrawal of cooperation would make rule impossible. Every archducal house has begun, quietly, to game the generation after next.

## Administration

Day-to-day governance runs through professional ministries: the Exchequer for taxation, royal finances and commercial regulation; Justice; Commerce for licensing and market oversight; External Affairs; Defence; Culture for broadcasting regulation and patronage; and a Reconstruction ministry that is theoretically temporary and showing every sign of permanence. These posts are not noble preserves and competent commoners rise to senior roles, though the very top tends to go to minor nobility or royal appointees.

Within their fiefdoms, houses govern as they like, subject to royal law, commercial regulation and custom, and the Throne intervenes only at the extremes. Noble disputes go to the Court of Peers, where nobles are judged by nobles; commoner cases go to ordinary magistrates' courts that function adequately and arbitrarily by turns.

## Politics

Factions form around shared industries, adjacent territories, ideology—traditionalists against modernisers, expansionists against consolidators—and the old currents of marriage, friendship and inherited enmity. The Queen plays these against one another, ensuring no coalition grows strong enough to threaten the Throne.

The reconstruction has left the court less comfortable than it was a generation ago. Houses that weathered the war have grown fat on the assets of houses that did not, the survivors resent the profiteers, the reconstruction debt is a standing humiliation, and a rising cohort of younger nobles who never knew the pre-war prosperity has begun to wonder aloud whether the recovery will ever reach them.
