# Union of Frontier Freeholds: Culture

Frontier culture is what happens when people who reject authority have to live together anyway.

## The four virtues

Self-reliance is the cardinal one: maintain your own gear, solve your own problems, defend your own, expect no rescue. It is not absolute—self-reliant people still form crews and mutual-aid arrangements—the point being that you can stand alone, not that you must.

Earned respect follows from it. Standing comes from demonstrated competence rather than credentials or birth: a veteran prospector's read on a claim outweighs a newcomer's degree, and reputation is built by action and spent by failure.

Mind your own business is self-reliance turned outward. The limit is supposed to be the point where another's affairs start affecting you, and the company store, the debt peonage and the slow purchase of the commons affect everyone eventually and nobody immediately.

Keep your word is the only thing enforcing a contract where no court will. The prospector who cheats a partner finds no future partners; the crew that abandons a contract finds no future contracts. Social enforcement works well on peers, who need each other again tomorrow, and far less well on a creditor who needs you only once.

## The shape of frontier life

The basic unit of the mobile frontier is the crew: five to fifty people working, travelling and often living together, bound by obligations—share risk and reward, back each other, leave cleanly—whose betrayal draws near-universal contempt. Around the crews sit claim partnerships and their bitter, common disputes; permanent settlement communities that grow their own neighbourhood associations and guilds from scratch; and the great itinerant population of prospectors, boom-followers and crews between contracts, who belong to the Freeholds collectively and so to nobody. Settlers think the itinerants have given up, and itinerants think the settlers have.

Gender carries no uniform norm, and different Freeholds, crews and trades settle it differently. Families adapt to mobility and risk: nuclear in settled places, extended kin-networks spread along trade routes, crew-families spanning generations, communal and compound arrangements where they suit. Children grow up early, are schooled variably, and assume they will one day leave, or deliberately stay.

## Belief and language

The frontier holds every religious position humans have carried out to it and enforces none—refugees from persecution, experimental communes, whole devout Freeholds, idiosyncratic personal practice. Its one near-universal faith is luck, spoken of as a real and capricious thing. Luck favours the prepared; don't push your luck; his luck ran out.

Trade Tongue dominates, because no Freehold is large enough to impose its own language and everyone has to deal with everyone. Heritage tongues persist and fade. The slang is a dialect of the work: a strike and a bust, to wash out after failure, dust for worthless, to float without a destination and settle to stop, to crew up and to claim-jump. Speech runs direct to the point of bluntness, with little small talk, verbal agreements taken seriously, and ambiguity treated as a hazard.

## The prospector myth

The core frontier story is that anyone can strike it rich: ship, gear, grit, and a lottery of discovery that ignores where you came from. Prospectors do strike it rich, and not on average—most find nothing, most booms bust, most real discovery is corporate rather than individual, equipment and survival take capital, and information and timing beat luck. Three Falls is the myth at full volume, and already turning.

The myth draws the labour the frontier runs on, and makes the failures feel deserved and the fortunes feel earned.

## Pastimes

Drinking, with the bar as social centre; gambling, from card games to outright claim speculation; sex work, present everywhere, legal status varying and practical acceptance near-total; combat sports, sometimes lethal. Vega serials and music are devoured despite arriving years late, and the frontier finds its own portrayal in them by turns amusing and offensive. Local culture is participatory—work songs and drinking songs, ballads of strikes and disasters, the storytelling tradition of the long lie, games played by house rules.

News and gossip is a pastime and also infrastructure: who struck what, which claim is producing, what the Mandate is doing at the border. It is why the hubs are information exchanges before they are anything else.

## How the frontier sees its neighbours

Bar-room consensus holds the Mandate in contempt and wariness, and buys Mandate goods anyway. It regards Arrhenos with pragmatic respect—needed services, no political strings, the all-male society thought odd but not troubling. It finds Vega soft and performative and consumes it enthusiastically. It feels a real kinship with the Drift Communities, and trades and mingles with them at the shared hubs with an ease it extends to almost nobody else.

Past the bar-room the consensus frays. The Freeholds hold billions of Mandate emigrants whose feelings about home are anything but simple contempt; settlements that owe their water plant to an Arrhene loan officer they named their children after; Vega expatriates running frontier operations who find the mockery cheaper than the fascination underneath it.
