# Elysian Collective: Governance

The Collective governs itself the way it terraforms: slowly, by consensus, with a deep institutional preference for deferring any decision that cannot be made comfortably.

## The Guild Council

Seven Grandmasters govern the sworn. Each holds a major discipline, and together they set priorities, allocate the treasury, accept or refuse projects, and settle what the Conclave cannot.

| First   | Atmospheric Engineering            | Lis Valtonen       |
| Second  | Biological Systems                 | Okafor Dembe       |
| Third   | Geological Modification            | Renn Castellari    |
| Fourth  | Hydrological Engineering           | Saya Miren-Goh     |
| Fifth   | Climatological Management          | Torben Axelkraft   |
| Sixth   | Gravitational Systems              | Prem Danava        |
| Seventh | Reclamation, the Lazarus Project   | Maret Solveig      |

Grandmasters are elected by the Masters of their discipline by ranked-choice vote. A candidate must have carried at least three major project cycles and hold the endorsement of twelve sitting Masters. Terms run twenty years, renewable once. Most Grandmasters spend decades in the field before they ascend, and arrive at governance reluctantly, treating it as a duty stolen from real work.

The Council works by consensus on anything strategic. Dissent is spoken and recorded, and what cannot reach consensus is deferred.

The seven no longer think quite alike. Valtonen of the First, senior and cautious, holds that the guild's neutrality and the guild's hierarchy are what have kept it alive for six centuries and should be disturbed as little as possible. Solveig of the Seventh, whose Lazarus crews are more than half Tenders and who has buried more of them than anyone else at the table, presses for reform—of the world-songs first and the franchise in time. The rest fall between, by temperament and by what their disciplines need.

## The Conclave

Once a year the working guild gathers aboard the Garden: part parliament, part professional congress, part festival. For members scattered across isolated sites it is the one time much of the Collective stands in one place.

Active projects report to the assembled guild, where peer scrutiny is unsparing, failure dissected in the open and success sung. The Council lays out proposed budgets and priorities, the assembled Masters vote, and the Council honours the vote. Conflicts between projects and between members are heard and ruled on. Research, technique and argument are traded across disciplines.

The Conclave is a gathering of the sworn. Masters vote and Journeymen attend; the Tenders who make up the larger half of the working Collective send no delegate and cast no vote. A growing number of the sworn find this indefensible.

The Conclave opens with the Recitation of Worlds: a spoken naming of every world the Collective has made habitable, from the first to the most recent reclassification. It presently takes four hours to recite in full, and the guild hears every name standing.

## Neutrality

The founding charter makes political neutrality an inviolable principle. The Collective terraforms for whoever can pay, without regard to the client's politics, ideology or conduct, and has built worlds for the Mandate and for Freeholders, for Vega houses and for independent polities.

Neutrality means no power need fear the Collective as a rival, and every power can hire it without seeming to bless its enemies. Three pressures keep testing it.

The Mandate's rebel systems have asked the Collective to terraform for them. To accept is revenue, and a clean demonstration that the guild truly serves all comers; to refuse is to placate the largest client the Collective has. The Council has deferred the decision so often that the deferral has become the policy, and both Yansieve and Tiamming have learned to read it.

Younger members increasingly argue that neutrality is not innocence, and that to hand a tyrant a living world is to take a share in whatever he does on it. The Council's reply is that the guild makes worlds habitable and what is done with a habitable world is not the guild's affair.

And a guild that decides who receives habitable worlds, and in what order, holds enormous unspent power. A faction has always murmured that some of it should be spent, on environmental standards, on protections for terraformed populations, on conditions of labour. That last has lately taken on a second meaning, because the labour most visibly in want of protection is the guild's own Tenders.

## Membership

The guild recruits its terraformers from across human space. Candidates apply to the Academy, where admission is fierce and weighed on scientific aptitude, problem-solving, and the psychological fitness for isolated long-duration work. The intake is deliberately drawn from everywhere: mixed perspectives make for better problem-solving, and a guild whose members come from every polity is easier to believe neutral.

Those admitted owe the Collective their professional loyalty. They may not practise terraforming independently, may not carry guild technique beyond the guild, and tithe a share of contract earnings to the treasury. In return they receive the finest training and equipment in human space, a community, and a craft.

Tenders are enrolled rather than sworn. They are taken on as labour from client populations, from frontier worlds, and from the guild's own unraised children, and are bound by the Collective's rules and rewarded from its treasury without entering its guild. They hold their own internal grades and rise within them. What they cannot do, short of being lifted into the Academy, is cross into the sworn.

Members who break guild rules—above all the prohibition on sharing proprietary technique—face charges before the Council, a defence heard, and a vote. For a sworn terraformer expulsion is ruin, and the threat alone keeps most in line. What becomes of an expelled member carrying Genesis secrets in her head is a matter the Collective does not discuss in public.
