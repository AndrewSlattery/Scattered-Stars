# The Drift Communities

Around 900 billion stateless people aboard mobile stations called havens, across a fleet nobody has ever counted, including the Drift. It is not a nation and not a culture. What its people share is an origin, a predicament and a stance.

The origin is the Cascade of 2756 C, the collapse that shattered the old order and set ships and station-crews adrift between dead authorities; many havens trace their independence to a vessel that simply never came home.

The predicament is that you cannot, aboard a moving station, make all your own fuel and parts and medicine.

The stance—the only thing that genuinely runs from the richest Grand Haven to the grimmest salvage hauler—is a practised, defiant joy: the conviction, hard-won against real hardship, that a life this fragile is exactly the kind worth celebrating.

## Not one people

Grand Havens are the postcard: jump-capable, self-sufficient for decades, centuries old, large and rich enough to choose their own course. They are rare. Haven Ascendant is one. Most Drift will never live aboard one and will visit only as paying guests.

Trade Havens are the working majority—nomadic stations selling medical, technical, entertainment, information-brokerage and neutral-ground services to far-flung worlds on a travelling circuit, often on margins thin enough that a bad season means a hard one. This is what most Drift life actually looks like.

Refugee Havens are the floor: sanctuaries for the lost, the disappeared and the debt-fleeing, offering anonymity and protection to people who ran clean out of any other door.

Beneath that, havens specialise—in salvage processing, in vice, in medicine, in information, in diplomatic neutrality, in cultural preservation. A Drifter born in a salvage hauler's recycling deck and a Drifter born in Ascendant's Core share a legal status, which is none, and a way of speaking about freedom, and almost no material reality at all. The gulf runs inside the great havens too: Ascendant's transient Outring holds more people than its archives, its halls and its promenade combined.

There is no government above the single haven. Each governs itself, by everything from elected convocations to a captain's word.

## Freedom is not free

Statelessness is the absence of a home market, and the difference is a bill that comes due every cycle. A haven cannot mine its own metals at scale, refine its own fuel indefinitely, or manufacture every part it will break. It must resupply, and the routes that reach the wandering Drift are not routes most carriers will fly.

The carrier that will is the Sable Cartel, whose logistics arm services Drift routes few others touch, and whose Nexus headquarters at Korsen's Anchorage is the nearest thing the Drift has to a central hub.

The bankers that will are the handful of houses that keep accounts for people with no country to vouch for them: the Reyes Trust out in the rimward deeps, the Khivan trade families along the coreward Circuit, a scattering of neutral houses of which the Arrhene are the best known, and a good many small firms that ask no questions and charge for the privilege.

Both relationships are fair, useful and quietly defining. A rich haven like Ascendant, sitting on centuries of reserves spread across a dozen institutions in six volumes of space, holds a long leash and barely feels it. A Trade Haven living job to job feels every inch of its short one.

## The hard-won joy

The Drift lives where everything is fleeting—worlds die, stations fail, records crumble—and its answer is a kind of disciplined celebration: if everything ends, then everything matters. The feast, the memory-keeping and the near-sacred duty of welcome have teeth, and a haven that will refuse entry to nobody for who they are will spend real blood defending the principle. Haven Ascendant has written the creed down most beautifully; the instinct is found on every deck, practised according to every deck's means.

## What the Drift is becoming

The one argument running fleet-wide is about the future of the body. Neo-Solar Republic enhancement—the Lattice, the morphologies, the long networked post-human life—has begun spreading into the Drift, and the havens are split by it. To some it is simply the next tool of survival, embraced without fuss. To others, and most sharply to the memory-keeping havens, it is identity erosion: a people whose whole purpose is preserving what humans were cannot easily watch its children decide to stop being that. No authority exists to settle the question. It will be settled haven by haven.
