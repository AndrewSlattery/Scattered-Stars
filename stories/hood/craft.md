# Craft — writing a serial that feels authored

*Hood* is written iteratively, across many instalments, through an ongoing conversation. The risk of that form is sprawl: a story that reads like a list of things that happened. These are the techniques that make a serialised draft feel **planned** instead — a web, not a clothesline. They are setting-agnostic; for the Arrhenos-specific rules see [setting-brief.md](setting-brief.md), and for how they fold into each turn see [direction.md](direction.md). The live application of all of this is the [bible](bible.md).

## Reincorporation over invention

When the story needs a character, place, object, or complication, first ask whether **something already established could serve** — perhaps in a new or unexpected way. A vendor from the [opening](outline.md) can become pivotal; the bar where Milo first listened can be where a friendship breaks. Reincorporation creates the sensation of design and gives the reader the pleasure of recognition. **Before introducing anyone or anything new, check the [bible](bible.md).** The cast is meant to stay [small](cast.md).

## Plant and payoff

Treat details as seeds. A specific object, a trait revealed in passing, an environmental fact — any of these can pay off later. Plant **forward** (drop details that *might* matter, without committing) and harvest **backward** (when you need something now, look at what's already there). Avoid the cheat — "he suddenly remembered the knife in his boot" — when the knife was never planted; either plant earlier or solve it with what exists. *Hood* is already loaded: the [Earth phone](cast.md), the [spaceless inventory](system.md), the [Drifter cover](protagonist.md), the [Trade-Tongue tell](setting-brief.md). Track loaded guns in the [bible](bible.md).

## "Therefore" and "but", not "and then"

Each beat should connect to the next by **causation or complication**, not mere sequence. *Test:* can you say why this scene follows the last? "It's just what happens next" means the scene hasn't earned its place. The [opening beats](outline.md) are written this way — the bare neck *forces* the cover; no money *forces* the pawn — keep it up.

## Scene and sequel rhythm

Alternate two units. A **scene**: goal → conflict → disaster (rarely exactly what was sought). A **sequel**: reaction → dilemma → decision. All-scene writing exhausts; all-sequel writing stalls. The interplay paces the book. The System's [State Card](system-state-card.md) is a natural sequel beat — a place to register reaction and decision in Milo's own idiom.

## Thread awareness

At any point, several threads are open — plot mysteries, evolving relationships, promises made to the reader. Discipline: open threads **deliberately**, develop them **periodically** (don't let one go dormant too long), and close them **satisfyingly**. *Hood*'s spine threads — [legitimacy, the phone/debt, the cover, the System](outline.md) — should each get touched, tightened, or paid as the book goes. **If you can't list the open threads, the story has sprawled** — consolidate before opening more. The [bible](bible.md) is where the list lives.

## Character consistency and voice

Keep speech patterns, behavioural tendencies, core motivation (what they *want* vs what they *need*), and relationships stable and evolving-on-purpose. Before writing a character's dialogue, recall their established voice and current emotional state. Milo's [register gap](protagonist.md) — confident inside, halting outside — is a fixed feature; let it thin only as the arc earns it.

## Pacing and arc awareness

Know roughly where you are on the shape — setup, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution. Early: establish and plant; don't rush to crisis. Middle: complicate and raise stakes; avoid wheel-spinning. Approaching the climax: converge threads, call back plants, lift the tempo. End: pay promises, close threads, allow a denouement. Uncertainty about *where you are* usually surfaces as pacing trouble. Map this onto the [three acts](outline.md).

## Constraint as generativity

Resist solving problems by adding elements. A [small cast](cast.md) deepens characterisation; [limited locations](setting-brief.md) build a sense of place; limited time creates urgency. When the owner sets a constraint, treat it as a creative gift. (The whole premise is a constraint engine: a man who can't read the world, can't prove who he is, and can't tell the truth.)

## "Yes, and"

Accept what's established and build on it. Don't contradict prior facts (unless deliberately, as a reveal); don't ignore inconvenient details — incorporate them. Treat earlier material as **given**, not draft. When something established creates difficulty, that difficulty is usually more interesting than erasing it.

## Periodic consolidation

At natural breaks (instalment ends, act transitions), take stock: what threads are open? what's planted but unharvested? who or where is being neglected? what's the current tonal state? what does the reader most want next — and should that want be met, delayed, or subverted? This can happen silently, but in *Hood* it's **written down** — that's what the [bible](bible.md) is for, and the [State Card](system-state-card.md) is its public face.

## Common pitfalls

New character where an existing one would serve; forgetting what's established (contradictions break immersion); all setup and no payoff; all payoff and no setup (convenient solutions feel unearned); relentless escalation without consolidation; characters who exist only to serve plot; tonal whiplash; and the "and then" trap. The defence against every one of them is the same: **keep the [bible](bible.md) current, and read it before you write.**

## On collaboration

When the owner gives direction — "continue", "have X happen", "go in Y direction" — treat it as collaborative worldbuilding: a constraint and a gift. Build on it rather than merely executing it; honour the contribution while keeping the established elements intact. The goal is a story that feels *authored* even though it emerged through dialogue.
