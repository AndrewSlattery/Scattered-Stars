# Chapter Three — The Desk

*Draft. First-person past tense. ~6,300 words. Maps to [outline](../outline.md) Beat 2 / [storyboard](../act-1-storyboard.md) Ch 3. Continues directly from [Ch 2](chapter-02-initialisation.md): the small hours, the Shambles, soaked through, already scanned-and-flagged once tonight.*

---

The room past the doorway was the most ordinary thing I'd seen since I died, and the ordinariness nearly undid me worse than the rest of it.

I'd braced for more wonders. After the upward fountain and the rainless rain and the bruise-coloured gardens, some part of me had got ready to walk into a marvel. Instead it was a waiting room. That's all it was. Benches along the walls, a counter at the far end, a scatter of men sitting in the particular slumped geometry of people waiting in the small hours for help they'd rather not need — elbows on knees, heads back against the wall, one of them asleep with his mouth open. There was a smell of something warm and antiseptic under the frying-oil air. There was a faint hum. There was a man behind the counter, and there were two or three others moving in the back in soft clothing, and it was a clinic, it was just a clinic, the night desk of a place that patched people up and asked them questions, and I had walked into a thousand rooms like it across my own grey life and the recognition went through me like a dropped thing.

I did what I always did in a new system. Before I committed to anything, I stood just inside the door, hood up, and I *read the room*.

It's the only genuinely useful instinct I've ever had and it didn't desert me even then, half-dead on my feet. I watched. I watched how the men at the counter presented themselves — each one leaning in, two fingers to the collar at his throat, that same automatic tap I'd seen at the fountain and the gate, and the man behind the counter glancing down at something I couldn't see, a reading, an account, a name, and nodding, and the whole transaction taking three seconds because the city already knew who everyone was before they opened their mouths. I watched a man come out from the back with a fresh dressing on his forearm and a younger one fuss at him about it. I watched the rhythm of the place: arrive, present the collar, be known, be helped. Arrive, present, be known.

And I watched what happened when a man couldn't.

There was one. Down the far end of a bench, apart from the others, a lean restless figure who kept getting up and sitting down, and when his turn came and he went to the counter he didn't tap his throat, he *talked*, fast and low and with his hands, and the man behind the counter listened with a patience that had a floor to it, a professional patience that was also a kind of holding-at-arm's-length, and I understood that I was looking at the other category. The ones who arrived and *couldn't* present. The ones the city didn't already know. And I understood, watching the careful distance in the counterman's face, that this was the category I was about to join, and that *care and questions* meant exactly what it said, in that order, and that the questions were the price of the care.

So I had about ninety seconds, standing in that doorway, to decide who I was going to be.

I want to be honest about how little I had to build with. I didn't know what year it was. I didn't know what planet it was. I didn't know what the collar was called or what the money was called or what lay outside this city or this world or how I'd be expected to know it. The one thing I had — the only thing — was the sign over the door. *No collar, no problem.* Somebody, somewhere in this city, had built a clinic specifically for people who turned up without a collar, which meant such people *existed*, in enough numbers to need a desk, which meant there was a slot. There was a shape in this world that a man with no collar and no account and no language could plausibly be, a shape the city had a word for, and if I could find the edge of that shape I could climb inside it.

I didn't know the word yet. But I'd heard fragments all night, leaking out of the System and the signs and the half-understood flow of speech, the patient translation routine sampling me and resolving me the way I'd resolve a pattern, and one of the fragments had surfaced more than once, a word the city kept reaching for when it tried to describe the kind of nothing I was. I didn't know what it meant. I clung to it anyway. It was the only handhold I had.

The word was *Drift*.

The counterman looked up and saw me hovering in the doorway, hooded, soaked, swaying, and his face did a small professional recalibration — *ah, one of those* — and he lifted a hand and beckoned me over, not unkindly, with the floor of patience already laid down under it, and I crossed the room toward the counter on legs that didn't entirely believe in me, and I became, in the next ten minutes, a Drifter.

---

Up close he was younger than the patience had made him sound, maybe thirty, with the comfortable build I was learning to expect and a plain dull collar that I'd later understand to mean he didn't care to advertise anything about himself, which is its own kind of advertisement. He had the unhurried competence of someone who'd done a lot of night shifts. He said something to me — a greeting, a question, the warm flowing nonsense — and waited.

And then the room did the thing the sign had done. There was a soft chime, and a half-second of nothing, and then a second voice, lower, threaded under his, speaking the worn-down English I could almost follow — *the clinic translating him for me*, the way the sign had translated itself, some patient routine in the walls listening to my muttering all night and now bridging the gap, badly, a beat behind, but bridging it.

"...need," it caught up, lagging over his easy sentence. "...are you hurt. Can you understand. This unit. Speaking."

"Yes," I said. In English. And then, because *yes* alone wasn't going to carry me, I made myself slow down, because I'd already worked out — reading the room, reading the lag — that the translation cut both ways, that it needed a beat to render *me* into *him*, and that the beat was my friend. A man who answers instantly is a man with answers ready. A man who pauses, who struggles, who arrives a sentence late and rough — that man reads as concussed, or shocked, or simply foreign past the edge of the machine's competence, and all three of those were better than the truth.

So I let myself be slow. It wasn't even hard; I *was* slow, I was wrecked, the slowness was ninety per cent real and I just stopped fighting the other ten. "Yes. I can — understand. A bit. It's — the words are coming and going."

The lag. His face, watching me, registering the gap, and softening fractionally toward the diagnosis I was feeding him without saying it: *this one's not right, go gentle.* He said something to the back, over his shoulder, and turned back to me, and the under-voice rendered it: "...sit. You can sit. You look..." A word the machine didn't have. It tried again. "...you look like you have come a long way."

Reader, I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out cracked and wrong and I turned it into a cough, but it got out, because *a long way* was the single most accurate and most absurd thing anyone could possibly have said to me, and the counterman took the cough for distress and came round the counter and got a hand under my elbow, a stranger's hand, the first kind touch in this whole impossible world, and steered me to a chair, and I let him, and I want it on the record that I was so tired and so frightened and so far from home that the simple animal fact of being helped to a chair by someone who didn't yet know to be careful of me put a lump in my throat the size of a fist.

In the corner of my eye, the box.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Skill gained: Xeno-Linguistics (Lv 1)
   — local speech: 4% intelligible
  ...keep listening
```

Four per cent. I'd take it. I'd built empires on thinner margins.

---

The questions started gently and got less gentle, the way questions do, and the whole survival of me came down to the next few minutes, and I have never been prouder of anything I've done, including the things I'm not ashamed of, than I am of the lie I built sitting in that chair with four per cent of the language and a fist in my throat.

He had a — I'll call it a tablet, a slim bright panel, and he sat across from me with it and began, and the under-voice rendered him a beat behind, and I answered into the lag.

*Name?*

"Milo." True. It came out before I'd decided, and I was glad it had, because a true name sits in your mouth differently from a false one and some part of me knew I'd need at least one true thing to stand on. He waited, as if for more, and I gave him a helpless look and let the more not come, and he wrote down *Milo* and didn't push, and I learned something: that here, a man with only one name was not an impossibility but a *category*, a sad small flag, the mark of someone from somewhere that didn't do names the way this place did.

*Where are you from?*

And there it was. The whole hinge of my life, swinging on one answer, with four per cent of the language and a patient man waiting and the truth — *I'm from a flat above an industrial estate eighteen hundred years ago on a planet you've maybe forgotten exists* — sitting in my chest like a stone I could never, ever put down on this counter.

I reached for the handhold.

"The Drift," I said.

I said it carefully, like a man saying a thing that hurt, which wasn't hard to fake because I had no idea if it was even a real answer or whether I'd just said the local word for *nonsense* and blown myself up in the first thirty seconds. I watched his face the way I'd watch an order book. And his face — *moved*. Not toward suspicion. Toward something softer and more tired and more useful to me than anything I could have engineered: recognition. A small inward nod. *Ah. The Drift. One of those.* The patience didn't go away but it changed register, from the patience you keep for a problem to the patience you keep for a casualty, and the difference saved my life.

The under-voice, lagging: "...the Drift. You're off a haven? Which—" the machine stumbled, caught up— "which haven. Do you have a haven."

I had no idea what a haven was. I knew it the way you know a word from a song in a language you don't speak — *haven*, something to do with the Drift, something to do with the nothing I was — and I did the only safe thing, which was to break.

"I don't—" I started, and stopped, and let my face do the work, and the work wasn't even acting, because the question *which haven, do you have a haven* had landed on a man who genuinely did not have a home in this universe or the previous one, a man whose home was a damp patch shaped like Italy eighteen centuries gone, and whatever showed on my face when I tried and failed to answer *where is your home* was real, was the realest thing in the room, and the counterman saw it and stopped writing.

"It's alright," the under-voice said, rendering whatever gentle thing he'd said. "You don't have to. Take your..." — the word *time*, arriving late.

I took my time. And in the taking of it I assembled, fast, in the cold clear back room of myself that had read ten thousand order books, the story. Not from knowledge — I had none — but from the *shape* of the questions, which is the thing nobody understands about lying well: you don't invent the lie, you let the questions tell you the lie they're expecting, and then you give it back to them slightly damaged so it reads as true. He'd asked *which haven* as if a haven were a place a person came off, like a ship, like a port. He'd softened at *the Drift* the way you soften at a known sadness. The word the city kept reaching for when it described my nothing was a word it already had a slot and a sympathy for. So the Drift was poor, and scattered, and the kind of place a man could be *from* and also have *lost*, and the kind of place that produced men with one name and no collar and a haunted look, and I was going to be exactly that man, and the beauty of it, the cold beautiful efficiency of it, was that almost everything true about me — the disorientation, the ignorance, the grief, the lack of an account, the not-knowing how anything worked — was *also* true of the man I was pretending to be. I barely had to lie. I just had to let them mistake the cause.

"There was a haven," I said, slow, rough, into the lag. "I don't — it's gone, or I'm — I got put off. There was trouble. I got robbed. They took—" and here I touched my own throat, the bare betraying skin above the hoodie, the gap where the collar should be, and I let the gesture finish the sentence, *they took the thing that made me a person*, and I watched the counterman's eyes follow my hand to my throat and fill in the rest exactly as I needed him to.

The box, helpfully, kept its own scorecard.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Improvised cover: "robbed Drifter, lost haven"
  Consistency check: PASS (you said almost nothing)
  ! the best lies are mostly true
```

*The best lies are mostly true.* It only ever knew what I knew. But it was right, and I clung to the rightness of it, because it was the rule I was going to live inside for a long time: say almost nothing, make it almost true, let them do the inventing. They wanted me to be a robbed Drifter. It cost them nothing and it cost me nothing and it explained everything, and the only people who pay for a lie that convenient are the ones who tell it, later, in the dark, when the man they've lied to has started to matter. But I didn't know that yet. That was Devan's bill, and it hadn't come due.

---

The questions wound down after that, into the gentler administrative kind — was I hurt, was I in pain, when had I last eaten, did I know where I was — and I answered the true ones truly and let the lag and the shock cover the rest, and at some point a second man came out from the back, older, with a violet thread of colour worked into the edge of his collar that I didn't understand and would later learn meant *the sciences*, meant *medical*, and he crouched in front of my chair with the unhurried attention of a man for whom my disaster was simply Tuesday, and he ran a small device down the air an inch from my body, not touching me, and watched a reading I couldn't see, and frowned.

The under-voice gave me the frown a beat later, rendering what he murmured, half to me and half to his colleague. "...old damage. A lot of old damage. This is..." The machine hunted. "...this is a body that has not been looked after."

And that, of everything, was the line that nearly broke me.

Because he didn't mean it as a judgement. He meant it as a *finding*, the neutral observation of a man reading a scan, and the scan was reading the truth of me — the truth no cover could touch — which was a wrist I'd broken at nineteen and never had set right, and teeth that had been quietly going for a decade because the dentist cost money I'd rather have spent on the chair, and a back rounded by ten thousand hours of sitting, and the low-grade everywhere wear of a body that had been run on bad food and worse sleep and no margin for thirty years, a body that had been, in his exact word, *not looked after*. And he said it the way you'd note that a coat was thin, with no blame in it at all, only a kind of mild professional sadness that anyone should arrive in such a state, in a place where, I was about to learn, nobody had to.

"We can fix most of this," the under-voice said, rendering him. "Now. While you're here. It's—" the machine fumbled, and landed on the flattest possible word— "...routine."

Routine.

I didn't understand, at first, what he meant. I thought he meant they'd *look* at it, schedule it, refer it, the way my world handled a body that had gone wrong — a waiting list, a form, a date six months out. I started to say I couldn't pay, the truest sentence I had, and he'd already moved on, already waved the younger one over, already begun the thing, and what the thing *was* — I have no good words for it, even now, even after I learned the dull mechanics, the cellular this and the regenerative that, the casual everyday miracle of a medicine eighteen hundred years past mine. There was a warmth. There was a pressure that wasn't pressure. The older man worked the small device over my wrist and my jaw and the base of my spine the way you'd run an iron over a creased shirt, unhurried, chatting to his colleague about something else entirely, and the wrist I'd broken at nineteen — the wrist that ached when it rained, that I'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a sound that's always there — *stopped*. The ache went out of it. I flexed it and it moved like a wrist in a memory, a young wrist, a wrist from before, and I sat in that chair in a clinic in the small hours of the future and I felt my own body being quietly, casually, *unbroken*, decades of accumulated damage smoothed out as a matter of course, for free, because in this place it would have been strange and a little shameful to do anything else, and I put my newly-mended hand over my mouth and I wept.

I'm not ashamed of it. I've been ashamed of plenty in this account and I'll be ashamed of plenty more but not of that. They thought it was the shock, or the relief, or the Drift, or the long way I'd come, and they were kind about it, the younger one fetched me water — water, freely, into a cup, no collar required, the contrast with the fountain nearly setting me off again — and they let me cry, and the truth that none of them could have known was that I was weeping for a much stupider and more specific thing than my circumstances. I was weeping because no one had ever looked after me, and it had taken falling out of the world entirely, into a society I'd been taught to read as a kind of sterile bureaucratic wonder, for a stranger with a tired face to look at the wreckage of my body and say *we can fix most of this, now, it's routine*, as if I were worth the fixing, as if everyone were, as if the not-fixing were the strange thing and not the other way round.

The box sat quiet in the corner of my eye for a long moment. When it spoke it didn't give me a stat. It just said:

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  ...this place is kinder than the last one.
```

And I have never been able to decide whether that was the most or the least it ever understood.

---

The torc came at the end, and the torc was where the kindness showed its teeth, though they didn't mean it to and I don't think they even saw it as teeth.

The counterman came back once I'd pulled myself together, and he had a thing in his hand, and the under-voice explained it to me as he turned it over — a band, a poor plain one, grey, lighter than the ones the men in the room wore, with a single dull point of light in it. And the machine gave me, clean, the proper name for it — the name for the rings of metal at every throat in this world, the thing I'd been calling a *collar* in my own head all night because I'd had no better word. *Torc*, it said. An old word, worn smooth, older even than the quid for all I knew. The thing was called a torc, and a man without one wasn't a man here, and the one he was holding out to me was the poorest possible kind: a *placeholder*, the machine rendered, after a struggle; a *provisional*; the word it kept circling was something between *temporary* and *on loan*.

"You can't be without one," the under-voice said, threading under the counterman's patient explanation. "Not here. You can't — pay, you can't be — known, you can't be helped past tonight. So. This is a..." — *provisional*, again — "...it gives you a — a temporary standing. You're in the record now. You exist." A pause, while he said something I'd have given a great deal to fully understand, watching my face to see how much landed. "...it is monitored. You understand. It's not a real one. It's so you can — get on your feet. There's a..." — and here the machine gave me the word clean, no struggle, because it was a word it had heard me reach for all night, a word that was about to become the spine of my fear— "...a follow-up. In a few days. You'll come back. We finish the registration properly. Yes?"

A follow-up. A few days. *We finish the registration properly.*

I sat very still, with my mended wrist in my lap, and I understood the shape of the gift exactly, the way I understood the shape of a market about to turn. The placeholder torc was a real kindness and a real leash, and the two were the same object. It would let me exist — pay for water, pass a gate, stop being a person-shaped null that drones returned NULL on and flagged, the very thing that had nearly had me picked up an hour ago. It would let me *get on my feet*. And in a few days it would require me to come back to this desk, or one like it, and finish a registration that I had survived tonight only by being too shocked and too foreign and too sad to answer properly, and the second time around, rested, with the lag worn down and the Xeno-Linguistics ticking up, there would be no shock to hide behind, and the questions would be the real ones — *which haven, what's your full record, where exactly, when exactly, who can confirm you* — and I would have nothing, and the kind tired men of this clinic would slowly realise that the robbed Drifter was a hole in the world wearing a hood, and the patience would curdle, and somewhere a more careful man with a less kind face would come to ask why.

So the torc was a clock. It bought me days and it charged me days. I took it anyway, because the alternative to a clock is a cliff, and I leaned in the way I'd watched everyone lean, and the counterman fitted it at my throat — it settled cool and then warm, snug, and there was a small sensation behind my ears, a click that wasn't a sound, the placeholder reaching into me the small allowed amount — and the under-voice that had been coming from the walls suddenly came from *closer*, from the band itself, clearer, the lag a fraction shorter, the torc doing for me what the room had been doing, carrying me its little portable bridge of translation. The world got, by one notch, more legible. I could have wept again at that, too, but I was empty.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Item equipped: Torc (PLACEHOLDER)
   — status: PROVISIONAL / MONITORED / flagged for review
   — you now read as: a person (barely)
  Quest updated: SURVIVE
   └ new: pass the follow-up (or vanish before it)
```

*Or vanish before it.* The System had started, already, doing the thing it would always do — taking the polite official version of my situation and translating it into the underworld grammar that was the truth of me. *Come back in a few days and finish the registration* meant, to the part of me wearing the Hood, *get legitimate properly or be gone before they look closely*, and both of those required the one thing I still didn't have.

The counterman, winding up, kind to the last, told me the practical things, and the under-voice gave them to me clean now through the torc, and I caught most of them, and one of them landed and stuck and rang like a struck glass.

He told me where to go to make the placeholder into something real — a tech place, upmarket, off the Shambles, where they did collars properly. He told me I'd need clothes that weren't soaked rags, and where for that. And he told me, gently, apologetically, as if breaking bad news, that all of it would cost, that I'd need *funds*, that a man couldn't get far here without them, and I should think about how I'd come by some — and the word the torc gave me for *funds*, the word he used, the plain ordinary local word for money that every man in that room would have used without thinking —

was *quid*.

I must have looked stupid, because he said it again, slower, thinking the translation had failed. "Quid," he repeated, and the torc, faithfully, redundantly, translated the untranslatable, gave me back my own word, *quid*, the word my grandfather had used, the word for a pound, for a note in a pocket, for *can you lend us a quid*, the most ordinary small English word for money there was — and here it was, in the mouth of a man eighteen hundred years and an unguessable distance from England, the living currency of a civilisation that had forgotten the island the word came from, *quid*, worn smooth and carried forward across the whole abyss I'd fallen down, surviving where I had not.

I don't know why that, of everything, was the thing that made the distance real. The upward fountain hadn't done it; the mended wrist hadn't done it. But the *quid* did. Because the fountain and the wrist were *future*, and the future I could brace against, the future was supposed to be strange. The quid was *home*, carried into the future, a piece of my own small grey island floating two thousand years downstream and still in use, still meaning *money*, still asked for across a counter by a tired man at the end of a night shift — and the homeliness of it in that alien mouth opened the distance under me wider than any wonder had, and I had to grip the arms of the chair.

"Quid," I said. "Right. Yeah. I'll — I'll think about the quid."

The box, which had been watching me the way it always watched me, which only ever knew what I knew, put it more plainly than I could have borne to:

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  ! you have 0 quid
  ! you have 0 plan
  ! the clock is running
```

---

They couldn't keep me; I wasn't hurt, not any more, they'd seen to that, and a clinic in the small hours has a finite number of chairs and an infinite supply of the night's casualties. The counterman walked me to the door, the same door I'd come in by, and on the threshold he did a thing I've never forgotten, a small thing, he put his hand on my shoulder — the easy bodily kindness of these people, touch that meant nothing and meant everything — and he said something the torc gave me almost whole, the Xeno-Linguistics ticking, four per cent climbing toward five.

"...not the worst place to land," he said. "The Shambles. It looks like — like a lot. But it hides people kindly. You'll find your feet. Come back, yes? In a few days. We'll sort you out properly."

*Come back. We'll sort you out properly.* He meant it as a promise. I heard it as a deadline. They were the same sentence, the way the torc was the same object, the way the whole night had been one long lesson in how a place could be genuinely, bottomlessly kind and still, without a flicker of malice, close around a man like a trap — because the kindness ran on knowing who you were, and I could not afford, ever, to be known.

I thanked him. I'd picked up the word for it by then, two syllables, I gave it back to him in his own language for the first time and watched it land and watched him smile at the off-worlder making the effort, and it was the first thing I'd said to anyone in this world in their own tongue and it was a thank-you and it was also a goodbye to a man I had comprehensively lied to and would never see again, and I stepped back out under the sign — *care & questions, no collar no problem*, except I had a torc now, a poor grey provisional one, so the sign no longer described me, I'd graduated one rung up the long ladder out of nothing — and into the warm frying-oil dark of the Shambles, mended, named, collared, monitored, flagged, on the clock, and still, in the only currency that mattered, the currency that had outlived my whole world to mock me from a tired man's mouth, completely and utterly broke.

The rain had stopped, somewhere up beyond the levels. It was very late, or very early. I had a body that didn't ache for the first time in years and not one quid to my name and a few days before a kind man's promise turned into a careful man's question, and I stood in the doorway of the future and did the only thing I knew how to do, the thing I'd done in the alley and the thing I'd do for the rest of this story:

I started reading the system, looking for the gap.

There's always a gap. The trick is being the only one bothered enough to look.
