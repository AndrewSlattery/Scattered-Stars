# Chapter Two — Boot

*Draft. First-person past tense. ~6,200 words. Maps to [outline](../outline.md) Beat 1 / [storyboard](../act-1-storyboard.md) Ch 2. System notifications render as set-off interface blocks — see [system.md](../system.md).*

---

The first thing I did in the future was throw up.

I want that recorded honestly, because the genre of story I'd grown up on — the man who falls into another world — tends to skip it, and the skipping is a lie. In the stories he stands, and squints heroically at the strange sky, and says something dry. What actually happens, when your body has been somewhere and your body is now somewhere else and the two facts won't reconcile, is that the animal in you panics through the nearest available exit, and you come up onto your hands and knees on wet stone in the dark and you are sick until there's nothing left, while the impossible smell — frying oil, ozone, that green sweetness — pours down over you and makes it worse.

So. Not heroic. Established.

When it passed I stayed down for a while, on all fours, breathing, because being on all fours was the most that my understanding of the situation could currently support. The stone under my hands was real. It was cold and slightly slick and it had a faint regular ridging to it, a texture, manufactured, not paving I knew. I held onto the texture. It was the only fact in the world that wasn't trying to kill me.

Then I made the mistake of looking up.

I've had a long time to assemble the words for what I saw and I still don't think they're adequate, because the problem wasn't any single thing, it was the *quantity* — the sheer rate of unfamiliar information arriving at a mind that had, ninety seconds earlier, been in a good chair looking at a damp patch shaped like Italy. Let me give it to you the way it came, in the wrong order, too fast.

I was in a sort of alley, except it had no roof and also wasn't outdoors, which is a sentence that meant nothing to me then and means a great deal now. Walls went up on both sides further than I could see, and they weren't blank — they had things growing on them, hanging gardens in the dark, and the leaves of the things were not green. They were the colour of a bruise, deep purple shading to copper at the edges, and they caught a light that came from no source I could find, a soft glow that seemed to live in the walkways themselves. Above me, far up, the gap between the walls was crossed and recrossed by bridges — translucent, glowing faintly along their lengths like something living seen by torchlight — and on the bridges there were shapes moving that were people, and they were going about their business in the middle of the night as if the night were a perfectly ordinary time for a city to be this awake.

Water was running somewhere. More than one somewhere. Bright thin splashes and, under them, a deeper rushing, and a smell of wet that should have been clean and wasn't, because it was tangled up with the frying and the ozone and a hundred kinds of cooking I couldn't name.

And there was a shimmer in the air at the mouth of the alley. A vertical line of distortion, like heat-haze stood on its end, and past it the rain that I could now see falling in the plaza beyond simply — stopped. Hit the shimmer and didn't come through. In the alley where I knelt it was dry. Two feet away, weather.

I think it was the rain that did it. Everything else I could file, however badly, under *foreign* — strange architecture, strange plants, strange light. But rain that obeyed an invisible wall was rain breaking a rule I'd had since birth, and my mind, presented with rain breaking a rule, did the thing minds do when the rules go, which is reach very fast for any framework at all that would let it keep functioning.

And mine had one ready. Mine had spent ten thousand hours in the one place where strangeness comes with an interface.

It happened behind my eyes, or it felt like behind my eyes — I've never settled whether what I see is genuinely *over* the world or simply very insistently *in* me, and I've stopped expecting to settle it. A box. A pane. It flickered into the lower left of my vision the way a notification slides into the corner of a screen, except there was no screen, there was just my field of view and now a thing in the corner of it, faint, jittering, struggling to hold its own edges, as if whatever was drawing it was doing so for the first time and getting it wrong.

```
  ▓ ... ?
  initiali—
  [ERR] no map data
  ████ booting
```

I stared at it. It guttered, steadied, tried again.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Location: UNKNOWN
  Objective: —
  Objective: SURVIVE
```

And I did the thing that I have since decided is the truest thing about me, truer than the freight, truer than the loneliness. Kneeling on alien stone eighteen hundred years from everyone I'd ever failed to properly love, having just been sick in front of a garden the colour of a bruise, looking at an impossible blue box that my own brain had apparently manufactured to keep me from coming apart —

I felt *relieved*.

Because a box I understood. *Objective: SURVIVE* I understood. It was wrong, it was insane, it was a coping mechanism wearing the costume of a heads-up display, and some cold clear part of me knew that even then. But it was legible. It gave the unbearable a *shape*, and a shape you can act on, and I had just spent my whole life learning that a wrong map you can act on beats a true world you can't read.

"Okay," I said, out loud, to the box, to the alley, to nobody. My voice came out cracked and small and very far from dry. "Okay. SURVIVE. Got it."

The box sat there, jittering gently, offering nothing further.

"Bit short on detail," I told it.

It declined to elaborate. They always would. I'd spend a long time learning that the System only ever told me what some buried part of me had already worked out and dressed up as information — that it was a mirror I'd mistaken for a guide — but that first night, in the alley, I was just grateful not to be alone with the rain.

---

I got up. That was harder than it sounds; my legs had the boneless quality of a thing recently assembled. I put a hand on the wall, into the cool of the bruise-coloured leaves — they were faintly warm, the leaves, which I hadn't expected and didn't like — and I got vertical and stood swaying and took an inventory of myself, which the box, unhelpfully, did not do for me.

I had my clothes: jeans, a t-shirt, the grey hoodie I'd been wearing in the chair because the flat was always cold. I had my trainers. I had, in my pockets, my keys, which were now keys to nothing in the universe, and a few coins, and — I felt for it and my heart did something complicated — my phone, dead weight, dark, useless, and the single most precious object I owned, because it was the only proof that the chair and the damp Italy and my brother's careful voice had ever existed at all. I had myself. That was the inventory. A man and his anachronisms.

I made my way to the mouth of the alley, to the shimmer, and stopped a careful distance back from it, because I had developed in the last four minutes a healthy respect for invisible walls. Up close it wasn't quite invisible; it had a faint oily sheen to it, a soap-bubble iridescence, and a sound, almost too low to hear, a sort of held breath. I put my hand out, slowly, the way you would to a dog you didn't know.

My fingers went through. No resistance, just a faint cool tingle, like the static off a screen. On the far side, rain. I felt it land on the back of my hand, ordinary rain, cold, and I drew my hand back wet and it was the most normal thing that had happened to me since the white came up over the monitor and I nearly cried with the relief of it. Rain. I knew rain.

I stepped through.

The plaza on the other side was the moment I understood that *foreign* wasn't going to be a big enough word, and that I was going to need *future*, with everything that word drags behind it.

It opened out — a wide irregular space, not square, nothing here was square, fitted into the impossible geometry of the place like water finding a level. The ground sloped, gently but unmistakably, and I'd come to learn that the whole district did this, that it was built across a hillside in a way that made *down* a slippery word, but in that first moment all I knew was that the plaza tilted and that this was somehow worse than if it had been flat. There were trees, the bruise-and-copper trees, real ones, growing up out of the stone in beds lit from below. There was a fountain in the middle and the fountain was wrong — the water went *up*, rose in a column and hung and turned over at the top in a slow blossom and fell back, contained by nothing I could see, lit from inside so the whole thing glowed like a held breath made of water — and around the fountain, in the rain, under the impossible upward water, there were *people*.

Dozens of them. More coming and going. And every single one of them was a man.

I didn't clock that consciously, not at first. It arrived as a low wrongness, the way the regular pulse in the order flow had arrived as a flinch before I had the words for it. A crowd that was somehow too uniform, a note held too long. It would be days before I understood it fully, before anyone explained, before I stopped flinching. That first night it just sat at the back of my attention, unexamined: *something about them, something about all of them*, under the much louder fact that they were tall.

That, I clocked straight away. They were *tall*. Not all of them, not freakishly, but the average of them sat a clear head-span above the average I'd grown up inside, and they were broad with it, built, the way you'd be if you'd never once in your life gone hungry or been cold or done the small daily damage that being poor does to a body. They moved like people who were comfortable. They wore clothes I had no categories for — drapes and closures and surfaces that shifted when the light hit them — and they walked through the upward-falling rainless rain of the plaza with the absolute ease of people in a place that belonged to them.

And at the throat of every one of them, catching the fountain-light, was a ring of metal.

A band. A collar, almost, but it sat lower, around the base of the neck, snug, and they were all different — I could see that even from across the plaza — some plain and dull like brushed steel, some bright, some with little lights that moved in them, a man near the fountain had one that glowed a soft blue and as I watched he touched it with two fingers and spoke and I understood, with the lurch of someone recognising a custom he is failing, that the band was a *thing*. A device. Everyone had one. It was as ordinary to them as a face.

I didn't have one.

I became aware of my own neck the way you become aware of a missing tooth, the tongue going to the gap. Bare. The collar of the hoodie, the bare skin above it, the absence of the ring of metal that every single person in this place wore as plainly as their own head.

A man crossing the plaza glanced at me. An ordinary glance, the kind you give a stranger, and then his eyes did a small specific thing — flicked to my throat, found nothing, and slid off me, wrong-footed, the way your eye slides off a face where a feature should be and isn't. He didn't stare. That was almost worse. He just registered *something missing* and looked away, faintly disturbed, and walked on, and I stood there feeling the gap at my neck like a brand.

The box, in the corner of my eye, chose that moment to find its feet.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Anomaly: no neural collar detected
  Social status: UNVERIFIED
  ! you do not read as a person here
```

That last line wasn't information. I knew that even as I read it. The System didn't know what the collar was any better than I did; it was just my own animal panic, the same instinct that had clocked the wrongness of the crowd, fed back to me in a tidy sentence so I could bear it. But God, it was an accurate sentence. *You do not read as a person here.* I put my hood up. I don't fully know why — instinct, the cold, the rain, the desire of every hunted thing to make itself a smaller target — I reached up and pulled the grey hood over my head, and it came down over my forehead and it covered, I realised, the bare betraying skin of my neck, the gap where the metal should be.

The box flickered.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Improvised concealment: NECK
  Class assigned: HOOD
```

I let out a sound that was half a laugh and half something with no name. A class. It had given me a *class*. The deepest, most embarrassing engine of me, the one that had spent a life turning grief into stats because stats it could survive, had looked at a man hiding his shameful naked throat in a doorway and labelled it a *build*, a role, *Hood*, and the worst part, the part I'd never quite forgive it for, was that it helped. Naming the disaster made the disaster a thing I was *doing* rather than a thing that was happening to me, and a thing you're doing has a next move.

"Fine," I said to it, under the hood, in the rain that wasn't reaching me. "Hood. Sure. What's my first quest, then."

*Objective: SURVIVE*, said the box, because it only ever knew what I knew, and what I knew was that I was alone and bare-necked and illegible in a city of comfortable giants, with no money, in the rain, in the future, and that I was going to have to find a way through this the way I'd found a way through everything — by reading the corner of the system that nobody else thought was worth reading.

So I went looking for the rules. And the city, with great patience, began to teach them to me, the only way a city ever teaches anything, which is by saying no.

---

The first no was water.

I'd been sick; I'd brought up everything I had; my mouth tasted of bile and ozone and my throat was a raw closed thing, and there in the middle of the plaza was a fountain the size of a bus pouring out an endless contained column of water, and I was thirsty in the specific desperate way of a body in shock. I went to it. Of course I went to it. And around the base, where the water gathered and was held in its invisible bowl, there were — fixtures. Little stations, waist-height, with basins, where a few people were stopping to drink, and I went to the nearest one and there was no tap, no button, no handle, just a smooth recess and a faint patient glow, and a man ahead of me leaned in and the water *came*, rose to meet his mouth, and he drank, and straightened, and as he straightened he touched two fingers to the ring at his throat, an automatic gesture, the way you'd tap a card, and walked on.

I leaned in. Nothing came. I waited, and put my hand into the recess, and felt about, and nothing came. The glow stayed patient. I understood — slowly, then all at once, the cold-water understanding that would become the texture of my first weeks here — that the water was not free. That nothing was free. That the man had *paid* for his mouthful with the touch of two fingers to the metal at his neck, an act so small and so automatic he didn't know he was doing it, the way you don't know you're breathing, and that the reason the water wouldn't come for me was that the city had reached out to take its tiny payment and found, at my throat, nothing to take it from. No collar. No person. No account.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Transaction declined: no collar
  ! you cannot pay for anything
```

"I can see that," I told it. My voice did something on the second word. I was so thirsty.

I'd love to tell you I problem-solved. The truth is I stood at the fountain station for a moment with my hood up and my bare throat and a thirst like a fist, and a kind of vertigo opened under me — not the slope of the plaza, a deeper slope, the understanding that I had fallen into a place that was rich and orderly and kind, genuinely kind, you could see the kindness in how unhurried and unhungry everyone was, and that the kindness was all routed through a thing I didn't have, and that without the thing I was going to stand at the edge of all this plenty and not be able to drink. There is a particular cruelty to being locked out of abundance. Scarcity I understood; I'd been poor in a tired ordinary way my whole life. This was new. This was water I could see, behind glass made of an economy.

A man at the next station finished drinking and moved off, and on an impulse made entirely of thirst I stepped into the place he'd left, quickly, while the glow was maybe still warm from his payment — I genuinely thought that, *while it's still warm*, as if it were a kettle — and leaned in, and for one second nothing, and then the water rose.

I have never in my life been so glad of anything. It came up cool and clean and tasting faintly of stone and I drank like an animal, gulping, my hand braced on the basin, the rawness in my throat easing, and I thought, in the giddy way of someone who has just stolen successfully for the first time, *there's the first rule, there's the exploit, the city has a lag in it and the lag is mine* —

and then a small sharp tone sounded from the station, not loud, but clean, a public sound, and the water cut off, and a soft line of light ran red around the rim of the basin, and across the plaza a few heads turned. Not many. Two, three. But they turned, toward the sound, toward me, the man at the broken station with his hood up and his face down, and I understood that the city had noticed the lag too, the way a body notices it's been bitten, and that it had said so, out loud, in a voice designed to make exactly three people look up.

I stepped back from the station. I kept my head down. I walked, not fast, fast would have been worse, away across the tilting plaza with the upward water glowing behind me, and I felt the three pairs of eyes lose interest one by one, and the System, in the corner of my vision, posted a thing I have never forgotten, because it was the moment the game stopped being a comfort and started, also, being a leash:

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Petty theft logged (public)
  Heat: minor
  ! this is how it goes for people like you
```

---

The second no was a door, and the door taught me what the first no had only implied: that I wasn't merely broke. That I was *unaccounted for*, which in a place like this was a different and larger kind of nakedness than not having money.

I'd left the plaza by the first opening I came to, because standing still felt dangerous, and the opening led into — I didn't have words for it then, I do now — an arcade, a colonnaded run of shopfronts half-sheltered by the levels above, and the architecture began doing the thing I'd come to know as the gradient, the slow imperceptible slide from outdoor to indoor with no door ever marking the change, the rain falling at one end and not at the other and no wall between. There were people here too, fewer, and shops lit warm, and the smell of cooking got stronger, and at the end of the arcade, where it tipped over into something more enclosed, there was a low broad opening with light spilling out and a current of people going down into it, and a sign above it that pulsed gently with characters I couldn't read.

I'd worked out by then that *down* was where the warmth was — the deeper you went off the open plazas, the more enclosed and lit and climate-held it got — and I was cold, the rain had got into me before I'd learned the trick of the shimmer-walls, and I was operating on the simple logic of a cold animal moving toward heat. So I went with the current toward the bright opening, and the current carried me down a short broad ramp toward a row of barriers — waist-high, smooth, paired, the obvious skeleton of a gate, a turnstile by any other name — and the people ahead of me were going through them without breaking stride, each one touching two fingers to the collar at their throat as they passed, that same automatic tap, and the barriers opening for each of them like water, like the city simply knew them and stood aside.

I knew, by then. I want that understood. I wasn't a fool; I'd had the water lesson. I knew before I reached the barrier that it wasn't going to open for me. But I was cold and frightened and the warmth was on the other side and there's a kind of hope that operates below the level of what you know, and I let the current carry me down to the gate, and I lifted my hand toward my bare throat in an awful little parody of the gesture everyone else was making, touching nothing, paying with nothing, and the barrier stayed shut, and behind me the current of people, which had been flowing, *stopped*.

Just for a second. A small human concertina, the people behind me checking their stride because the man in front — me — had walked up to a gate and not gone through it, had stood there with his hand at his bare neck doing nothing, and the wrongness of that rippled back through them, three or four men breaking their rhythm to look at me, and one of them, close behind, a big easy-moving man with a collar that had a green light in it, said something.

I didn't understand it. That was the moment I understood that I didn't understand *anything*, that on top of everything else I was deaf and dumb here, that the rules I'd been so relieved to start learning were written in a language that arrived at my ears as a warm flowing nonsense, musical, unbroken, completely opaque. The big man said his sentence — a question, I thought, from the shape of it, not unkind, possibly *are you all right* or *are you stuck* or *are you going to move* — and he was looking at my throat now, openly, the way you'd look at a man who'd walked up to a cashpoint and tried to pay with his hand, and his expression was sliding from mild concern toward something more careful, and I did the only thing my body knew how to do, which was get out.

"Sorry," I said, in English, which meant nothing to him, the word landing in the warm flow like a stone. "Sorry. Wrong — sorry." And I ducked sideways out of the current, away from the gate, back up the ramp against the flow of comfortable men going down into the warmth I couldn't buy my way into, and I felt the big man's eyes on my back, and I felt — this was new, this was a flavour of fear I hadn't had at the fountain — I felt *noticed*. Not as a thief. As a *problem*. As a man who didn't read as a person, doing person-shaped things badly, in public, where people could see.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Access denied: transit gate
  Language: not recognised
  Cover: NONE
  ! you are conspicuous
```

*Cover*, it said. I noticed the word even then, even cold and frightened and fleeing up a ramp. Some part of me, the part that read systems, filed it away. The problem wasn't only that I couldn't pay or speak. The problem was that I had no *story*. A man can be foreign; foreign is a thing the world has a slot for. But I was foreign with no account and no language and no collar and no explanation, and a man like that isn't a foreigner, he's an alarm waiting to be raised, and I was going to have to become someone the world had a slot for before the world decided what I was on its own.

I didn't have the someone yet. That would come later, at a desk, under questioning, improvised over an abyss. For now I just knew I needed it, the way you know you need a coat before you know which one.

---

The third no was the one that nearly finished me, and it didn't even involve a transaction. It was just a man doing his job.

I'd gone back up, away from the gate, and worked my way along the gradient looking for I-didn't-know-what — somewhere to be that wasn't a barrier — and I'd found a stretch of half-alley, walls on both sides and only an awning overhead, puddles on the ground from where the rain got in at the edges, lit by the warm glow of the walkways and the spill from a few small shopfronts. It was quieter. It was the closest thing to a back street I'd found, and back streets I understood, back streets were where you went to be unwatched, and I leaned against a wall in the dark between two pools of shopfront light and tried to think, which I hadn't properly done since the white came up over the monitor.

Here is as far as I got with thinking: *I am somewhere I cannot drink, cannot enter, cannot speak, cannot pay, and cannot explain. I have a dead phone, some useless keys, and a coping mechanism that thinks it's a video game. The one asset I have is the same asset I've always had — I can read a system if I sit with it. So sit with it. Read this one. Find the lag, find the gap, find the corner nobody's watching.*

It was a good thought. It was, in fact, the entire strategy of my survival, arrived at in a wet half-alley in the first hour, and I've never improved on it. But I didn't get to act on it, because that was the moment the light found me.

It came around the corner at the far end of the half-alley — not a torch, a *presence*, a low hovering thing the size of a cat, matte and dark, moving with the unbothered patience of a machine on a route. It had no face but it had attention; you could feel the attention come off it like heat. A drone, an eye, a warden, I didn't have the word — I'd learn the locals barely thought about them, they were just part of the weather of a public space, the way a CCTV camera was part of the weather of mine. It drifted down the half-alley at walking pace, and as it came it painted things lightly with a band of soft light, the shopfronts, the puddles, the few people passing, and each person it touched it touched and released, touched and released, a soft acknowledging sweep, *you, fine; you, fine*, reading the collar, finding the account, moving on.

It reached me.

The band of light came across me and *stopped*.

I have tried many times to describe the next few seconds and the closest I've come is this: it was like being looked at by a closed door. The thing had no eyes and made no sound and yet its whole patient attention gathered onto me and *held*, because it had done to me what it did to everyone — reached out to read the person — and found no person to read. No collar. No account. No language it could hail me in. Just a shape, hooded, bare-necked, standing in the dark of a back alley where the warm flowing rules didn't quite reach, returning nothing.

And in the corner of my vision the box that was my own fear wearing a costume went red for the first time.

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  ! SCANNED
  ! identity: returns NULL
  ! status: ESCALATING
  RUN
```

It hung in front of the little dark machine, the band of light pinning me to the wall, and I felt the attention deepen — felt, or imagined, or both, I've never been able to draw the line, that the thing was *deciding*, that somewhere up the chain a question was being asked about the null in the half-alley, and that the answer to the question was going to be a man, a real one, with a job, who came to find out why a person-shaped hole was standing in the dark not reading as a person — and every animal instinct I had, the ones that had kept things like me alive across the whole eighteen hundred years I'd skipped, stood up at once and screamed the same word the box was screaming.

I ran.

Not heroically. I shoved off the wall and I went, hood up, head down, the wrong way, the way that wasn't the gate and wasn't the plaza, deeper into the half-alley and through a gap where it forked and into a narrower run and through an opening into a covered space full of the smell of frying and the clatter of a kitchen, and I kept going, past tables, past startled faces, into another corridor that was suddenly fully indoors, skylit, glowing, a maze, a three-dimensional maze of levels and bridges and gaps and sudden balconies, exactly the kind of place a thing like me was built to disappear into — and I disappeared into it. I went up a short flight that turned into a walkway that crossed over the very half-alley I'd fled, and I looked down through the translucent glowing floor of the bridge and saw, far below, the little dark machine still hanging where I'd left it, in no hurry, its attention released now that its null had fled, already drifting on along its patient route, *you, fine; you, fine*.

It hadn't chased me. It hadn't needed to. It had logged me and moved on, and somewhere a record now existed of a person-shaped null that had been scanned and had run, and that record would be a problem later, the way everything I did in those first hours would be a problem later — but in the moment all I knew was that the attention was off me, that the maze had closed around me like water, and that I was, for the first time since the white, *hidden*.

I stood on the glowing bridge in the middle of the maze, gripping a rail I didn't trust, shaking, soaked, my breath going like a bellows, and I understood the one genuinely useful thing the night had to teach me, the thing that would keep me alive:

The city was a machine you logged into. Out in the open, on the plazas and at the gates and under the patient drones, you had to be a *person* — accounted, collared, legible — and I couldn't be, not yet, maybe not ever. But the machine had gaps. It had this — the maze, the back-of-house, the gradient's deep interior where the rules thinned out and the chaos was old and unplanned and nobody could watch every fork of it. The Shambles, I'd learn it was called, and the name was a joke and also exactly right. Out there, I was a null. In here, I was just one more shape in a place that had given up on accounting for its shapes three centuries ago.

I was going to have to live in the gaps until I could afford to be a person. That was the rule. That was the first real rule the future taught me, and it taught it to me by trying, very gently and without any malice at all, to have me picked up.

---

I don't know how long I stayed on the bridge. Long enough for the shaking to ease, for the bellows of my breathing to slow, for the cold to come back in now that the running heat had drained off. Long enough to be, underneath the fear, simply and enormously tired — two hours' sleep, a death, a resurrection, an hour of the city saying no, and my body was sending up the message that whatever else was true, it was *done*, it needed warmth and rest and it needed them before it needed answers.

I made myself move. Down off the bridge, deeper in, toward warmth, the cold-animal logic again, and the maze obliged the way it would always oblige, opening a way that led down through a covered arcade where the air got warmer and the rain was a memory, and the shopfronts here were still lit even at this dead hour, small enterprises that apparently never closed, and the people were fewer and rougher and paid me no attention at all, which after the plaza was its own kind of mercy.

And it was there, half-dead on my feet, that I caught the first thing in this whole world that I could *read*.

It was a sign. Above a doorway, wider than the others, with a steadier light, the doorway itself open and giving onto a sort of room with benches and a counter and a few people sitting and waiting in the particular slumped way that people wait in the small hours of a place that exists to help them. The sign above it pulsed gently, characters I didn't know, the warm opaque script that had defeated me all night — and then, as I stood looking at it, swaying, it *changed*. Flickered, resolved, and gave me, underneath the characters I couldn't read, a second line in characters I *could* — not English, not quite, an English with the bones showing, the spelling drifted and the words worn smooth by an unimaginable amount of time, but English, recognisably, miraculously, *English*:

`care & questions — all welcome — no collar no problem`

I stood and read it three times.

I understood, dimly, what had happened, though it would take me days to understand it properly: that something in this place — the sign, the city, some patient routine running underneath everything the way the drone ran its route — had been listening to me all night. To my muttering. To my *sorry, sorry, wrong*. To whatever the System was, leaking out of me in a language no one here had heard in two thousand years, and it had been *working*, the way I worked at a pattern, sampling and resolving, and it had got enough of me now to offer me a line back. The translation was bad and slow and a beat behind. It didn't matter. It was a hand in the dark. *No collar, no problem.* The one door, in a whole city of closed ones, that claimed not to care about the thing I didn't have.

I knew it might be a trap. The part of me that read systems flagged it instantly — *care and questions*, and the *questions* part was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence, a place that helped the collarless was also a place that *catalogued* the collarless, and I was a person-shaped null who'd already been scanned once tonight, and walking willingly into a room full of people whose job was to ask questions was not, on the face of it, the move of a man trying to stay in the gaps.

But I was so tired. And I was so cold. And I was so completely, comprehensively out of any other door.

The box, in the corner of my eye, had gone quiet a while ago, the red long faded. Now, as I stood swaying in front of the one sign in the world I could read, it stirred, and offered me, with what I can only call gentleness, the smallest possible push:

```
  ▓▓ SYSTEM ▓▓
  Objective: SURVIVE
  Lead found: shelter / aid (unverified)
  Risk: questions
  ...go in?
```

It only ever knew what I knew. And what I knew, standing there, was that *survive* and *stay hidden* had finally come apart, that I could not hide my way to a drink of water and a place to sleep, that the gaps would keep me free and the gaps would also, slowly, let me die of exposure and thirst in a city of plenty, and that somewhere in the next room was the first hand anyone had offered me since the white came up over the monitor, and that I was going to have to take it, and lie to it, and hope.

I didn't have the lie yet. I'd build it in there, at the desk, out of nothing, with my heart going — the robbed man, the off-worlder, the story the world had a slot for. But that's the next part.

I pulled my hood a little lower over my bare throat. I took a breath of the warm frying-oil air of the future.

I went in.
