# Chapter One — Old World

*Draft. First-person past tense. ~6,000 words. Maps to [outline](../outline.md) Beat 0 / [storyboard](../act-1-storyboard.md) Ch 1.*

---

The night I stopped existing, I was making a killing in freight.

Not real freight. I want to be clear about that up front, because later on a lot of people are going to assume I was somebody — a trader, an engineer, a man with a profession you could put on a card — and the truth is I moved sealed boxes that other people had packed from one shelf to a slightly better shelf, and at night I went home and pretended to move much larger boxes across a galaxy that didn't exist. That was the shape of me. Two jobs, both of them about getting things from where they were to where they ought to be, and only one of them paid, and it wasn't the one I was good at.

The one I was good at was called *Ironroad*.

If you've never played it, I can't really make you understand, and if you have, I don't need to. It was a logistics game. That's the boring way to say it and also the only honest one. You ran a freight network — first one line, then a junction, then a region, then, if you were the kind of obsessive who didn't have anything better to do at three in the morning, a whole continent of routes humming under your hands like a body you'd learned the breathing of. Ore went one way and fuel went the other and grain sat in silos waiting on a price, and the price moved because ten thousand other players were doing the same thing in the same world, and the whole point — the entire glittering point of the thing — was that nobody could hold it all in their head.

Except you could get *closer*. That was the secret nobody advertised. Most players treated it like a spreadsheet with weather. You watched your numbers, you reacted, you patched the leak after it had already cost you. But if you sat with it long enough — if you stopped looking at the numbers and started looking at the *shape* the numbers made — the network stopped being a spreadsheet and became a thing with a grain to it, like wood, and you could feel which way it wanted to split before it split.

I could do that. It was the only thing in my life I could do that nobody had to take my word for.

That night I'd been running the Coronation Belt — a stupid name, somebody on the dev team had been feeling romantic — which was the long ore corridor between two systems that hated each other, and a war event had kicked off four hours earlier and snarled half the lanes. Everyone was bleeding. The big consortiums, the ones with logos and Discord servers and actual real money flowing under the table, were rerouting in a panic and stepping on each other and driving the spot price of a refined alloy through the roof because they were all making the same obvious move at once.

And I just — saw it. The way you see that a queue at the supermarket is about to collapse because the woman three back has a trolley and no patience. I saw that everyone's obvious move was going to converge on one junction in about ninety seconds, and that the junction would jam, and that the jam would mean a window — narrow, maybe two real minutes — where a small operator with hauliers already in position could undercut the panic and sell into it before the price corrected.

I had hauliers already in position. I always had hauliers already in position. It's not foresight; it's just paying attention to the thing nobody else thinks is worth attention.

I want to describe the next two minutes properly because I'm aware they're the high point of the chapter of my life that was about to end, and a man should get to keep his high points.

I didn't touch most of my fleet. That was the discipline of it — the move was to *not* move, to let everyone else's frantic energy do the work and slot one cargo through the gap they'd left. I had a single train of refined alloy sitting a junction back, dark, brakes on, and I released it into the window like dropping a coin into a slot you've measured with your eye, and it slid through the jam while the big boys were still queued behind their own cleverness, and it sold — I still remember the figure, which tells you something about me — it sold for four hundred and eleven thousand credits against a build cost of sixty.

In the channel, somebody I'd never spoken to typed: *who is THROUGHLINE and how did they know.*

Throughline was me. I'd picked the handle at fifteen and never grown out of it.

I didn't answer. I never answered. There was a pleasure in being the question instead of the man, and besides, the honest answer — *I knew because I have nothing else, mate, I have so completely nothing else that I have made a study of the exact corner of the universe you can afford to ignore* — wasn't the kind of thing you put in a channel.

I leaned back. The chair did its tired exhale. On the screen the network glowed its little green and amber glow, all its lanes flowing again now the jam had cleared, and for about thirty seconds I felt the specific warm completeness that I have never, before or since, felt anywhere that had weather and other people in it. Everything was where it ought to be. Everything was moving. I had read the world correctly and the world had agreed with me, in numbers, which is the only language that can't lie to your face.

Then my phone buzzed with the shift reminder, and the warmth went out of me like someone had opened a door.

---

I worked nights at a parcel hub on the edge of an industrial estate that the city had stopped pretending to care about some time in the 1990s. You'll have driven past one. Long steel shed, lit like an interrogation, a car park full of the cars of people who would rather have been anywhere, and inside, a river of cardboard.

My official title was Flow Coordinator, which I'd found genuinely funny for about a week. What it meant was that I sat in a glass box above the sortation floor and watched a wall of screens show me the parcels coming in and the lorries going out and the choke points building between them, and I told the floor — by tapping at a console, or, when the system was down, which was often, by leaning on a microphone — where to send people and product so the river didn't back up.

It was, and I say this with the bitterness fully drained out of it by time and distance, exactly the same job as *Ironroad*. Routes, junctions, flows, the grain of a system you could feel if you bothered. The difference was that in *Ironroad* the network rewarded me and remembered me and let me be the question nobody could answer, and at the hub the network belonged to a logistics company headquartered in a country I'd never been to, and the only feedback I ever got was when something went wrong, and the only people who spoke to me were the ones who had to.

There was a man called Raj who ran the loading bays and was decent to me in the offhand way of someone who's decent to everyone, and there was a supervisor called Dunne who'd been promoted out of the kind of competence that makes you good at a job and into the kind of meeting that makes you bad at life, and there was a whole floor of people I knew by their hi-vis and their gait and their preferred swearing and not, in most cases, by their names, because the agency rotated them through so fast that learning a name was a small grief waiting to happen.

That night Dunne caught me on the stairs and told me, in the tone of a man delivering good news, that the company was bringing in a new system. Predictive flow management, he said. It would do what I did, he said, except — and here he laughed, the laugh of a man who has not understood the sentence he is about to say — except it would do it *automatically*. Learn the patterns. No more guesswork.

"It's not guesswork," I said.

"Whatever you call it," said Dunne, kindly, and patted my shoulder, and went off to a meeting about it.

I stood on the stairs for a second with the river of cardboard going past below me and I thought about the alloy train sliding through the jam, the clean coin-in-a-slot certainty of it, four hundred and eleven thousand credits, *who is THROUGHLINE and how did they know* — and I thought about a machine learning to do the one thing I could do, the *only* thing, doing it without ever once feeling the grain of it, and I want to tell you I felt rage, because rage would be a more flattering thing to have felt.

What I actually felt was recognised. As if the universe had finally got round to confirming a suspicion I'd been carrying for years: that the thing I was good at wasn't rare, it was just unpaid, and any sufficiently bored machine could be taught it, and the only reason I'd ever felt special doing it was that the people around me had been too tired to bother.

I worked my shift. The system went down twice and I leaned on the microphone and pulled the floor through both jams by feel, by grain, by the thing the machine was going to learn to do for free, and nobody thanked me, because why would they, the river didn't back up, and a river that doesn't back up is invisible. That's the whole tragedy of the job, if you want it in one line. You only exist when you fail.

---

I had a flat. I should describe it but there's so little to describe that the lack is the description. One room and a kitchen the estate agent had called a kitchenette to justify the rent. A bed I'd bought off a man who was emigrating, which I'd thought was a hopeful sort of provenance until I realised I'd be sleeping every night in furniture someone had been glad to leave the country to get away from. A window that looked at another window. A good chair, the only thing I'd spent real money on, because if you're going to spend your life sitting down you should at least not ruin your back doing it, and the chair was where I lived, in the way that other people live in a marriage or a garden.

I had people, technically. That's the thing nobody tells you about loneliness — it isn't usually an absence of people, it's a population of near-misses. There was a group chat from university that pinged about twice a month with photos of other people's holidays and weddings and, increasingly, children, to which I contributed a thumbs-up and the occasional joke that I drafted more carefully than anything I'd written for work. There was a brother in another city who I loved and rang at Christmas and whose voice, on the phone, had the cautious warmth you'd use on someone you'd heard had been unwell. There were the lads in the *Ironroad* channel, who knew me as Throughline and would have been genuinely upset, in an abstract way, to learn that Throughline had died, and would have posted a sad react, and would have meant it, and would have moved on by the next server reset, which is exactly the amount of grief I'd have wanted from them and no more.

That night I got in from the hub at half six in the morning, which is its own particular flavour of wrong — the world starting up as you shut down, the birds doing their idiot optimism, the school-run beginning while you're trying to find the off-switch — and I did the thing I did every morning, which was sit in the good chair, and open *Ironroad*, and let the network take me.

I told myself it was to unwind. It was not to unwind. I want to be honest in this account because honesty is the one thing the next part of my life would beat permanently into me, so: it was not to unwind. It was that the game was the only place I was the size I felt inside. Out here I was a man a machine could replace, a thumbs-up in a chat, a voice you'd be careful with on the phone. In there I was Throughline, and the world was legible, and effort showed up as numbers, and the numbers went up, and nobody could take my word for any of it because the word wasn't required.

I played until the light through the looking-at-another-window window went from grey to a different grey. And somewhere in there — I'd had maybe two hours' sleep in the last twenty-four, I want that on the record too, because it's the obvious explanation and I've never been able to fully rule it out — somewhere in there I did the thing I am least proud of and most defined by.

I made a wish.

Not on a star. There were no stars; there was a ceiling with a damp patch shaped like Italy. I made it into the game's chat box, the global one, where it would scroll past ten thousand strangers and mean nothing, which is the only place a man like me could ever have said it. I'd watched somebody lose an hour of work to a server hiccup and rage-quit, and I'd felt the particular tiredness of someone who has spent his whole life being good at a game that doesn't count and bad at a life that does, and I typed:

*honestly i'd take this over the real thing. clear rules. you can SEE the points going up. somebody give me the reset button, i'll start again properly, i swear i'd play it right the second time*

And I hit enter, and it scrolled away into the river of other people's nonsense, and I felt the small relief of having said a true thing where it couldn't do any damage.

I have thought about those words more than I have thought about anything else I have ever said. *Clear rules. You can see the points going up. Give me the reset button. I'd play it right the second time.* I said them as a joke, into a void, half-asleep, the way you'd say *I could murder a coffee*. I did not know — how could I have known — that something was listening in a register I didn't have a word for, and that it would take me at my exact, literal, idiot word, and that for a long time afterwards I would lie awake on the far side of an impossible distance and replay the wording and think: *I should have been more careful. I should have specified. I never said anything about a torc.*

But that's getting ahead. I didn't have the vocabulary for any of it yet. I just made my wish, and went back to the network, and that's when I found the glitch.

---

Here is the part I have never been able to explain, and have therefore learned to tell plainly, because plainness is the only thing that survives a story nobody believes.

There was a pattern in the data that should not have been there.

I have to make you understand how *wrong* this was, in the specific terms of the thing, or it'll just sound like a man getting spooked by a screen. *Ironroad's* economy was procedural but it wasn't *random* — that was the whole game, the whole reason I loved it. Randomness you can't read. This had grain. Underneath the ten thousand players and the war event and the alloy price there were deep currents, the slow tides the developers had built in, and over years of doing nothing else I had learned the feel of those tides the way a fisherman learns a coast.

And there was something in the tide that wasn't the tide.

I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know too well — not as a sound but as a flinch. A regularity in the order flow across the Coronation Belt, a pulse, faint, far under the noise, that didn't match anything the game was supposed to do. I told myself it was a bot. The economy was full of bots; people ran scripts to farm credits, and a script leaves a signature, a too-even rhythm, the tell of a thing with no grain.

So I did what I always did with a signature. I followed it. I pulled my own freight off the lanes that mattered and started, almost idly, almost the way you worry a loose tooth, mapping the pulse — where it touched the network, where it didn't, the intervals between. I built a little model of it in the corner of my screen, the way I built a model of everything, and I sat with it, and I let it tell me its shape.

It took about twenty minutes to understand that the pulse was not a bot, because a bot is trying to make money and this was not trying to make money. It wasn't buying low and selling high. It wasn't doing anything a player would do. It was just — *sampling*. Touching the network at points, lightly, in a sequence, like fingers running down a row of switches to find out which ones were live. And the sequence wasn't random and it wasn't economic and after a while, with the specific cold focus that was the best of me, I saw what it actually was.

It was a search.

Something was searching the network. Not for a price, not for an exploit. For a *player*. It was testing the order flow to find the one operator who would respond to a pattern this faint — running the equivalent of a sound too high for almost anyone to hear, and waiting to see who turned their head.

And I had turned my head. I had pulled my fleet off the lanes that made money and spent twenty minutes mapping a signal that, by every rule of the game I had given my life to, I should have ignored, because it wasn't worth anything. I had done the one thing almost no other player in ten thousand would do. I had paid attention to the corner of the universe you can afford to ignore.

I had answered.

The cold focus tipped, very gently, into something else. I sat very still in the good chair, in the grey light, two hours of sleep behind me and a wish still warm in my mouth, and I watched the pulse in the corner of my screen change. It had been sampling the whole network, broad and faint. Now it stopped being broad. It pulled in. Every light touch it had been making across the Coronation Belt withdrew, folded inward, and concentrated — and I knew, with the certainty I'd felt about the alloy train, the coin-in-the-slot certainty that had never once been wrong about a network in my life, that it was concentrating on *me*. On Throughline. On the one operator who had heard the note and turned.

The model in the corner of my screen, the little map I'd built of the thing, did something a model is not supposed to do.

It completed itself.

I hadn't finished it. I'd mapped maybe a third of the pattern, enough to recognise it, the way you recognise a face from a fragment. But as I watched, the rest of it drew itself in — the parts I hadn't found, the intervals I hadn't measured — filling in faster than I could read, the whole shape resolving at once, and it was beautiful and it was *aimed*, and the feeling that went through me was not fear, not yet, it was the appalling intimacy of being *recognised back*. Of having spent a whole life as the question nobody could answer, and finding, at the worst possible moment, in the one place I felt safe, that something had been quietly working out the answer. That something had run a search across an entire invented world to find a man with exactly my one useless gift, and had found him, and now knew his name.

The lights in the flat browned out. Not flickered — *browned*, dimmed and held, the way they do when something enormous somewhere draws more power than the grid expected, and the screen flared white, and the white did not stop at the edges of the screen.

I'd like to say I did something. Pulled the plug. Ran. The honest version is that I sat in my good chair, the only thing I'd ever spent real money on, and I felt the pattern reach the edge of the monitor and keep coming, out into the room, and the last fully human thought I had on Earth — I've turned it over so many times it's worn smooth — was not *what is this* and was not *help*.

It was: *oh. It's a reset button.*

It was a stupid thought. It was the thought of a tired man who had made a wish twenty minutes earlier and whose mind, faced with the impossible, reached for the most recent thing it understood. But I've come to think it was also, in some way I'll never be able to prove, *correct* — that whatever was reaching for me had gone looking, specifically, for someone who wanted the thing it was about to do, and had found a market for it in a flat above an industrial estate, in a man who'd just said, out loud, into a void, *give me the reset button, I'd play it right the second time.*

The white came up over everything like a tide coming in over a coast a fisherman thought he knew.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me, in the half-second I had left to be surprised. I'd assumed, in the abstract way you assume things about your own ending, that it would hurt. Instead there was a sensation I have only ever been able to compare to the moment a word you've been hunting for finally arrives — that click, that *there it is*, the satisfaction of a thing falling into the place it was always meant to occupy. As if I were the word, and something vast had been hunting for me, and had finally got me, and was — there's no better way to say it — *relieved*.

And then the click, and the white, and the warm completeness I had only ever felt looking at a network that was all flowing the way it should —

and then it was gone, all of it, the chair and the damp Italy and the looking-at-another-window window and the river of cardboard and Raj and my brother's careful voice and the four hundred and eleven thousand credits and the whole grey legible size-too-small shape of me —

and there was a smell.

That was the first thing on the other side. Before sight, before the floor, before the noise. A smell, thick and immediate and *wrong*, frying oil and wet stone and underneath it something green and sweet that I had no name for and would not have a name for, for a long time, and over all of it a taste in the air like the air had been switched on, like ozone, like the inside of a storm.

I was lying on something hard and damp.

I was, I would learn, about eighteen hundred years and an unguessable distance from the good chair.

But I didn't know that yet. All I knew, lying in the dark with that impossible smell coming down on me, two hours of sleep and a wish and a reset behind me, was that I had asked for a world with clear rules where you could see the points going up.

I was about to find out the rules.
