# Protagonist — Milo

Milo is the only person on [Arrhenos](../../worlds/arrhenos/index.md) who has never seen a [torc](../../worlds/arrhenos/society.md) before, and the only one running his life off a heads-up display nobody else can see. Everything that makes him a problem here, and a story, comes from those two facts.

## Earth, 2025

Establish the old life before the [event](outline.md) that ends it — and **show it**, don't catalogue it. The brief is a man for whom the real world is illegible and unrewarding, who feels most competent inside games because games, unlike his life, have rules, feedback, and a sense that effort visibly pays off.

- **A life of soft failure.** Not tragic, not dramatic — just flat and a size too small. A job that asks little and returns less (an IT desk, a late-shift something, a gig that runs on autopilot). Few real ties. Order and mastery outsourced to a screen.
- **The edge (locked): systems and pattern literacy.** The one thing Milo is genuinely, unusually good at is *reading systems* — flows, routes, timings, the hidden order under apparent chaos. A lifetime of games has trained it; his work and his life have wasted it. It is invisible and worthless on Earth. On Arrhenos it becomes his livelihood (the [board job](outline.md)) and the reason he's [useful to the runners](cast.md) — and the [System](system.md) is, in a sense, this faculty turned all the way up and given a HUD.
- **The competence gap.** Reading systems, he is decisive and fluent; everywhere else — small talk, status, the social dance — he is hesitant and a half-step behind. He knows the difference and it galls him. The romance is where the gap finally has to close.
- **The wish — plant it explicitly.** Somewhere early, Milo articulates the desire the world will grant with horrible literalness: for clear rules, for a place where you can *see* the points going up, for a reset button on a life he can't seem to start. The setting answers the wish and makes him regret the wording. Pay it off thematically at the [climax](outline.md).

His Earth particulars (exact job, the games he plays, the shape of his loneliness) are open — pick details that will **pay off later** (see [craft.md](craft.md) on planting). His old-world phone and whatever he carried become real, dangerous objects here — see [the phone thread](cast.md).

## The crossing

The **method is the writer's choice** and should stay unexplained — a freak quantum accident, a portal, a crash, an abduction — but **not random**. Seed a faint *wrongness* at the threshold: the sense that he didn't merely fall but was *pulled*, *selected* — a glitch he half-noticed, a pattern that shouldn't have been there, the feeling of being reached for. Don't explain it; this is the series' founding mystery (see [outline.md](outline.md) and the [bible](bible.md)). What matters in the moment is the **shift**: a hard sensory cut from grey Earth ordinariness to the wet, frying-oil, over-lit overwhelm of [the Shambles](../../worlds/arrhenos/locations/brovdingonai/shambles.md). The System boots against that overload; the gamification is born at the instant of maximum incomprehension. See [system.md](system.md).

## In Arrhenos

- **The bare neck.** He arrives without a torc. On a world where the torc is identity, wallet, medical record, and phone in one band of metal, a bare neck is instantly legible as *off-worlder, and a destitute or fugitive one*. His first survival instinct — pulling up a hood to hide the neck — gives the System its joke and gives him his **class** (see below). A back-alley clinic eventually fits him a **placeholder torc**; a real, fully provisioned one is a later, costlier acquisition.
- **The cover.** Under questioning he reaches for the only story that explains a man with no records, no torc, no country, and a strange way of speaking: that he is a robbed [**Drift**](../../powers/drift-communities/overview.md) off-worlder. The cover is good because the Drift are genuinely stateless, genuinely various, and genuinely poor at the bottom — a man from a [Refugee Haven](../../powers/drift-communities/overview.md) could plausibly have nothing and know nothing. It is also a cover he has to *research while wearing it*, frantically reading up on the Drift, on the lost ~1,800 years, and on basic Arrhene life to stop the story falling apart mid-sentence.
- **The language.** Local tech recognises and translates his speech through a laggy [interpreter routine](../../worlds/arrhenos/culture.md); he experiences this, naturally, as a clunky in-game translation subroutine that he can *level up*. He genuinely improves — recognising words and phrases before the interpreter catches them — which both the System and the reader can track. (Canon hook: [Trade Tongue carries gender and Arrhene languages do not](../../worlds/arrhenos/women-and-gender.md), so a real Arrhene has a tell when speaking it. Milo, posing as a Drifter, has *different* tells — his own century's grammar leaking through — a quiet ongoing risk for whoever is paying attention.)

## Arc

From **playing the world** to **living in it**. Mechanically: from no torc and no money to legitimacy, from solo-rogue improvisation toward belonging. Internally: from treating the System as the truth and people as numbers, toward holding the System at arm's length and letting at least one person become more than a stat. The romance (see [cast.md](cast.md)) is the lever. The open question the book keeps live: **is the System a gift, a crutch, or a cage** — and what is lost, as well as gained, when he finally trusts the world enough to close it.

## Voice

Modern, self-deprecating, fluent in gamer and internet idiom — a register that is **eighteen centuries archaic** to everyone around him and which he mostly keeps inside his own head. Interior narration is wry and quick; spoken Arrhene (via the interpreter, improving over time) is halting and careful. The gap between his confident inner monologue and his stumbling outward performance is a recurring source of both comedy and loneliness. He tags everything reflexively — naming enemies, rarity-coding objects, reading rooms as encounters — and the writing should let that habit slowly thin as real life gets its hooks into him.
