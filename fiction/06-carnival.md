# Six — Carnival

*Thall 1/6 to Thall 2/2*

**Thall 1/6. The day before.**

**Thall 1/7. Day one.**

It started at the fourth hour, which is an hour before dawn, and by the tenth hour it had gone wrong in every way it was available to go wrong.

The queue reached forty and stayed there. Not because they were slow — because Bram was cooking eleven things to order on one and a half griddles, and every fourth customer wanted the one that took nine minutes, and the nine-minute one blocked the griddle, and the whole line stopped and breathed and lost its temper. They ran out of the second-best thing by the eleventh hour and had two hundred kilos of the fourth-best thing that nobody ordered because it was ninth on a list of eleven and nobody reads past six.

Wen dropped a tray. Bram burned his forearm on the flue. The man on the next pitch, who was selling a single fried pastry and nothing else and had a queue of eleven that moved constantly, took about four thousand quid off them by lunch.

And you found out what you were for.

Because it turns out that a queue is a queue. It turns out that forty angry people who cannot get a thing they want are exactly, precisely, structurally identical to a P1 with forty users on it, and you had done that four hundred times, and you knew the whole shape of it in your hands.

You went out from behind the counter and worked the line.

You had about ninety words by then and you needed about nine. You went down that queue and you told every man in it, in appalling grammar, three things: what it would be, how long, and what to have instead if he was in a hurry. You pulled the two nine-minute orders forward and cooked them together. You put a hand up and stopped the line for ten seconds when the griddle cleared so it went out clean. You told a man he had eleven minutes and got it to him in nine and watched his whole face change, because the oldest trick in the trade is to say twelve and deliver in nine and it has worked on every human being who has ever lived.

By the fifteenth hour the queue was still forty and nobody in it was angry.

Bram looked up once, in the middle of it, with sweat running off his jaw and a burn coming up on his arm, and saw you halfway down the line with your hands out doing the thing, and did not say anything, and went back to the griddle.

They took nine hundred and forty quid and spent eleven hundred.

**Thall 1/8. Day two.**

You did the numbers between the twentieth hour and the second, in a hired storeroom, sitting on a crate, with your nose bleeding into a rag and eleven columns standing in the air, and you were finished by half past one and did not sleep at all.

Then you went to Sey's, because he had said the second day, and Sey put a Landwick client's four-year run in front of you and you did six hours on it with a hot wire behind your left eye, and he watched you for a while and said nothing.

"Three items. Not eleven. The fold, the pie, and the tea. Price the pie up forty per cent —"

`— *forty* —`

"— because it's the best thing on this plateau and you are charging market rate for a market item and it is not a market item. And bin the other eight."

He said — the layer got most of it, and what it did not get you understood from his face — that you did not know what any of these things were. That the eight you wanted binned were four years of work each. That the ninth one, the one nobody had ordered, was the only surviving reconstruction of a thing that eleven hundred years ago had been eaten by half a continent and that he had spent two years on the pastry alone. That he had not taken this pitch to sell three things. That the man next door selling one fried pastry was selling one fried pastry and had been doing it for thirty years and would do it for thirty more and was that what you wanted.

That you had been on this planet for five days.

You let all of it land. You did not defend any of it. And then, when he had run out, you said the only thing you had:

"You're going to be shut by Vorago. You know that, don't you. Oriol knows it. Your landlord knows it — that's why he likes you, he likes you because you're the most interesting tenant he's ever had and he's been carrying you for six years and one day he's going to sell the building. Half a second. He read it.

**Thall 1/9. Day three.**

Three items. One griddle. A price on the pie that made Bram physically wince every time he said it and that nobody — not one man in six days — ever questioned, because a man who is charging a lot for something is understood to have a reason.

The queue moved. The queue moved so well that people joined it *because* it moved, which is a thing you had known abstractly about queues and had never watched happen in front of you.

Thirty-one hundred quid.

And the tea.

He served it in small cups, hot, with milk, at eleven quid, on a card he had written himself in three languages including one nobody had spoken for a millennium, and the first day he sold forty and about a third of them were handed back after one mouthful with an expression of profound betrayal.

But a man of about sixty took his and went quiet and stood at the end of the counter and drank the whole thing looking at nothing, and came back at the sixteenth hour, and came back again the next morning, and on the last day brought four people.

And at the fourteenth hour, Elio Webb turned up with his entire household.

`— it is milk.`

Corin took his cup and stood at the end and drank it slowly and did not say anything about it at all, and after a while he came round to where you were stacking and said:

`— i looked up the memory havens.`

`— there are about forty that would call themselves that. i went through the — [ ] — the registry of them, the public one, and there is not one called Ninth Hour.`

And then he said, in exactly the same flat interested tone:

`— which is not strange, obviously. the register is four hundred years out of date and half of them do not file and my ecology master says a haven can rename itself in a — [ ] — in an afternoon and most of them have. i just thought i would tell you it is not in there, in case you ever have to give it to somebody official, because they will look, and then they will look at you.`

He drank the rest of his tea.

`— it is quite nice, actually. i do not know why Nikias said that.`

And he went off to find his fathers, and you stood in a storeroom doorway with a crate in your arms and understood, in a completely new way, that this boy was going to be the whole of your problem.

They shut at the twentieth hour and sat on the back step of the storeroom with the door open and the noise of the market coming up the alley, and neither of them said anything for about eleven minutes.

The fountains were doing something up on the higher levels. You could not see them from the step but the whole alley was going pale blue and then copper and then pale blue again, on a slow cycle, off the underside of the walkway.

Bram had his sleeves pushed up and a burn dressing on his forearm and flour to the elbow and he had not sat down since the fourth hour and he looked, honestly, terrible.

`— we are going to make it,` he said.

"You're going to make about eight thousand clear."

`— do not say a number.`

"Eight thousand and something. It might be nine after tomorrow."

He put his head back against the doorframe and laughed at the ceiling for a while.

Then he said: `— i have never made money. i want you to understand that. six years. i have never once made money, not a — [ ] — not once, not a single quarter. Oriol has been telling me for six years and he is right and i cannot hear it from him and i heard it from you at two in the morning on the second day and i have been trying to work out why since.`

`— i know why. it is because you were not telling me i was wrong. you were telling me it was worth saving.`

Here is what you did.

You had about four seconds, and you knew exactly what they were — you had been thirty-one years learning to recognise those four seconds, in a country where you had to be sure, and you were sure — and you sat on that step and you did not move, and you looked at the wall opposite, and you let them go past.

Not because you did not want to. Understand that. Not for a single instant because you did not want to.

Because you had spent that afternoon watching a man decide he had misremembered a jar. Because your name was Milo Hale and you were from Warrington and the year was 2025 and everything, *everything*, every word of every conversation you had had on that step for two hours about pies and about your mother's kitchen, had been sitting inside a lie that a fifty-five-year-old clerk had built for you out of good manners on your first afternoon. Because sixty-four years of litigation started with eleven thousand quid that a man did not mention. `— fourth hour,` he said, in the doorway. `— it is the parade tomorrow. it will be — [ ] — it will be biblical.`

He went in. You sat on the step for another twenty minutes on your own with the light going blue and copper on the underside of the walkway.

**Thall 2/2. Day six. The parade.**

Everything you have read about that night is roughly true and undersells it.

The whole city's fountains came under one programme at the eighteenth hour — two hundred and something of them, across forty square kilometres, running one enormous slow composition together, so that a thing would start down in the industrial district and arrive at the Shambles ninety seconds later like weather. The Shambles Fountain Committee, which is eleven men with a long-standing grievance against municipal coordination, ran its own programme on top of it, deliberately out of phase, as it has every year since 3806.

The parade came through the market on the second level at the nineteenth hour: floating platforms at walking pace with costumed men on them lit from inside their own clothes, and the crowd went from dense to something you had genuinely never experienced. Not London-dense. Not festival-dense. A three-dimensional crowd, on six levels, in a building that is also a street, with people on every stair and every rail and every terrace and a great many people on things that were not designed to have people on them.

You took eleven hundred quid in the first hour.

You did fourteen uses in that hour: float, stock, trays, a spill, a cup a man dropped that you caught out of the air in a way that you should not have been able to and covered by laughing.

Your nose had been going every forty minutes since the sixteenth hour. You had a rag in your left hand permanently. The wire behind your eyes had stopped being a wire and become a kind of white pressure that made the edges of things very slightly wrong, and twice you had put a hand on the counter because the floor had moved.

Bram asked you three times if you were all right.

You said yes three times.

⁂

At the twentieth hour a group of about eleven young men got up onto the roof of a temporary pitch on the third level to see the platforms come round the bend.

The pitch was three days old. It was a hired frame with a canvas top and a bolted rail, rated for equipment, and the men on it were probably twenty-two and were not doing anything malicious, and about forty other people had done the same thing on other frames that night and got away with it.

The frame went at the north-west corner. You heard it before you saw it — a sound like a stack of chairs — and everybody on the second level looked up.

The men fell inward, which saved all of them. What went outward, over the edge of the third level, was the pitch's cooking rig: a hired griddle unit on a stand with a live fuel cell under it, about seventy kilos of hot steel and gas, turning over once in the air, coming down into a crowd standing eleven deep on the second-level walkway.

And you were four metres away and you could see, exactly, in the fountain light, the two people it was going to land on, and one of them was about seven years old and was on his father's shoulders looking the other way at the platforms.

⁂

You had a rag in your hand and a queue in front of you and no time to think about anything at all, which is, you have decided since, the only reason you did it, because there is no version of that half-second in which a thinking man steps into the fall line of seventy kilos of burning metal.

You got both hands under it.

You have a permanent white scar across the inside of both forearms and the heel of the right hand, and you took the whole weight for a fraction of a second — not long enough to break anything, long enough to go down onto one knee and feel your shoulder do something it has never entirely forgiven — and in that fraction, with your palms on a hot griddle plate and a crowd screaming behind you, you formed the cleanest, hardest, most completely articulated want of your life for the second time in five days.

**Not here. Not on them. Gone.**

And it went.

⁂

There was no flash and no sound and nothing to see, and seventy kilos of falling ironmongery did not arrive.

The crowd did what crowds do, which is scatter first and reason afterwards. Somewhere behind you a man was shouting. The father with the boy on his shoulders had gone eleven feet sideways and was standing with both hands up on his son's legs looking at a piece of walkway with nothing on it.

And you were on your knees on the second level with your palms open and the skin coming off them, and the thing behind your eyes did not go off like a flashbulb this time.

This time it went off like the road.

⁂

The pressure came front to back, exactly the same, the whole shove of it, a wall made of the wall. Your ears went. Both nostrils this time, and your left ear, which you did not know about until afterwards. The market went silent in the way that has no medium in it, for about a second and a half, and you had time for exactly one thought and the thought was not profound.

It was: *slot four is on fire.*

Then the ground came up.

⁂

You do not remember being turned over. You remember, in pieces, the following:

The fountain light on the underside of the walkway going blue and then copper.

A great deal of shouting, none of which you could understand, and the specific relief of not being expected to.

Somebody's hands under your head, and a burn dressing on a forearm about four inches from your face, and flour, and a man saying your name in the plateau tongue with the stress in the right place — which he had been doing for six days and which you had never once told him was the only two syllables on that planet that arrived without a machine in between.

`— Milo. Milo. Milo, look at me —`

And a boy of sixteen crouched on the other side of you, very calm, very white, holding a cup of water he had got from somewhere, who had been standing on the stair above the north-west corner with a clear view of the whole thing and who did not say one word about it to anybody, then or for a long time.

And then a green flash on a shoulder, and a bag going down on the stone, and a light in your left eye, and a frame coming out of a case and unfolding over your chest.
