# Five — Sey & Halloway

*Thall 1/5*

The stairs to Level 9 went up through the northwest corner of the market in four flights with a turn between each, and by the third one you had stopped noticing them, which was the first genuinely strange thing about that morning.

You had slept for two hours and forty minutes. You had a head like a bad Sunday and a crust of dried blood in your left nostril that you had scrubbed at in a communal washroom for eleven minutes and not entirely removed. You had used a thing forty times in ninety minutes that took something out of you every time, and you were paying for it, and you knew you were paying for it, and you went up four flights of stairs at speed without breathing hard, because on Tuesday morning a man had spent thirty-five minutes taking a decade of small damage out of your body and you had not yet had time to understand what that meant.

Thirty-one years old and you had never once, in your adult life, gone up stairs without a small background negotiation. You did not know what to do with the surplus.

The garrets were the top three levels of the northwest side, where the buildings had gaps between them and the light came down. It was studios up there — you could see it before you could read a word of it, because a room where somebody makes things looks the same in any century: bad chairs, good light, everything on the floor. Doors propped open with weights. A man in his fifties standing in a doorway with a cup, watching the light, doing nothing at all, in the way of a person who is in fact working extremely hard.

And in the middle of it, on the ninth level, between a ceramicist and something that was either a print workshop or an extremely untidy laboratory, there was a door with two names on it in a plain heavy script, and a plate below the names that said something short, and the whole thing had been there a very long time.

`— [ ] & Halloway. Reconciliations.`

Your layer took four goes at the first name and never got it, because it was set for the plateau tongue and the door was in Standard, and that was the first hint you had of a thing that was about to make your week considerably worse.

You knocked at four minutes to the tenth hour.

⁂

The office was one long room under a sloped roof with three skylights in it, and it was the least futuristic space you had entered on that planet.

There were shelves. There were physical shelves, floor to ceiling on the long wall, and on them there were boxes — actual boxes, in a grey composite, with handwritten labels — and there were four hundred of them and they were not decorative. There were three desks with worn edges. There was a kettle arrangement in the corner that was clearly the subject of a long-running dispute. There was a window at the far end that had been propped open with a rolled-up something for so long that the rolled-up something had gone yellow.

There was a smell of dust and warm electronics and, faintly, of the ceramicist's kiln through the wall.

Three men. One at each desk.

The one nearest the door was about forty-five, in a good jacket, with a small blue enamel flash pinned at the collar — the first insignia you had seen worn deliberately, on a man who wanted you to see it. He looked up when you came in and looked back down again immediately, which is a thing people do when they have already decided something about you.

The one at the far end was sixty-odd, very still, working with his face about thirty centimetres from a projection, and he did not look up at all and did not look up for the next four hours.

And the one in the middle put down what he was holding and said, in Standard, which your layer took a second and a half to find:

`— you are the one with the year.`

⁂

Odran Sey was seventy-one and looked about forty-five and had the specific quality that you have met perhaps twice in your life in senior people, which is that he was not performing anything at all. He did not stand up. He did not do the warm opening. He waved you at a chair opposite him with two fingers and then he did not speak for about eleven seconds while he looked at you, and you understood that the interview had already started and that the first question had been *how does he handle eleven seconds of nothing*, and you sat still and let it happen, because eleven seconds of nothing was the easiest thing anybody had asked of you in three days.

Then he pulled the screening page up between you, rotated so you could read it, and said:

`— show me.`

Not *explain*. Not *tell me about yourself*. You had prepared, on the walk up, four different accounts of who you were, and he did not want any of them, and never asked for one, that day or ever.

So you showed him.

It took you nine minutes. You did it in figures, because figures did not need translating, and you found within about ninety seconds that this was going to work, that you could point at a column and say a number and he would follow it, and that the whole conversation could be conducted in the one language you and he both spoke fluently. You laid out the periodic drift. You laid out the sign flips against the turn of the year. You put your finger on the step in the middle of the run and said, "There. That's not an error, that's the adjustment," and he said `— yes —` in a flat voice that told you nothing.

And then you said the thing, and he sat forward.

"It's been corrected twice. Somebody restated the old ledgers when the year changed, properly, and it's good work, it's all there. And then a hundred and something years later somebody else found the original books, and they were clean, and they looked untouched, and he did the restatement again. On top. He wasn't sloppy. He was *careful*. That's why nobody's found it — you're all looking for a mistake and it isn't a mistake, it's two people doing an excellent job at each other."

Half a second. He read it.

He was quiet for a while. Then:

`— a hundred and eighteen days. i have had four board men look at that page. one of them charged me.`

⁂

Then he asked the hard question, and you had known it was coming since the labour hall and had not thought of an answer.

`— how did you know to look at the calendar.`

Because that was the thing. The arithmetic was arithmetic; anyone could follow it once you pointed. What he wanted to know — what a good employer always wants to know, in any century — was not whether you could do it but why your hand had gone to that drawer first, out of forty drawers, in eleven minutes.

And the honest answer was: *because I come from a civilisation that spent seventy years bodging together machines that disagreed about midnight. Because my whole species once nearly frightened itself into a recession over a two-digit year. Because I have personally lost a fortnight of my life to a warehouse in Rotterdam whose stock count went wrong twice a year, in March and in October, for reasons that took three men and a consultant to establish. Because where I am from, time was the single most reliably broken thing there was, and every one of us grew up with the reflex, and none of us knew we had it.*

You could not say any of that.

So you said the other thing, and the other thing was true, and this is the part you have never entirely forgiven yourself for, because it was so *easy*:

"Because on a haven, every record you hold came from somewhere else. You've got a system's books and a station's books and a company's books and a family's books and every one of them was kept somewhere with its own year, and half of them were restated by somebody who didn't say so. You don't look for fraud first. You look at the clock first. It's always the clock."

He read it.

`— yes,` he said. `— that is exactly right and nobody here believes it, because our clocks have agreed with each other for eleven hundred years and it makes us stupid.`

And he hired you, in the next sentence, without any ceremony whatsoever.

⁂

Then he told you the terms and they were bad.

`— eleven quid the hour.`

"The posting said fourteen."

`— the posting said fourteen for a man with a — [ ] — with standing. you have a case number. i looked. i looked before you came up the stairs, because i am not a fool and neither are you. eleven.`

You said, "Twelve."

`— eleven.`

You said, "Twelve, and I'll do the boxes."

He looked at the four hundred boxes on the long wall and then back at you, and something happened around his eyes that was not quite a smile.

`— twelve. and you will regret the boxes.`

The rest of it he laid out flatly and without apology, and you wrote none of it down because you no longer needed to.

You were unlicensed, which meant you had no standing at all: you could not sign anything, could not be named on anything, could not speak to a client, could not take work outside the office, and could not be in a room with a client's principal without a board man present. Every piece of work you produced would be signed off by a licensed reconciler, whose name would go on it, and whose name would be on it in ten years and in a hundred, in the archive, permanently, and yours would not be anywhere.

`— you will find that unpleasant,` Sey said, `— and you will find it more unpleasant in three months than you do now, and there is nothing i can do about it, because it is not my rule.`

You said, "How do I get standing?"

And he told you, and this is the number that has run underneath everything since.

`— you sit a board. the boards are open to anybody at any age, and if you are what i think you are you would pass one inside two years. but you cannot sit a board, because a board is sat by a person, and you are a case file. so first you go to the registry and you become a person, and for that you need someone to attest to you, and nobody will attest to a man they met in the market.`

`— a firm can attest. a firm's attestation is worth something because a firm has a name to lose.`

`— be here in a year and i will write it.`

⁂

A year. Three hundred and sixty days, at twelve quid the hour.

You did the arithmetic on the stairs at the end of the day and then again in the pod that night and it came out the same both times and it has come out the same every time since, and you would like it recorded that you did not lie to yourself about it for one single day.

Twelve an hour. Call it eight hours, call it six days, and Sey did work six days. Five hundred and seventy-six the week. The racks at two hundred and ninety, food at two hundred, the tram pass, the layer subscription you had not known you were paying for until it took the money on Thursday.

You could live. That was all. You could exactly, precisely live, and put aside perhaps two hundred a week if you were careful and did not go out, and in a hundred and seventy-nine days that would be about five thousand quid.

And Sorian wanted twelve.

⁂

Kavi Ness was forty-four and had passed his board at thirty-one and wore the blue flash every day of his life, and he shook your hand and was perfectly pleasant to you and hated you within about two hours, and he was entirely right to and you never held it against him.

His name was going on your work. Sey said so in front of both of you, without softening it, in a way that made clear the arrangement was routine and that Ness had done it before with other unlicensed men.

What Sey did not say, and what everybody in the room understood by the second hour, was that this time was going to be different, because the other unlicensed men had been juniors and you were not a junior. You were a man who had cracked a hundred-and-eighteen-day page in eleven minutes and who was going to do it again, repeatedly, and Kavi Ness was going to sign all of it, and every client who was impressed was going to be impressed with him.

It is very easy to write him as a small man. He was not. Here is what he was: a competent, conscientious, unremarkable professional in his forties who had worked extremely hard for a qualification, who was genuinely and reliably good at the tedious eighty per cent of the job, who had never in twenty years produced anything anybody would call brilliant, and who had made his peace with that, and who now had to sit two metres from somebody who did brilliant before lunch without appearing to try, and put his own name on it, and be thanked for it by clients.

He was decent about it for eleven days. You have thought about that a good deal since — that he managed eleven days — and about the fact that on the twelfth day the thing he said was not *you are not qualified*, which would have been fair, but `— you do not even know what any of it means —`, which was fairer, and which was true, and which cost you a great deal more.

Hallam Ost, at the far desk, was sixty-one, had been in that room for thirty-one years, handled four clients and no others, spoke perhaps forty words a day, and turned out — three weeks later, when you were in trouble — to be the only one of the three who would put himself between you and anything.

⁂

The other thing you learned that morning, and it took the legs out from under you, was that you had spent three days learning the wrong language.

The street spoke the plateau tongue. That was the thing the market ran on, the thing Bram had said his name in, the thing you had eleven words of and now forty-seven. It was local; it went about six hundred kilometres and then became a different local thing.

The records were in Campottonì Standard. All of them. Every ledger, every instrument, every filing, every professional communication on the planet for four hundred years, in the capital's language, which every educated man on Arrhenos also spoke and which was the reason the whole place could function.

`— you will need both,` said Sey, without looking up. `— everybody needs both. we start Standard at six and finish at fourteen and by twenty we have forgotten which one we are in. you are learning them from — [ ] — from nothing, at thirty-one, which is a stupid thing to have to do and it cannot be helped.`

Two languages. Fifteen thousand words each, and forty-seven of one and none of the other, and a translation layer that dropped one word in six and would be in your ear for a year.

You went and looked out of the propped-open window for a minute and then you came back and sat down and started, because that is what you do.

⁂

The work was the Ossen dissolution and it had been open for sixty-four years.

Here is what an Arrhene household is, in law, and it took you a fortnight to properly understand and about four minutes to grasp well enough to work: it is a registered economic and domestic unit, formed by contract, of any size, renewed at intervals of some years, which may widen, consolidate, dissolve, and reform, and which people move in and out of across lives that run past two hundred. It is not a marriage in the sense you were raised on. It is closer to a partnership with love in it, or sometimes without.

Which means that a society of two billion men generates, continuously, an absolutely colossal quantity of the most tangled bookkeeping in settled space.

The Ossen household formed in 3771 with three men, widened to four in 3779 and to five in 3784, and dissolved in 3798, and the dissolution accounts had never closed, because two of the five died within a year of it and one went off-world and never answered, and the remaining two produced twenty-seven years of accounts and eleven amendments and then themselves died, and their raised sons' households had been contesting the residue ever since.

Four separate reconcilers had been at it over six decades. All four had done it properly. All four had done it in pieces, because there was no other way — two hundred and eleven entries across twenty-seven years with eleven amendments layered over them, and a human being can hold about seven numbers, and so you take a year at a time, and you balance the year, and you move on, and every year balances.

Every single year balanced. That is why nobody had found it.

You held the whole thing.

⁂

You want to know what that was actually like, because I have described it as a superpower and it was not, quite.

It was like this. You sat at a desk with a projection open, and you looked at a column, and you thought *keep it*, and it stayed — exact, ordered, unwavering, at the left of your vision, not fading at the fourth second the way numbers do. And then another. And another. And by the middle of the afternoon you had eleven columns standing in the air in your own alphabet in perfect condition and you could look from any one of them to any other and back, indefinitely, without loss.

And you had to keep them all in a room that also contained a headache like a hot wire behind your left eye, and a nosebleed at about the fourth hour which you dealt with in the corridor with a handkerchief you had bought on the way in for exactly this reason, and by six in the evening a tremor in both hands that you had to hide by keeping them flat on the desk.

It is not free. It has never once been free. What it is, is *possible*, and nothing else on that planet could do it, and that is a different thing from easy.

And at about the fifth hour, with eleven years standing side by side in the air that nobody in six decades had ever had in one place at one time, you saw it.

⁂

There was a line in the household's quarterly outgoings called something the layer gave as `maintenance, external`, and it ran from 3779 to 3798, and it was small, and it was irregular, and in any single year it was completely unremarkable — forty quid here, sixty there, once four hundred, twice nothing at all for three quarters together.

Across nineteen years it was eleven thousand four hundred quid.

And it was not maintenance of anything, because you had the property schedule up beside it and there was nothing external to maintain, and there never had been.

And it stopped in the second quarter of 3798, which was the quarter Teiran Ossen died, and it never resumed, and that — that right there — was the actual reason the dissolution accounts had never balanced against the estate. Not fraud. Not error. A line that had a man attached to it, and when the man stopped, the line stopped, and nobody living had ever known why.

You followed it for another hour and you found the other end of it, because the payments carried a routing that four reconcilers had read as a bank code and which was not a bank code, it was a place, and the place was a small town about two hundred kilometres north-east with a ceramics museum in it that Elio Webb liked.

And in that town, from 3779, there had been a household of two men, and one of those men had been in the Ossen household until 3779, and had left it, badly, in the year it widened.

Nineteen years. Every quarter. Quietly, out of the joint account, in amounts small enough that nobody ever asked, to a man he had hurt and had not stopped being responsible for.

And he never told them.

⁂

You sat in a room under a sloped roof with the ceramicist's kiln ticking through the wall and you understood the whole of it in about four seconds, and then you sat with it for a good deal longer than that.

Because there was nothing wrong with any of it. That was the thing that got you. In this place, on this planet, in this culture — a man who leaves a household and goes on quietly paying is not a scandal, he is not even *interesting*. He could have said it at dinner. He could have put it in the contract. Households widen and dissolve and reform, love is not rivalrous, and every man in that house would have shrugged and passed the dish.

The only wrong thing in the entire nineteen years was that he did not say.

And because he did not say, it went into the ledger as an unexplained line, and the unexplained line went into a dissolution, and the dissolution went into an estate, and the estate went into sixty-four years of litigation between four households of men who had never met Teiran Ossen and who had spent, at a rough estimate that you did not make that day but made later and wished you hadn't, something in the region of two hundred thousand quid arguing about eleven thousand.

A kindness, kept quiet, that turned into a lawsuit and outlived everybody in it.

You wrote it up. It took you forty minutes and you did it in figures with the layer limping along behind, and you gave it to Kavi Ness, and Kavi Ness read it twice and went slightly grey and took it to Sey, and Sey read it once and said one word, and that was the Ossen dissolution finished after sixty-four years.

Ness's name went on it. That is in the archive now, and it will be in the archive in three hundred years.

You did not mind, that day. You minded later.

What you did that day, walking down four flights of stairs at half past six with a hot wire behind your eye, was understand — completely, with total clarity, the way you understand a thing that has just been demonstrated to you in a controlled environment over eight hours — that in this country the concealment is the injury. Not the act. Never the act. The keeping quiet.

And then you went out into the market and carried on keeping quiet, because there was nothing else you could do, and because you had already started, and because the alternative was a room with no floor in it.

⁂

The other thing happened at about the sixth hour and you have never told anybody about it.

You were resting your eyes — genuinely resting them, with eleven columns dismissed and nothing in the air — and Sey had put a second file on your desk with a note that said `— when you are done, this one has been irritating me since Nivir —` and you had opened it out of nothing but curiosity, and it was a completely unrelated client: a small agricultural lending house, a run of corrections to a set of security valuations, 3844.

And you looked at the shape of the corrections and something in you went cold, because you had seen that shape three hours earlier in the Ossen file, in a different decade, for a different client, in a matter that had nothing whatsoever to do with agricultural lending.

Not the numbers. The *hand*. The way the correction was structured: always at the quarter, always as a restatement rather than an adjustment, always with the original left legible underneath, always — and this was the thing — always rounding in the same direction on the same kind of entry, which is a habit, and habits belong to people.

You held both and put them side by side, which nobody would ever do, because there is no reason on earth to put a household dissolution from 3798 next to a lending house's valuations from 3844.

And then, because you are what you are, you got up and went to the long wall and pulled a box at random from a decade in between and opened a third file, and it was there too.

You closed the file. You put the box back on the wall in the right place, because you were, by then, the sort of man who put boxes back in the right place. You said nothing to Sey, nothing to Ness, nothing to anybody, and you thought about it on the stairs and on the walkway and at the fountain and in the pod at midnight, and you have thought about it since roughly every day.

⁂

You went to the Fountain Steps at the end of the day because it was the only place in that city you could reliably find.

Not the shuttered counter — you could find that too, now, but the counter had been shut for three days and you had stopped being able to look at it. You went to the plaza. The wide shallow staircase in three flights, the great slow upward column of water, the purple-copper trees, the terrace where a man had knelt down in front of you and put his hand out and waited.

It was the copper hour. The fountains were coming on across the lower districts. There were maybe two hundred men on those steps at the end of a working day, sitting in twos and threes, eating, talking, doing nothing, and the light was coming in sideways off the west face of the market and turning all of it the colour of a struck match.

He was on the third flight, about a third of the way along, with a canvas bag beside him and his elbows on his knees, eating something out of a twist of paper, and he was not looking for anybody. He had the posture of a man who has looked for somebody three times and has stopped looking and is just sitting where he would have looked.

You got about six metres before he saw you.

He came up off that step so fast he put his foot in the bag.

⁂

It was not a graceful reunion, and you would not swap it.

He said about eleven things in the first eight seconds and your layer got approximately two of them, and one of the ones it got was `— i went to the steps —` and one was `— the old — [ ] — told me a man came —` and the rest was noise with a shape, and he was grinning, and he had flour on the side of his hand and a burn across two knuckles that was three days old.

Then he stopped, visibly, and did a thing with his hand at his own throat, and something in the air between you changed, and the text came through *clean*.

`— is that better? mine is a good one. i put it on the — it will carry both of us if you let it.`

It was better. It was so much better that you stood there for a second reading it twice.

His layer was not the public one. Mern would have known what it was; you did not, then. What it meant in practice, for the next four months, was that the two of you talked through a system where he got you nearly whole and you got him in pieces, and so he did most of the talking, and you did most of the guessing, and that is, more or less permanently, the shape of the two of you.

`— i have been shut. i am sorry. i should have — i did not think, i just went, i had the kitchen for three days and if you do not take the kitchen when it is free you do not get it again until — anyway i have been in a hired kitchen on Level 2 for three days and i have not been outside and i am doing a thing for Carnival and it is going to be either the best thing i have ever done or i will lose everything i have, and Oriol says i say that every year, which is true, but this time —`

He caught himself. He looked at you properly. The grin came off.

`— are you all right? where did you sleep?`

⁂

You told him. Not all of it. But you told him about the walk-in with the green cross and about the man behind the counter who had found the puck, and about the bunkhouse near the deep line, and about the boy who had bought you a coat, and about the office at the top of four flights with four hundred boxes on the wall.

`— you have a job.`

"Since this morning."

`— you have been here four days.`

"Three."

He sat back down on the step and looked at you for a moment, and then he laughed, once, in a slightly winded way, and said something the layer gave as `— i thought i was going to have to —` and did not finish, and never did finish, and you have wondered about the end of that sentence for a long time.

⁂

Then he asked about food, and everything opened up.

It started as small talk and it was not small talk for eleven seconds. He had a bag of things beside him and he had, apparently, been sitting on those steps for an hour and a half with the specific intention of eating his way through a problem, and once he had established that you would sit down he simply began, at speed, in the middle:

`— what is a rasher.`

You said, "Sorry?"

`— rasher. i have it in three sources. it is a — [ ] — it is a quantity of something or it is a cut of something, and one source uses it as a number, and the number does not work as a number, and i have been at it for two years. is it a cut. tell me it is a cut.`

"It's a cut," you said. "It's a slice. Off the side of a pig. You fry it."

He went completely still.

Here is what you had not understood until that moment, and it changed the whole of your first year: Bram Drummond had spent six years doing historical reconstructions of pre-Scattering Earth cooking, from fragments, in a language nobody spoke, for ingredients that did not exist, working off an archive corpus that was three-quarters missing and a quarter mistranscribed, in a provincial city where about eleven people cared, and it had made him, by the standards of that world, a genuine minor expert on a subject nobody could check him on.

And he had just been handed a primary source. A living one. From a memory haven, obviously; from the actual archive people, the ones who kept it; a man who had *grown up* with it.

He got a display out. He was fumbling. He was so excited he could not work his own equipment.

`— sit down. sit down. can you — how long have you got.`

⁂

You were on those steps for two hours and forty minutes and it was the happiest you had been since a road ended outside Warrington.

Because for two hours and forty minutes, you did not lie about one single thing.

You told him a rasher was a slice, and that it came from the side and not the leg, and that the difference mattered enormously and you could not explain why. You told him that the thing his source called a breakfast item and also a supper item was both, genuinely, and that this was not a contradiction in his sources, it was just a thing people did. You told him about the difference between something fried and something griddled and about why the fat mattered. You told him about a pie and about why it had a lid, and about gravy, and about the specific sequence of a roast dinner and what went in the oven when, and he wrote all of it down and asked eleven follow-up questions and three of them were better than anything you had ever thought about food in your life.

You told him — and this took twenty minutes and he would not let you stop — about a Sunday at your mother's house: about the shape of the day, and about the fact that the meal is at three in the afternoon which is a stupid time for a meal, and about the smell in the hall when you came in, and about the fact that nobody in your entire country can agree about the correct treatment of a potato, and about the specific noise of a kitchen with two people in it who have done this four hundred times.

`— you are describing a — [ ] — a ceremony.`

"It's not. It's just Sunday."

`— it is a ceremony. you do not know it because you were in it.`

And you sat on a step in the copper light and thought: *he is entirely right, and I will never see one again,* and then you drank some water and asked him what else he had.

⁂

He had tea.

He produced it about two hours in, out of a flask, with the manner of a man producing the thing he has been building up to, and he poured it into two small cups and handed you one and watched your face with the total, unbearable attention of a professional showing you his work.

It was not tea. It was close. It was a hot infusion of a leaf that was not that leaf, made too hot and left far too long, and it was thin and it was tannic and it was extraordinarily bitter and there was something floral in it that had no business being there.

And it was, unmistakably, an eleven-hundred-year-old reconstruction of a cup of tea, and somebody had worked very hard on it.

You drank a mouthful.

`— well?`

You said, "You need to put milk in it."

He said `— what —`

"Milk. About that much. In first or after, people fight about it. Not much. It takes the edge off the tannin and it goes — " and you stopped, because you had absolutely no idea how to describe what milk does to tea to a man who had never seen it happen, and because he had gone perfectly white.

`— there is a — there is a line. there is a line in the — [ ] — in one of the fragments and it says a — [ ] — a white measure, and every reconstruction for four hundred years has read it as a — as a *measure*, as a unit, a white measure, and it makes no sense as a unit and everybody has assumed the transcription is broken and *you are telling me it is milk* —`

"It's milk."

`— it is an *ingredient* —`

"It's milk, Bram."

He put his cup down on the step and put both hands on top of his head and said something with great feeling that the layer declined entirely and rendered as `[ ]`, and then he laughed for about fifteen seconds, and then he said:

`— how do you *know* that.`

And there it was. There it always is.

And you looked at a man sitting on a step in the last of the light with flour on his hand and a three-day burn across his knuckles, who had just handed you two and a half hours of the only unguarded happiness you had had on that planet, and you said:

"We had it. On the haven. It's — everyone has it. It's not special. It's just what you do."

He nodded. He believed you completely. He wrote it down.

You watched yourself do it, from somewhere about a metre behind your own head, and you thought about eleven thousand four hundred quid paid quarterly for nineteen years by a man who never said, and about sixty-four years of litigation, and you filed it, and you smiled at him, and you asked him what else he had in the bag.

⁂

He asked you at the very end, packing up, in a rush, not looking at you, in the manner of a man who has been working up to something for an hour and has decided to get it over with:

`— come and work the counter with me. for Carnival. it is six days, it starts on the seventh, i have the pitch and i have the — i have far too much of everything and i have nobody, because i never have anybody, because i am — Oriol says i am a — [ ] — and he is right. i cannot pay you what a — i can pay you something. it will be terrible. it will be the worst six days of your life, we will start at the fourth hour and finish at the twentieth and you will hate me by the third day, everybody does —`

"Yes," you said.

`— you have a job. i know you have a job, you said, so obviously if you cannot —`

"Bram. Yes."

He stopped, and looked at you, and grinned, and it was the same grin as the one on the walkway three days earlier when you had finally got his name right on the third go.

⁂

You had been working on it all afternoon, in the corridor outside the office, under your breath, between columns, with the layer running the sound back at you eleven times until you had the stress in the right place and the soft consonant soft.

It is two words in the plateau tongue and it is not really *thank you*. It is closer to *that was yours and I have it*, which is a thing you did not learn until months later and which, when you did learn it, made you sit down.

You said it out loud on the steps with your own mouth, in his language, and you got it wrong.

He waited. He did not correct you and he did not laugh, he just waited, with his bag over his shoulder, in the last of the copper light.

You said it again.

And he said it back to you, once, quietly, and picked up his bag, and went off up the steps two at a time into the market to a kitchen he had for one more night, and you stood at the bottom of a staircase where you had once been on your hands and knees screaming, and watched him go.
