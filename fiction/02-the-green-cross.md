# Two — The Green Cross

*Thall 1/3, morning to midday*

The man did not explain, because he could not. What he did instead was better.

He kept hold of your hand for about four seconds after you were on your feet — long enough for you to stop swaying, not so long that it became a thing — and then he let go and took a step back and made a small open gesture with both palms, low and slow, that meant *stay there*. You stayed there. He turned and walked back across the plaza to the little six-stool frontage with the two hanging lamps and leaned over the counter and said something to the man at the next pitch along, who was old, seventy or thereabouts, with white in his beard and forearms like mooring rope, and who was in the middle of arranging something raw and dark red on a tray.

The old man looked past him at you. Then he looked back at your man. Then he said something short, in a tone you had heard from every senior technician you had ever worked with, and which travelled across a thousand years and half a market entirely intact and meant: *again?*

Your man said something back, fast and a bit sheepish, and made the palms gesture again, and the old man rolled his eyes and put down his tray and came out from behind his own pitch with the specific heavy slowness of a person who is going to do the thing and be annoyed about it for the rest of the day. A lad of about seventeen materialised from somewhere — behind the cabinet, up a ladder, you never saw where — and got a set of instructions delivered at speed with a lot of pointing, and nodded four times, and immediately did something to the fryer that made your man come back and correct him.

Then he untied his apron, hung it on a hook, said one more thing to the old man that made the old man wave a hand at him without looking up, and came back over.

You had watched all of it with your heart going like a bird in a box. But you had watched it. Some part of you was already at work, because some part of you was always already at work — the part that listened to a caller for eight seconds and knew whether the problem was the machine or the man — and it had extracted, from ninety seconds of incomprehensible speech, the following: that he had abandoned a business to help you; that this was not the first time he had abandoned that business; that the old man had covered for him before and would again and would complain about it; and that the boy could not be trusted with the fryer.

The market did not stay one place. That was the thing you could not get your head around, and you tried for four hundred metres and failed.

It began as a plaza with weather in it and became, without any door being involved, a wide colonnade with sun coming through in bars; and then the colonnade turned a corner and the sky went away and there was a ceiling of some pale ribbed material with lights let into it, and it was cool, and there was a hum of ventilation; and then eleven paces later the ceiling simply stopped and there was open air and a bridge going over you and a man's washing hanging out of a window four floors up; and then you were in a half-alley with walls on both sides and canvas overhead and rain still coming down on the canvas from a shower you had not known was happening. You had gone from outdoors to indoors and back four times without passing a threshold. There was no edge anywhere. The building and the street were the same object and had been for three hundred years, and everybody around you was moving through it at walking pace without ever once looking up.

And it went *down*. You kept expecting a ground floor and there wasn't one. He took you down a broad flight of stairs at the end of the colonnade and you came out on a level that had, when you looked back up, been the roof of the thing you had been standing on, and there were shops under it, and under those there were more, and somewhere below that there was a vibration coming up through the floor every ninety seconds or so that you eventually understood was a train.

You made yourself work. It was that or scream, and you had already tried screaming.

The prices first. Every stall had a display or a board or a projected panel, and the panels all had, at the right-hand end, a short group of characters in a distinct heavier weight than the rest, and those characters came from a much smaller set than the ones in the words. Nine or ten forms, maybe eleven. Then the collars.

You had spent your first four minutes on this world in a private and total conviction that you had arrived in a slave state, and the conviction had been sitting in your chest since, cold and heavy, colouring everything. It came apart on the corner of a covered arcade, in about eleven seconds, and it came apart because you watched a transaction.

That should have been a relief and it was, for about six seconds, and then it was much worse, because a collar you can be forced into is a horror you can at least name, and a collar that everybody wants is a country, and you were standing in the middle of one with your hands empty.

He looked back at you a lot as you walked. Not nervously — checking, the way you check a bag you are carrying loose. Twice he slowed and let you catch up. Once, when a low sled-thing with crates on it came round a corner too fast, he put his arm across in front of you without breaking stride, and took it away again, and did not comment.

And about halfway, on a stretch of covered walkway with nothing much on either side, he stopped, and turned, and tapped himself twice on the chest with two fingers, and said a word.

You looked at him.

He did it again. Two taps. The word. Then he pointed at you, open-handed, and raised his eyebrows and waited.

You had spent four years learning to say people's names correctly down a telephone line. It was, genuinely, a skill you had; it was one of about three things you were unambiguously good at, and you had built it deliberately, because when a frightened man in a warehouse in Rotterdam hears his own name said properly by the person who is about to help him, something in his shoulders comes down two inches, and you had decided at twenty-seven that this was worth being good at even though no bar chart would ever record it.

You got it wrong — you put the stress on the wrong syllable and hardened a consonant that was meant to be soft — and something happened in his face that was the first genuinely uncomplicated thing you had seen since the road ended, which was that he laughed. The alley he turned into last was the quietest place you had been since you arrived: three metres wide, walls both sides, roofed by somebody's terrace, with a run of shuttered frontages and a stack of empty crates and one door standing open with light coming out of it. Something happened in your chest that you did not have a good name for. It was not hope exactly. It was the specific feeling of finding, in a foreign country, at night, exhausted, a single object that belongs to you: that there had been a gap, an unimaginable gap, a gap you had not yet permitted yourself to measure — and that *something had come across it*. Some tiny stupid piece of the furniture of your world had made the whole journey and got here before you and was waiting, lit, over a door in an alley, doing the same job it had always done.

You made a sound and put your hand over your mouth.

Bram came back and stood next to you and looked up at the cross, and then at you, and he did not understand, obviously, he could not possibly have understood; but he watched your face for a second and then just stood there quietly beside you and let you have it, which was, you thought later, an extraordinarily decent piece of guesswork.

Then he touched your elbow and walked you to the doorway and stopped on the threshold. He did not come in. He put his hand flat on the doorframe and said something short — four or five words, warm, with a rising end that was almost certainly *you'll be all right* — and then he did the chin-jerk again, and grinned, and was gone back up the alley at a jog, and you stood in the light of a strange door and realised you had absolutely no way of ever finding him again.

Inside there was a waiting room and it smelled of disinfectant and floor polish and, underneath, of tea.

You had thought — you did not know what you had thought. Something white and curved and humming, probably. What there actually was: a rectangular room about the size of a GP's surgery with a bench along one wall in a scuffed green material, a counter at the far end with a shutter that could come down and hadn't, four chairs that did not match, a wall panel with text scrolling slowly across it, and a poster of some kind with an illustration of a hand on it that had been up long enough for one corner to have gone slightly loose.

You never did find out for certain what that building was. You went back looking for it, much later, and by then you could read the sign, and the sign did not settle it.

The man behind the counter was about fifty-five, thick through the chest and going soft with it, with cropped grey hair and a heavy plain torc worn very low and loose, the way a man wears a watch he has stopped noticing. He had a mug. He was doing something with two projected panels side by side and he had the exact facial expression of every second-line engineer at four o'clock on a Friday.

He looked up. He saw a man in a soaked black coat of no recognisable cut, unshaven, grey-faced, blood dried under his nose, no torc, standing in a puddle of his own making.

He put the mug down.

What followed was four or five minutes of pure, undignified, mutually exhausting failure, and it was, in its way, the worst part of the whole day, worse than the road, because on the road you had not been *trying*.

He asked you something. You said, "I'm sorry, I don't understand." He asked it again, slower, and put a hand up to his own throat and made a small circling gesture at the band. You said, "I don't understand, I'm sorry, I don't —" and heard your own voice climbing and stopped. He tried a different language: you could hear that it was a different language, it had different vowels and a different music, and it was equally, absolutely, uniformly opaque. He tried a third, two words, testing. Nothing. He said something to himself that was clearly a mild swear.

You put both hands flat on the counter and said, deliberately, in the calmest voice you could build, "I need help and I can't understand you and I'm not being difficult," which was six seconds of noise to him and which you needed to say anyway.

He looked at you for a long moment. Not unkindly. Assessingly.

Then he said one word over his shoulder, and a man in the back said something back, and there was a scrape of a drawer, and the counter man reached under and came up with a puck.

It was about the size and shape of a hockey puck and it was heavier than it looked. Dull metal, warm-coloured, worn smooth around the rim, with a ring of very small indicator marks around the edge and nothing else on it at all — no screen, no button, no port. He set it on the counter between you and turned it a quarter-turn, and it lit around the rim, and made a sound like a struck glass.

You flinched back from it and he made a *no, no, it's fine* gesture with his palm, the same gesture the world over.

Then it spoke, and it spoke in about nine languages one after another in a flat unhurried male voice, three or four seconds each, working through them like a man trying keys on a ring. You listened to seven of them go past with your fingernails in your palms.

The eighth was English.

It said: `— identify. Say something. Anything. Say something. Identify —`

The accent was from nowhere. The vowels were a bit long. It sounded like a recording of a recording, and one word in it was pronounced in a way no living person had ever pronounced it.

You made a noise like a man punched in the stomach. The counter man's eyebrows went up.

`— continue speaking. The sample is insufficient. Continue speaking —`

His name was Ambrose Kell. He had been on that counter eleven years and before that he had done something else for twenty, and neither of those facts came out for a while, but it is worth knowing what he was, because he was the first person in the future who ever asked you a question.

The translation was terrible. You want to understand how terrible, because it shaped everything that happened in that room and half of what happened afterwards. It ran two to four seconds behind. It dropped words it could not source and rendered the gaps as a flat tone. Its register was assembled out of whatever it had: mostly a kind of stiff instructional English, occasionally something much older and stranger, once or twice a phrase so exactly like a corporate telephone menu that you had to look away. It translated concepts by nearest archival equivalent, which meant that for the first twenty minutes it insisted on calling the collar a *neck-ring*, and it never once managed a place name, rendering every one of them as a burst of raw sound with a marker on either side.

They took you into the back and they scanned you, and it was, of everything that happened that day, the one part that was not frightening at all, which was itself a kind of shock.

The attendant on shift was called Ilan Reyk and he was maybe thirty-three and he had the manner of every good nurse you have ever met: brisk, wholly unimpressed, physically matter-of-fact in a way that would have got him thrown out of an English hospital. He put you on a padded table without ceremony, told you through the puck to take the coat and shirt off, and when you hesitated he simply waited, mildly, with the patience of a man for whom a body is a body and clothes are a scheduling problem. The interface got better while you were on the table, and it got better because you got bored.

That was all it was. You lay there for thirty-five minutes with nothing to do and a HUD in front of your face, and you started poking at it the way you would poke at any new system, and you found out three things.

It did not respond to your hands. You had tried, in the first hour, to swipe at it like an idiot. It did not respond to your eyes either; looking at a panel did nothing.

It responded to *wanting a specific thing*. Not to a vague wish — a vague wish got you a vague panel, or nothing. But if you formed a clean, particular, articulated question, the way you would type it into a search box, something arrived. And if you wanted a panel gone, and meant it, it went.

Which meant that the interface was hard to use for exactly one reason, and the reason was you. Your whole adult life had been a soft haze of half-wanting: half-wanting a different job, half-wanting to ring your mother back, half-wanting to press the button on the fresh-start server, half-wanting Tom to text. The thing hanging in front of your eyes did not respond to any of that at all. It responded to a man who knew what he was asking for and asked for it in one clear line, and it turned out that if you wanted to survive here you were going to have to become that man in about a fortnight.

By the time Reyk was finished you could bring a panel up and dismiss it without moving your face, which was going to matter more than anything else that happened that day.

Then he fitted the torc.

He got it out of a drawer of identical ones. That was the first insult and you did not have the context to feel it yet. It was matte grey, a polymer shell over some cheap pale alloy, with a visible seam along the underside and a short string of characters printed — printed, not engraved — on the inside of the left arm. It was very slightly too big. It weighed about as much as a pair of glasses.

He held it open and gestured at your neck.
