# Three — The Half Level

*Thall 1/3, afternoon to night*

You came out of that alley into the middle of the afternoon with fourteen hundred and fifty quid, a stranger's collar round your throat, and a body that did not hurt anywhere.

That last one nearly put you on the floor. You had gone into that building carrying about nine separate small pains you had stopped being able to feel individually — the wrist, the back, the knee, the base of the thumb, the low grinding thing under the left shoulder blade that had been there since the year you turned twenty-eight — and they had been taken away in thirty-five minutes by a man who had not thought it worth mentioning, and you kept flexing your right hand as you walked and getting nothing back. There was no click in the knee going down the alley steps. You could turn your head all the way to the left.

You had spent a decade budgeting for those pains without knowing it. Every reach, every stair, every night on a bad mattress had been quietly costed and paid, and now the bills had stopped and there was all this money in the account and nothing to spend it on and you did not know how to walk.

Also there was no ringing.

You got about forty metres up the alley and had to stop and put a hand on the wall.

⁂

The Shambles had turned over while you were inside. The morning crowd was gone and the afternoon one was in — slower, looser, more of it sitting down. Men eating late lunches on steps. A cluster of boys of about thirteen doing something complicated and territorial with a ball on a lower terrace. Two men painting a shopfront and arguing about the colour with a third who did not appear to be involved in any way.

And you were thirsty in a way you had never been thirsty in your life. You had been running on adrenaline and a beaker of clinic water for six hours and your mouth had gone to leather.

The fountain was on a small landing where three walkways met, a modest one, nothing like the great upward column in the plaza: a low basin about waist-high with a shallow standing sheet of water in it, and above the sheet, held about ten centimetres clear of the surface, a slow-turning torus of water like a smoke ring made of glass. Cold came off it. That photocopier smell.

You had no idea how to drink from it and you were not going to be the man who put his face in a public water feature on his first day, so you did what you had done all morning, which was wait and watch, and a man in his sixties came up, put two fingers into the side of the ring, and the ring simply — opened. It parted around his hand like a curtain and re-formed behind it, and he cupped and drank from the moving water, twice, wiped his mouth on the back of his wrist, and went.

You put your fingers in.

It let you. It was cold and it moved against your hand with real weight, and there was no sensation of a field or a surface, just water that had been told something and had agreed to it. You drank four handfuls. It tasted of absolutely nothing, which is the most expensive thing water can taste of, and the cold of it went all the way down into your chest and you stood there with your hand in a hole in a ring of levitating water and thought, with a kind of exhausted delight: *all right. All right. That one's just nice.*

And then the man at the next fountain over said something to you, and you found out what the collar did.

He was talking about the boys with the ball, probably — he gestured at them and said eight or nine words with a rueful lift at the end. And there was a stutter, a beat, and then text arrived. Not out loud. Not the puck's flat archive voice. Text, small and grey, sitting in the lower left of your vision at reading distance, in your own alphabet:

`— little [ ] have been at it since tenth hour, mark it, one of them will go through the [ ] glass and then we will all hear about it —`

You made a strangled noise.

He took it for agreement and went off happy.

It took you ten minutes on a step to work out the shape of it. The loaner had the public translation layer on it, the same bad one as the puck, and it was talking to the torcs around you — negotiating a channel, pushing and pulling text, doing it automatically with anybody within a few metres who had not switched the courtesy off. When you paid attention to someone, it gave you them. When you spoke, it pushed a rendering of what you had said into the torcs of the people nearest you, and they read it privately, wherever they read things.

Which meant that from now on, when you said something to a man, he would drop his eyes for half a second — a tiny flick down and left, the exact motion of a person checking a phone under a table — and then respond.

You would learn to live inside that half-second. It is where you lived for months. Every sentence you spoke on that planet for the first half of a year went out into a small delay and came back changed, and you never once got to see what it had turned into on the way.

⁂

Kellwell Lane was eighty metres of pedestrian alley on the third level with the roof half on and half off, and it had two theatres, a comedy room with a hand-lettered board outside, four busker pitches of which three were occupied, and three bars.

You went into the third one because it was the quietest and because you had been on your feet for six hours and could no longer reasonably be described as a functioning person. It was called something you couldn't read; you learned later it was the Half Level, on account of sitting between two floors of the building next door and being reachable from either, which is the single most Shambles fact about it. Inside: a long room, dim, a bar down one side in some dark composite worn pale at the elbows, a dozen tables, a hatch through to a kitchen, and a smell of frying and hops and clean floor.

It was three in the afternoon and there were maybe fourteen men in it.

You had one thought, and the thought was: *sit down before you fall down*, and you took a stool at the end of the bar and put your hands flat on it and let your eyes close for a second.

When you opened them the barman was in front of you.

Here is what you had going for you: nothing. No language. No local knowledge. No idea what anything cost, what anything was, what the etiquette was, whether you paid now or later, whether you ordered by name or by pointing.

Here is what you did, because it is what you had done every night of your working life when a stranger came at you with a problem and you had no information: you smiled, you kept your hands visible, and you said the true thing.

"I've got no idea what to order," you said. "It's my first day. Whatever you'd have."

Half a second. The flick. He read it.

Then he looked at you properly — at the grey polymer loaner, at the coat, at the state of you — and something in his face made a decision, and he pulled a short heavy glass off the shelf and put it under a tap and gave you something dark and slightly sour with a head like porridge, and he did not charge you for it, and he did not say anything about it either, and he was gone down the bar before you could work out how to react.

It was the best drink of your life. It was also, given that you had eaten half a petrol station sandwich twenty-one hours ago in another millennium, an extremely stupid drink to have, and it went into your bloodstream like water into sand.

Which is how you ended up at the table.

⁂

They came in about twenty minutes later, four of them, off a shift.

Two of them had green flashes on the collar of the jacket — you did not know what that meant yet, you only knew it meant *something*, because it was the first time you had seen anything on Arrhenos that looked like a uniform marking. They were loud in the ordinary way. They took the big table at the back by long-established right. They started talking before they sat down and did not stop for four hours.

And one of them, going past you to the bar, knocked your elbow, and turned to apologise, and stopped.

Because you were a man alone at the end of a bar in a wet wool coat of no known cut with a municipal loaner round his neck, and in a city of seven hundred thousand people where everybody knew what a municipal loaner meant, that was not an ambiguous sight.

His name was Pell Arden and he was fifty-eight and built like a door and he had the specific gift, which you have met maybe four times in your life, of being unable to leave anybody sitting on their own. He asked you something. You said, "Sorry — I don't — my translation's terrible," and there was the flick, and he read it, and then he leaned in and read it again, and then he laughed, delighted, and shouted something at the back table.

You were at the back table forty seconds later.

⁂

The four of them:

**Pell Arden**, fifty-eight, thirty-one years in the agritech towers on the industrial edge, running climate stacks for a vertical farm the size of a cathedral. Loud, generous to the point of aggression, bought rounds far too fast, told stories with a lot of hand.

**Darien Foss**, forty-one, agricultural systems monitoring, which as far as you could tell meant he sat between the towers and the money and told each of them what the other was doing. Lean, unhurried, the quietest man at the table and the one everyone actually listened to. He watched you all afternoon in a way that was not suspicion and was not nothing.

**Tobin Cray**, thirty-four, something to do with the transit network, who said about one sentence in ten and whose sentences reliably ended the topic.

And a nineteen-year-old, slumped in the corner seat, who had clearly been dragged along and was making sure everybody knew it, and who came alive between one second and the next when he worked out what you were.

His name was Alexios and everybody called him Lex, and he was Darien's — you took eleven minutes to work out the relationship and got it wrong twice. Not his son. Not his nephew. The eldest son of the two men Darien had been with for six years, one of whom Darien was not married to and the other of whom Darien was also not married to, and all four of them lived in a building where forty-three households shared a dining hall, except that Darien kept his own flat, and had a key, and took the youngest boy to sport on eight-day.

You got all of this out of about ninety seconds of ordinary table conversation and had to go to the toilet afterwards to sit down.

⁂

The rounds nearly finished you.

You worked it out because working things out is the only thing you have ever been good at, and because after twenty minutes you noticed that nobody ever asked. Nobody said *my round*. Nobody offered. What happened was that a glass went empty, and eleven or twelve seconds later a particular man stood up without comment, and the particular man was different every time, and the sequence was not random.

You watched three cycles and got it: Pell, Tobin, Darien, Pell, Tobin, Darien. Lex was not in the rotation, which you assumed meant money and which turned out to mean age. It went round in a fixed order with no announcement whatsoever, and the order was, as far as you could ever establish, decided sometime in the previous decade and never revisited.

And after the fourth round it stopped being three-handed, because Pell got up when it was not his turn, and Tobin said something short, and Pell said something back with a jerk of the head at you, and Darien said one word, and Tobin shrugged, and the sequence became four.

They had put you in. They had put you in without asking you and without telling you, and the only way you knew was that when Darien's round came and went, nobody stood up, and the table went on talking, and after about fifteen seconds the silence started to have a shape.

You stood up.

It took you an unforgivable amount of time at the bar, and the barman helped without appearing to, and the payment nearly beat you — you had watched three men do it and you still fumbled it, going for the thumb when this bar's plate wanted the flat palm, and having to be shown, and going red. Forty-one quid. Four-tenths of a day. You carried the glasses back on a tray you had not been given and had simply taken, which was apparently also wrong, and Pell laughed so hard at the tray that he had to put his head on the table.

But you had bought your round in the right slot with the right glasses and you never dropped out of the rotation again, and if you want to know the single most important thing you did in your first week on that planet, it was standing up eleven seconds late at a table in a bar on Kellwell Lane.

Then Tobin said something to the hatch, and a plate came, and you found out about the fold.

You had eaten half a sandwich in twenty-one hours. The fold was a flatbread the size of your two hands, folded once, packed with something dark and spiced and pulled and a shocking quantity of sharp pickled vegetable, and it came in a twist of waxed paper because it is a walking food and nobody had told the kitchen that this table was sitting down.

You ate it in about ninety seconds and there was a silence at the table and you looked up and all four of them were watching you eat with the frank interest of men at a zoo.

"Sorry," you said, with your mouth full, and the flick went round the table, and Pell ordered three more.

⁂

Then you shut up and listened for two hours, and this is what the world turned out to be.

Pell had four sons and complained about all of them at length and could not conceal, at any point, that he thought they were the finest four men ever produced. The eldest was thirty-six and worked in the same towers and this was a source of enormous public irritation and total private satisfaction. The second was at Zespol doing something with cold-country ecology that Pell described in the tone of a man describing a hobby he had given up hoping his child would grow out of. The third was in the merchant fleet and had not been home in fourteen months and there was a small silence after that one. The fourth was eleven.

Pell was fifty-eight and had an eleven-year-old.

Tobin had none and had never wanted any and said so flatly when Pell needled him about it, in a way that made clear the needling was ritual and about ninety years old.

Darien had none of his own and had three, in the sense that mattered, and the way he talked about the youngest — the one who was eleven and wanted to be a jockey and would not be, because his coaches saw a runner and he had the wrong build for it and could not be told — was so exactly the way any decent man talks about a kid he loves and cannot help that you had to look at the table.

And nowhere in two hours did anybody mention a mother.

Not as an absence. That is what you could not get past. There was no gap where a mother should be, no careful stepping around, no widowerhood in anybody's voice. Pell had four sons and he had *got* them, one at a time, over thirty-five years, from an institution he referred to so casually that the translator kept rendering it as `the [ ]` and then, eventually, once, as `the Continuity` with a capital letter it had decided on by itself. Two of his sons he had raised with a man he had been married to for nineteen years and was not married to now, amicably, and who came to dinner. One he had raised alone with the help of four neighbours and a man who ran a bakery. And the eleven-year-old he was raising with two other men in a household you never fully mapped, one of whom he loved and one of whom he described, without any embarrassment at all, as somebody he was extremely fond of and had never once wanted to go to bed with, which was, he said, the ideal arrangement for raising a child and he wished he'd worked it out forty years earlier.

Darien had met Lex's two fathers separately. They had married each other sixteen years ago because they had been matched to raise a boy together, had done the two-in-the-morning years side by side, and had come out the far end of it in love — *in that order*, the translator managed, `they became the household first and the love came after, which he says is backwards and which everyone else says is the usual way round`.

And it went past. That was the thing. It went past in the ordinary flow of a Tuesday afternoon's talk, in among the price of the tram pass and whether the Titans would go up this year and a long detailed grievance about a supplier, and nobody stopped, because there was nothing to stop for.

You sat in a bar on a planet you could not name and understood, slowly, with a rising sensation that was not joy and not grief and had elements of both and mostly consisted of vertigo, that you had spent thirty-one years being careful about something that did not exist here.

Not permitted. Not tolerated. Not *accepted*, which is a word that requires somebody to be doing the accepting. There was no word for what you were, because there was nothing to hold it up against, and therefore there was no word for what your father had never quite said in 2009 either, and no word for the eleven months you spent not telling anybody at your first job, and no word for the specific way you had learned to say *seeing someone* instead of *seeing a bloke*, which you had stopped needing to do about six years ago and had never entirely stopped doing.

All of it. The whole apparatus. Gone. Not defeated — *never built*.

You would have expected that to feel like an enormous door opening. What it actually felt like, sitting there with a glass in your hand, was standing in a field where a house you had spent your whole life navigating around had never been put up, and finding that you were still, involuntarily, walking round the shape of it.

Tobin, at some point in the third hour, said something to you and put two fingers briefly on the back of your hand and looked at you with a mild, entirely unloaded question in his face, and you did not read it, because the register was three thousand years out of your range, and you said something pleasant and vague and he took his hand back and went on with what he was saying and it never came up again and was never awkward for one second.

You worked out what that had been about four days later, lying awake.

⁂

They asked about the Drift in the fourth round, and they asked the way you would ask a Norwegian about snow.

That was the thing you had braced for and got wrong. You had been sitting there for two hours waiting to be interrogated and what actually arrived was a mild, slightly bored, entirely well-meant tourist curiosity, delivered by men who had a picture of the Drift assembled almost entirely out of Vegan entertainment serials and who were about sixty per cent wrong in ways that were far more useful to you than being right.

`— is it true you can see out. from where you sleep. one of my sons says they put the — [ ] — the sleeping racks against the hull —`

`— how do you sleep at all, with it moving —`

`— they do the feasts. the big ones. is that real or is that for the — [ ] — for the cameras —`

And from Lex, leaning forward on both elbows, at nineteen, with the total absence of tact that is the birthright of nineteen:

`— is it true there are women. like, actually. in the corridor, every day. do you just — see them. all the time.`

And Darien said his name in a particular tone and Lex said `what` in the eternal voice of a teenager who knows exactly what.

So you did.

You told them about a corridor with eleven doors on it where you knew the sound of three of the neighbours' televisions and had never learned any of their names. You told them about a flat with a boiler that had needed replacing for two years and a landlord who did not, and about how in the winter you could hear the pipes going in the wall like something breathing, and about the specific way you stopped noticing a noise like that and then noticed it again on the one night you couldn't sleep. You told them about the lift being out for five weeks and about carrying shopping up eight flights and about the man on the fourth floor who always held the door.

You told them that yes, you saw women every day, all the time, everywhere, and that it was so unremarkable that you had never once in your life had a thought about it until this afternoon; that your mother made a particular kind of dreadful tea and had opinions about your hair; that when you were fifteen you sat next to a girl called Nadia in maths for a year and she used to draw on your exercise book and you had not thought about her in a decade and had thought about her twice in the last four hours.

You told them about the corridor and the boiler and the shopping and Nadia, and every single word of it was true, and they took all of it as testimony from a tin can eleven light years away, and nodded, and Pell said something that came through as `— that is the same everywhere, then. the man who holds the door. there is one on every deck —` and raised his glass at you.

And you have never in your life been so close to going to pieces in public.

You did lie, twice, and both times you were asked something you could not sidestep. You said the haven you came off had gone on without you, which by then you had said four times and had begun to believe. And when Tobin asked, quietly, at the very end, what its name was, you said a word — you said the first sound your mouth produced — and it was `Ninth Hour`, and the translator did something odd with it, and Tobin nodded and let it go, and you sat with your ears roaring, and that is how the guild you drifted out of got carried across the gap and nailed onto your life for good.

⁂

Lex got you at the door.

The others were arguing about who was walking which way and Darien was doing the quiet arithmetic of a man working out whether the off-worlder had anywhere to sleep, and Lex slid in front of you with his hands in his jacket and said, in a great rush, the thing he had clearly been building up to for an hour and a half.

`— you cannot go about in that.`

You looked down at yourself. Wool overcoat, four hundred years out of date and about a thousand miles out of context, still faintly damp at the hem, over a work shirt with a collar that had been ironed in a flat that no longer existed.

`— no, but really. everyone in here has been looking at you all afternoon and it is not because you are Drift. i can take you. i know where to go. these lot buy their clothes at — [ ] — they buy their clothes where their fathers bought their clothes —`

Somewhere behind him Tobin said something without turning round and Lex went scarlet.

And here is the thing you noticed, and the reason you said yes: the boy's torc was wrong. Not shabby — expensive, actually, worked in a pale metal with a fine line of something green let into it, obviously chosen with great care by someone with money. But it sat differently, thinner and lighter and a shade too high on the throat, and it was the only other torc in that room that was not an adult's, and he had spent the entire afternoon touching it and pushing it down.

Two of you at that table wearing the wrong collar. He had clocked it in the first minute and you had clocked it in the third hour, and it is the only reason any of what came after happened.

"All right," you said. "Yes. Please. Thank you."

⁂

The shop he took you to was four floors up and out of the market entirely, in the good streets on the west side where the walkways were wider and had trees in them, and it was called Auren, and it was a chain, and it was the sort of chain that goes to enormous trouble to look like it isn't one.

You knew within eight seconds that you could not afford to be in it. It is a thing you can smell. There was too much floor. There were nine garments on the whole of the first level, hung a metre apart like exhibits, and there was a man whose entire job was to be pleasant near the door, and Lex walked in with the total unconcern of somebody who has never once in his life had to look at a price before touching something.

"Lex," you said. "Lex. I've got fourteen hundred quid and I need to eat with it."

The flick. He read it. He waved his hand.

`— it is fine.`

"It is not fine, I —"

`— it is fine, it goes on the household, my fathers will not even — look. look at this one. this is what they are wearing in Landwick. not here. here they are still doing the — [ ] — this is a year ahead, this is what it is going to be —`

And you were tired, and half-drunk, and you had been awake for twenty-four hours across two millennia, and he was nineteen and thrilled and doing you a genuine kindness in the only currency he had ever been given, and you let it happen.

It took an hour and ten minutes. You were measured by a frame that came down out of the ceiling and did in four seconds what a tailor would have done in twenty minutes. The clothes were not remarkable to look at — that was the whole point of them, and it took you weeks to understand it — dark, plain, soft, closer to the body than anything you had ever worn, with a long unstructured overcoat in something that weighed nothing and shed rain like glass and cost, you found out by squinting at the display when Lex's back was turned, nine hundred and forty quid.

Nine days.

The total was two thousand eight hundred and sixty.

Lex paid it with a flat palm over the plate and did not look at the number.

You dug your heels in over the torc and it was the first fight you had on that planet, and you won it, and the winning of it told you something useful about yourself.

Lex wanted to take you to a place on the same level with a lit frontage and a display of torcs turning slowly on rods. You said no. He said `why`, at some length, and the honest answer was: *because a shopfront that beautiful is selling a look, and this thing is going to be my identity, my money, my medical record and my ability to talk, and I would like it fitted by somebody boring who has done it four thousand times.*

What came out through the translator was probably worse than that. Lex sulked for a hundred metres.

Iolas Mern was sixty-something and had a room on Level 6 with a bench, a stool, two chairs, a wall of shallow drawers, and no display of any kind. He looked at the municipal loaner for about a second and a half, said `right`, and got to work, and did not ask you a single question about yourself that was not directly relevant to a neck.

It took ninety minutes. It was, apart from the clinic, the most competent thing you saw in your first month. He measured you eleven ways. He asked how you slept, whether you'd ever broken your collarbone — you said yes and he said `it does not read as broken` and you said `it was fixed this morning` and he wrote something down without comment. He talked you through the tiers with the flat disinterest of a man who does not care which one you buy, and steered you, at two separate points, downward.

And then he came to registration, and stopped, and put both hands on the bench.

`— it will not be yours.`

"Sorry?"

`— the hardware is yours. i will fit it, it is a good one, it will last you forty years. but it can only be — [ ] — registered against what you have, and what you have is a case number. it is a municipal issue with a better shell on it. it will pay, it will talk, it will call for a doctor. it will not say who you are, because there is nothing to say.`

You sat on that stool for a moment.

`— you will want to go to the registry,` he said, `and swear to something, and be somebody. that is not my trade. i only wanted you to know that you are buying a very good — [ ] — a very good coat, and not a house.`

The word happened on the way out of Mern's.

Mern said something to you at the door — a short send-off, four or five syllables — and you understood it, and the subtitle arrived about a second and a half afterwards and said `come back in a year and i will look at it`, and by the time it arrived you had already known.

Not all of it. One word out of the five. The word for *come back*, or possibly *again*, or possibly *one more* — you had heard it forty times that afternoon at the table, every single time a glass went down and a man stood up, and somewhere between the third and the eleventh repetition your brain had quietly filed it without asking you, and it had come out at the door, ahead of the machine, on its own.

He walked you round afterwards, because he was nineteen and had an off-worlder and was not going to waste him, and because you had nowhere to be, which he had not asked about and which he had definitely worked out.

He showed you the stadium from the outside — a great sunken oval bowl with the field below ground level, closed and empty, and he was scathing about the local team for four straight minutes with the passionate specificity of somebody who has never missed a home match. He showed you a walkway where the whole west side of the city dropped away in terraces and the light was going that peculiar cold copper that the modified trees did in autumn, and the fountains were coming on all across the lower districts as the sun went, one after another, each one turning into a soft standing lamp of moving water. He complained about the city continuously and could not keep the love out of his voice for eleven consecutive words.

And on a bench on that walkway you emptied your coat pockets to move things into the new one, and the phone came out.

He stopped mid-sentence.

⁂

You should understand that you had barely looked at it since the clinic. It was thirty-eight per cent and dead to the world and it had been in your pocket all day the way a wound is in your body all day, and you had not taken it out because taking it out meant looking at the lock screen, and the lock screen had a photograph on it.

Lex reached, and then did not, and asked, and you gave it to him.

He turned it over twice, very carefully, holding it the way you hold something in a shop before you have decided. Then he said one word to himself and then he said, with a completely different energy to anything he had produced all day:

`— is this real.`

⁂

The shop was three floors up where two buildings leaned together and left a wedge of space between them that somebody had glassed over about two centuries ago, and it was up an actual staircase, and it had no frontage worth the name: a door, a window with a shutter half down, and inside a low room absolutely packed with things that had been made a very long time ago and had been kept.

`— there is a proper one of these in Landwick,` Lex said, in the doorway, with the reflex of a boy who cannot let a single thing be good on its own terms. `— the real Sorian's. this one is — i mean, he is fine —`

The man behind the counter said something dry without looking up that made Lex go quiet for about ten minutes.

Sorian was in his sixties and was, in a city where the men run tall, extremely short, which he had clearly decided about forty years ago to make everybody else's problem. He worked standing on a low platform behind his own counter so that he could look people in the eye, and the platform was worn into two hollows. He did it there and then, on the counter, with three tools, in about forty minutes, and he was so careful with it that Lex got bored and went and looked at things at the other end of the shop, and you stood and watched a short old man in the year 3862 take the back off your phone with the reverence of a man handling scripture, and you did not say a word the entire time.

The extraction took another twenty minutes and cost three hundred quid, which he charged you for and did not discount, and which you respected.

And then everything you had was inside the collar around your throat, and the phone was a beautiful dead rectangle on a square of cloth that belonged, for the next hundred and eighty days, to a pawnbroker.

He wrote the ticket out by hand. He read it to you slowly and made you say back the important parts. Nine thousand four hundred quid advanced. Five per cent on each thirty days, compounding, which he stated twice. One hundred and eighty days to redeem, extendable once at his discretion, and at the end of that either you brought him roughly twelve thousand quid or the thing went to a buyer he already had in mind, and he told you that, unprompted, which he did not have to do.

Then he tore the ticket out of the book along a perforation and handed it to you, and it was paper, actual paper, the first paper you had touched since the receipt in your wallet, and it had a date on it in a script you could not read and eleven characters of which you now knew four.

You put it in your inside pocket and you have never once, since, been in a room where you did not know where it was.

⁂

You paid Lex back on the walkway outside, and he did not want you to.

He went through all of it — that it was on the household, that his fathers genuinely would not notice, that it was a stupid thing to argue about, that you would need it more than they did — and every single word of it was true and generous and he meant it, and none of it was the point, and you could not explain the point through a machine that dropped one word in six.

So you did not try. You just did the flat palm over his plate, six thousand two hundred and sixty quid, and said, "Thank you for today. It's the only good thing that's happened to me since I got here and I'm not going to be somebody you paid for."

The bunkhouse was down past the market's lowest level, near where the deep train came through, in a building that had been something industrial and had been converted eighty years ago and not much touched since. Everybody called it the racks. It cost forty-six quid a night, or two hundred and ninety for a week paid up front, and you paid for three weeks, and the man on the desk did not look at you twice.

It was the cheapest bed in the city and it was the most advanced object you had ever slept in.

The pods went three high in rows down a hall the size of a small warehouse, and yours was on the middle tier, and it was a box a bit over two metres long that you got into feet first. Inside: a mattress that was not a mattress, that took eleven seconds to work out what shape you were and then stopped being anything at all. Air that came in cool and clean and smelled of nothing whatsoever, in a room containing ninety men. A wall panel that ran a view — the previous occupant had left it on a slow grey coastline somewhere with rain coming in off the water, and you could not change it, because the menu was in a language you could not read, and so you lay and watched somebody else's sea for three weeks and grew extremely attached to it. A shower down the hall that used, you established later, a fifth of a litre and left you cleaner than you had ever been in your life.

And when you pulled the panel across, the sound went. Not muffled. *Gone.* Ninety men, a deep-level train every ninety seconds, a city — and a box you could hear your own blood in.
