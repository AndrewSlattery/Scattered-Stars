The counter was shut.

You found it at half past six in the morning, on the fourth try, having walked past the same run of frontages three times without recognising any of it, because you had come to it the first time from a direction you could not reconstruct and because the whole district changed character with the light. Six stools upended on the counter. A shutter down and locked, in a dull ribbed material the colour of a filing cabinet. The two hanging lamps dark. A hand-lettered card fixed to the shutter with four words on it that you photographed with your eyes and could not read, and which your translation layer, when you finally got it to attend to the thing, rendered as:

`— back [ ] . do not [ ] the [ ] .`

Which told you precisely nothing, four separate times, because you made it try four times.

You stood in front of a shut shop in the half-dark for eleven minutes like a man at a graveside. There was no letterbox. There was no way to leave a note, because you could not write, and no way to leave a message, because you had never got his torc and had not known, at the time, that this was a thing you would need, in the way that you never know until afterwards which of the day's small omissions is going to be the one.

You had a whole day and no idea what to do with it, so you did what you had done in Prague in 2018 and in Lisbon in 2022, both times alone, both times having told people at work it was a great trip: you got a day pass and rode everything.

Thirty-one quid at a plate in the Shambles, from a machine that took you six minutes to operate and which a boy of about nine eventually operated for you, with an expression of open contempt that you found completely charming.

**The groundside tram** was the ordinary one, and it was ordinary in the way that public transport is ordinary everywhere and always has been. Two articulated cars running on a reservation down the middle of a boulevard at about the speed of a decent cyclist, doors that opened too slowly, a man asleep at the back with his head against the glass and his mouth open, four people standing when there were nine free seats. It rumbled. It smelled faintly of feet and cleaning product. There was a strip of advertising above the windows in which the same four brands were plainly having the same argument with each other that four brands have always had, and you understood not one word of it and understood all of it perfectly.

You rode it out east for forty minutes into the industrial district and watched the agritech towers come up out of the plateau.

You had thought — you had, without examining it, assumed — that a vertical farm would be a greenhouse. They were not greenhouses. They were blank, and enormous, and there were eleven of them in a loose row along the low ground, each one thirty or forty storeys of windowless composite with heat-exchange fins running up the flanks like the gills of something, and the only thing that told you what they were was the loading yards at the base where the produce came out, in stacked crates, on flatbeds, in quantities that made your eyes go funny. Somewhere inside each of those things was the equivalent of about four thousand hectares of Cheshire, in the dark, in trays, under lights, and Pell Arden had spent thirty-one years keeping one of them at the right temperature.

**The skyline** was the beautiful one and you nearly cried on it, which by that point in your life was not a high bar.

It ran on elevated rail eleven storeys up, in and out and between the buildings rather than over them, so that you were never above the city, you were *inside* it at height. It whined rather than rumbled. It went past windows. Somebody was having an argument in a kitchen on the west side of the northern loop and you went past at forty kilometres an hour and saw about a second and a half of it and thought about them for the rest of the day.

And on the long southern run it came out of the buildings entirely and crossed the top of the slope, and the whole city was underneath you: the terraces going down and down, the Shambles a great irregular scab of roofs and gaps and glass in the middle of it with steam coming off it in four places, the sunken oval of the stadium, the residential districts to the west with their green tops, the towers out east, and everywhere, in every direction, the fountains. Two hundred of them. Not spectacular in daylight — most were only bright discs and slow rings and standing sheets, catching sun — but there were so many that the city read from above as a thing with water in every joint of it, and you sat with your forehead near the glass and thought about a country where somebody, at some point, had decided that this was worth the money, and had gone on deciding it for three hundred years.

**The dusker** you did not enjoy.

It ran deep — properly deep, three or four levels below the lowest street — in bored tunnel, and it was fast, and it was dark, and the stations were long low caverns lit in a cold blue that made everybody in them look worse than they were. It shifted a lot of people very quickly and it had the particular flat exhausted energy of an underground railway anywhere, which you had assumed was a product of the Northern Line and grief and which it turns out is a product of being underground.

It was also where you found out what the tall thing actually meant.

Standing, in a packed car, at half past nine in the morning, you could see over precisely nobody. You are a hundred and seventy-eight centimetres. You had been, your entire life, unremarkably average — a bit under in a room of tall men, a bit over in a room of short ones, and mostly nothing. In that carriage you came up to the shoulder of the man next to you and the top of your head was below the handrail line that everybody else was using, and when you looked down the car all you could see was chests and the backs of jackets and, up near the ceiling, a horizon of heads.

The intercity terminal was the biggest interior you had ever stood in.

It was a single hall the size of four cathedrals laid end to end, with a roof of long shallow arches in something pale that carried the light down and diffused it so that there were no shadows anywhere, and it was full — properly full, not market-full, station-full, with the specific churn of a place where nobody is staying. Eleven platforms in a rank down one side, and the trains at them were not trains. They were long seamless things about the length of two football pitches that sat in a groove rather than on a rail, and when one left it did not accelerate so much as simply, smoothly, cease to be at that platform.

You stood in the middle of the concourse for a long time.

There was a departure board. It was projected in the air above the centre of the hall, four-sided, twenty metres up, and it was enormous and legible and you could not read a single destination on it.

But you could read the times.

That was the thing that got you, standing there with your neck bent back. Two days of grinding at it and you had the numerals — nine of the ten cold, and the tenth one, the one you had guessed was a five, turned out to be a zero, which annoyed you disproportionately and which the layer confirmed with the flat indifference of a machine that had known all along. So you stood in the great hall of the future under a board listing sixty departures and you knew exactly what time every single one of them left and you had no idea where any of them was going.

The longest one was eleven hours. You worked out, from the fact that the layer let slip the name three times in twenty minutes, that the big northern one was called Landwick, and you knew from Lex's four hours of scathing worship that Landwick was the real city and this was not, and that it was three thousand kilometres away, and that in eleven hours you could be there.

And that was when the uniform came round the end of the barrier.

He was not coming for you. You knew that afterwards. He was a transit officer of some kind in a dark blue overall with an orange flash at the shoulder, and he was walking down the line of gates at about four kilometres an hour looking at nothing much, and he was, in point of fact, on his way to help a very old man who had got his bag jammed.

But he was walking towards you, and you had, in the inside pocket of a nine-hundred-quid coat that a teenager had bought you: a laminated identity card from a company that would not exist for another eighteen centuries; a set of keys to a flat in a country that had been dust for longer than that; a wallet containing four bank cards and eleven pounds fifty in coins with a woman's head on them; and a piece of paper with a pawnbroker's hand on it that was the only proof that any of it had ever happened.

Every single object in the world that could destroy you, in one pocket, in a station, with a uniform coming.

And you wanted them gone.

You want to understand how much you wanted it. Not vaguely. Not the ordinary soup of half-wanting that you had lived in for thirty-one years — not *I wish I hadn't brought them*, not *I should have left them at the bunkhouse*. This was the cleanest, hardest, most completely articulated want of your entire life, and it arrived whole, in about a fifth of a second, and it said: **not on me. Not on me. Somewhere that is not on me.**

Your coat went light.

There is no better way to describe it. There was no flash and no sound and nothing to see. The pocket simply became empty, the way a pocket is empty when you have left the thing at home, and the weight against your ribs was gone, and the officer walked past you at a distance of a metre and a half and knelt down to free an old man's bag from a gate mechanism.

And behind your eyes something went off like a flashbulb in a small room, and your nose started to bleed.

⁂

You had thirty seconds of the worst fear of your life, which is saying something, because you had recently had a road stop existing.

You went through the coat four times. You went through it with two hands, then with your eyes, then on your knees on the floor of the concourse with the coat off and inside out and men going round you. You retraced eleven metres of your own path staring at the ground. You had, in that half-minute, absolutely and completely concluded that the pocket had a hole in it, that you had shed the entire evidence of your existence across a public terminal in a city you could not name, and that somebody was going to pick up a laminated card with English writing on it and a photograph of your face.

Then you stopped. You sat down on the floor with your back against a pillar, and you made yourself breathe, and you did the thing you did on the desk when a P1 was running and everybody was shouting, which was to stop *doing* and start *thinking*, and you asked the only useful question available.

*What did I do immediately before it happened?*

And you knew. You had known for twenty seconds and had been refusing it.

So you sat on the floor of the future with blood on your top lip and you formed the want again, exactly, with the same edges, but pointing the other way: **the ticket. The paper one. In my right hand.**

It was in your right hand.

You sat under that pillar for eleven minutes and did not move.

Because here is the thing you had been holding onto with both hands since the first hour: *the interface is me.* You had said it to yourself on the flagstones and on the medical table and in the pod at two in the morning. It has no information I don't have. It knows what I know, it guesses what I guess, it uses my employer's ticket template because it is made out of the inside of my head, and it is a coping mechanism and it is doing an excellent job and I am not mad, I am a frightened man with a very good imagination.

You had been sleeping on that. It was the whole floor.

And a coat pocket had just become empty.

There was no version of that which was psychological. A hallucination does not reduce the mass of a garment. Either four objects had ceased to be in a place and then resumed being in a place, or you had lost an interval of time in a busy terminal and hidden your own belongings somewhere and retrieved them without knowing, which was a theory you examined seriously for ninety seconds and discarded, because the ticket had arrived in your closed hand.

So one of two things was true. Either the thing in front of your eyes was not just you — or you were not just you. And when you turned those over, you found that the second one frightened you considerably more, and that you had already known which one it was, because of the nosebleed.

You had arrived on this planet with a nosebleed. Left nostril. A hard spike behind the eyes and a pressure front to back like being pushed into a wall made of the wall.

You had just had exactly, precisely, identically that, at about a thousandth of the intensity, in a train station, because you wanted four objects to be somewhere else.

You had been getting better at the interface for two days. You had learned on the medical table that it answered clean intention and ignored vague wanting. You had learned in the market that if you asked it a properly formed question you got something and if you flailed at it you got nothing.

What you had not tried was asking it to *hold* something.

You wanted the fourth column and the eleventh column side by side, and you could not, because a human being can hold about seven numbers in working memory and you needed nineteen, and you did the thing everyone does, which is start again from the top, and you lost it, and you started again, and you lost it, and on the third pass you thought, with real irritation and total clarity: **stop losing it. Keep it. Keep it exactly, and put it there, next to that.**

And it did.

The whole column. Twenty-two figures, held, unwavering, at the left of your vision, in your own alphabet, in perfect order, not fading, not drifting, not doing the thing that numbers do in your head after four seconds where the middle of the list goes soft. And when you looked at the eleventh column and thought *and this one*, that came too, and sat beside it, and the two of them stayed there while you looked from one to the other for as long as you liked.

You had never in your life been able to think like that. Nobody has. You had spent four years compensating for not being able to think like that — with notes, with spreadsheets, with three windows open and a pen — and it turned out that the whole of that compensating apparatus had been a workaround for a limitation that you were, apparently, no longer subject to.

Twelve minutes later you had it.

It was not a discrepancy. That was the entire point and it was why a hundred and eighteen days of local men had bounced off it, and it was not because they were stupid, it was because they were *from here*.

The difference in the third column was not constant, but it was periodic, and it drifted, and it drifted by a hair over one part in three hundred and sixty-five, and the sign flipped at intervals, and when you laid the flips against the dates they landed at the turn of the year. Every year. For a hundred and forty years. And then, at one point in the run, the whole thing stepped, once, hard, and never came back.

Two systems disagreeing about how long a year was.

Because this planet had had its *orbit adjusted* to fit a standard calendar. They had moved a world to make the paperwork easier. And on either side of that adjustment there were ledgers, hundreds of years of them, kept in a local year of one length and then restated into a standard year of another, and somewhere in the restating, centuries ago, somebody had applied a correction, and somebody else — later, carefully, conscientiously, working from a clean copy — had applied it again.

Corrected twice. That was all it was. The oldest error in the world.

And you knew it in eleven minutes, not because you were clever, but because you came from a civilisation that had spent seventy solid years doing nothing else: bodging together machines that disagreed about midnight, about leap years, about which end of the date the day went, about whether the century had two digits or four. Your entire species had once nearly frightened itself to death over exactly this and had spent a fortune fixing it and had then done it again with a thirty-two-bit clock. You had a colleague who had lost a fortnight of his life to a Rotterdam depot whose stock counts were wrong by one day, twice a year, in March and October.

There was nobody on that plateau who had ever needed to have that reflex. Their systems had agreed with each other for eleven centuries. They had *fixed* time. And so the one thing your entire miserable trade had accidentally made you an expert in was the one thing this world had comprehensively forgotten how to look for.

⁂

The night was Lex's and it was enormous and you paid for every round you were in and would not be argued with.

It was the run-up to Carnival, which meant three things: that the decorations were already going up in the Shambles despite the official programme starting elsewhere in three days; that every venue in the city had extended its hours and was faintly embarrassed about it; and that Brovdingonai — a city which, by its own proud account, goes out early and comes home at a reasonable hour — was staying out until four in the morning and telling itself this was an exception.

Lex's older friends were four men between twenty-two and twenty-eight, and the dynamic was instantly legible and would have been legible in any pub in England: Lex was the youngest, he was tolerated with genuine affection, they took the piss out of him without mercy, and he had brought an actual off-worlder and was going to dine out on it for a year.

The Coppice was a converted timber warehouse on Kellwell Lane with three floors and live music on two of them and a roof terrace with a view down over the lower districts where the fountains were lit. It was, Lex explained at length and with enormous scorn, the closest this city came to glamour, and would not pass for glamour in Landwick for a single second, and he loved it and could not say so.

The band on the second floor was four men and a thing you never did identify, playing something with a lot of low end, and it was good, and you stood in the middle of a crowd at eleven at night on a planet you could not spell, holding a drink, with a nineteen-year-old shouting a critique of the drummer into your ear that you were receiving as subtitles at a delay of half a second, and you were happy.

That is worth writing down, because there was not much of it that year. You were happy for about three hours.

⁂

And you spent all three of them being looked at.

You noticed it first on the stairs and you assumed you were wrong, because you had thirty-one years of evidence that you were the kind of man who is not looked at. You were the one at the edge of the group photograph. You had spent nine years playing a class that nobody watches. On the apps you had been an entirely middling proposition and had known it and had made a personality out of knowing it.

But you were in a room where every man was ten to fifteen centimetres taller than you and built like an athlete because that is simply what happens when medicine works and the baths are municipal and everybody has been alive for eighty years without ageing, and in that room you were small, and pale, and shaped like somebody who had spent a decade at a desk, and none of that had been sanded off by two hundred years of gene therapy, and the effect was not what you would have predicted at all.

You were *strange*. And strange, in a city where the whole population is drawn from a narrow and extremely well-maintained band, turns out to be interesting.

Three separate men came and stood next to you at the rail on the roof terrace over the course of the evening and started conversations that went nowhere, because a conversation through a bad translation layer with a man who has eleven words is not a conversation. One of them touched your shoulder to make a point and left his hand there for three seconds longer than an English hand would have. One of them asked you something you could not read at all, twice, and then laughed and gave up and got you a drink anyway.

And you could not read any of it. Not one signal. You had spent your entire adult life running a very sophisticated, very expensive, extremely well-tested piece of software for exactly this — for reading a room, for the second glance, for the difference between friendly and interested and the third thing that is neither and needs handling carefully — and it did not run here. Not one instruction of it. The whole thing was compiled for a machine that did not exist on this planet: for a world with two kinds of people in it, and a history, and consequences, and a set of learned reflexes about who you can look at for how long.

Here there was nothing to be careful about. And so all the carefulness had nowhere to go and just sat in you, humming, like a fan in an empty room.

⁂

A Brovdingonai night ends in water or it isn't over. Lex told you that at half one in the morning as though he had invented it, and everybody else groaned, which told you he had heard it from his fathers.

The baths were four minutes' walk and were open all night in Carnival week and cost twenty-two quid.

You had one moment in the changing hall, and it lasted about four seconds, and it was the last serious moment of Earth you had that night: seven men undressing without the smallest self-consciousness in a well-lit room, talking across each other about a referee, and one of them turning to ask you something mid-sentence with his shirt over his head. Nobody looked at anybody. Nobody didn't look at anybody. There was no charge in the room at all — not suppressed, not managed, *absent* — and the absence was so complete that your own thirty-one years of carefully calibrated eye discipline stood up in you like a man standing up at the wrong moment in a church.

You got in the water.

It was two big pools and three small ones and a cold plunge, in a hall with a vaulted roof and lights under the water, and the second pool was hot enough to hurt and you sat on the ledge in it up to your collarbones and felt two days come out of your spine.

And a man you had not met, about forty, in the pool, said something to you that the layer rendered as:

`— you are with the [ ] boy's lot. i have not seen you. do you want to come up to the — [ ] — after. it is quieter.`

And you read it, and then read it again, and you understood it — properly, completely, the actual meaning and not the words — and the understanding arrived without effort or ambiguity for the first time in two days, because it turns out that this particular sentence is the one thing that translates cleanly across eighteen centuries and any number of light years.

You said, "That's kind. Not tonight."

He read it. He said `— all right —` and something else that was probably about the water temperature, and turned back to his conversation, and that was the entire event, and it left no mark of any kind on anybody in that pool except you.

And that is what you lay awake about.

Not the offer. The *shrug*. There was no risk in it and no cost to refusing and no charge in it having happened and no second glance afterwards and no calculation on either side, and the whole enormous careful architecture that you had built at fifteen and rebuilt at nineteen and been quietly maintaining ever since — every bit of that machinery had just been asked to process a situation with none of the inputs it was designed for, and had returned nothing, and you had sat in a hot pool at two in the morning feeling something that took you a long time to name and which was, in the end, closest to grief.

All that work. All that care. All those years of it. And here it was worth nothing, because here it had never been necessary, and there was nobody in that building you could have explained that to in a thousand words, let alone eleven.

⁂

You got back to the racks at ten past four and you did not sleep, and this is the part you are proudest of, because it is the only part of that day where you behaved like yourself.

You pulled the panel across. Ninety men and a deep train every ninety seconds and then a box you could hear your own blood in. You sat cross-legged on a mattress that was not a mattress with somebody else's grey coastline running on the wall, and you did the thing you had been trained to do by four years on a service desk and nine years of trying to work out which of a raid boss's three mechanics was actually killing people, which is that you did not theorise.

At half past five you did the last one, and you did it because you had thought of it at about test three and had been refusing to think about it since.

There was a beetle in the washroom, on the wall by the drain: about the size of your thumbnail, dark, faintly iridescent, one of the modified species that live in the rooftop gardens and get everywhere. You caught it under a beaker and carried it back to the pod with your heart going, and you sat with it in your cupped hands for four minutes, arguing with yourself.

Then you did it.

It went. There was no flash for it and no resistance and nothing at all, and your hands were empty, and the whole world went very quiet.

You brought it back eleven seconds later.

It sat on your palm. It did not lie still. It did not shudder or curl or do any of the things a hurt thing does. It cleaned one antenna with a foreleg in the unhurried manner of a creature that has not been anywhere, and then it walked to the edge of your hand and considered the drop and opened a wing case and thought better of it, and you put it very carefully out through the gap in the panel and it went off into the dark of the rack hall to do whatever it does.

And you sat there.

Because it worked on living things.

It works on living things, and it costs a nosebleed, and the nosebleed is the same nosebleed you woke up with on warm stone in a market at the top of the morning two days ago with your hands still at ten and two on a steering wheel that was not there.

Somewhere — on a flooded road under a motorway bridge outside Warrington at eleven minutes past five on the morning of Wednesday the fifteenth of October, 2025 — something had wanted a man to not be there.

And it had cost it a nosebleed too.
