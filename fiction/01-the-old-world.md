# One — The Old World

*Warrington, 14–15 October 2025 → the Fountain Steps, Thall 1/3, 3862 C*

You were thirty-one years old and you had a system for everything, which was, in the end, the whole problem.

The system for the night shift went like this. You arrived at ten to nine and swiped in at the side door because the front one had a mechanism that took two hands and you were usually carrying coffee. You took the second desk from the window, not the first, because the first got the draught. You logged in, you cleared the overnight queue down to whatever was genuinely broken, and then you sat in the long grey hours between two and five with the building all to yourself and the motion sensors going out over the empty half of the floor, one bank of lights after another, until the office looked like a ship settling into deep water.

The lights over your own desk had a timer of eleven minutes. You knew it was eleven minutes because you had counted, twice, on separate nights, using the clock in the corner of the ticketing system, and you had written it down. So every eleven minutes, without lifting your eyes from the screen, you raised your left arm above your head and waved it in a slow arc, like a man being rescued from a sea nobody had noticed him fall into, and the lights came back on, and you carried on.

Nobody had ever seen you do this. That was the part you thought about, later, when you had a great deal of time to think and very little else. Six hundred and something nights of raising your arm in an empty room so a sensor would agree that you existed, and there was no version of that story you could have told anyone that would have made it funny.

The queue that Tuesday night was mostly rubbish. A depot manager in Rotterdam who could not print, because he never could, because he kept installing a driver from a memory stick that somebody's nephew had given him in 2019. Eleven multi-factor lockouts, which meant eleven people had got a new phone at the weekend and had restored it from a backup and had not thought about the little six-digit numbers until the moment they needed them. A P2 from the warehouse system that turned out to be a scheduled reboot that somebody in infrastructure had forgotten to put on the calendar, again. You closed it with a note that was polite and clear and contained no trace whatsoever of what you thought, because your notes were public and your annual review quoted them.

You were good at this. That was the thing that never quite fitted into anybody's picture of you, including your own. You were properly, unglamorously good at it — you could hear a fault in the way a person described it, the way a mechanic hears a bearing; you could tell from the first eight words of a ticket whether the user was frightened or angry or lying, and you handled all three differently and well. In four years you had escalated fewer tickets than anyone on the team and had never once been rude to anybody, including the man from finance who called you *son* and the woman in procurement who cried on the phone about a spreadsheet and then apologised for eleven minutes, which was, you had noticed, exactly one lighting cycle.

None of it went anywhere. That was not self-pity; it was an observation you had tested. There was no ladder. There was a service desk, and above the service desk there was a service desk manager, and he had been there nine years and was forty-four and had a caravan. Twice a year your own manager sent an email about *utilisation* with a bar chart in it, and the bar chart had never once in four years been about anything you could actually change.

At two-forty you ate the sandwich you had bought at the petrol station on the way in — egg and cress, on bread with the structural integrity of a wet flannel — and left half of it in the packet on the desk, because you always did, because eating a whole one at three in the morning made you feel like something being fattened.

At three you did the drive-round: the server room, the comms cupboard on the second floor, the UPS panel. This was not your job. It had been added to your job during a reorganisation in which four people had left and none had been replaced, and you had accepted it in the specific way you accepted things, which was completely and without complaint and with a small cold stone laid down somewhere at the back of your chest. The server room ran at eighteen degrees and roared. You liked it in there. It was the one place in the building where the noise was so total that thinking became optional.

At three-fifty you opened the laptop.

⁂

*Aetherbourne* had been running for eleven years and you had played it for nine of them. You had been there for the bad expansion and the good one and the one everybody argued about, and you had a Mender at the level cap with a set of gear that had taken you five months to assemble and that a patch had made irrelevant in a single Thursday afternoon.

A Mender was a healer. You had never played anything else, not seriously — you had rolled the big armoured ones and the ones with the swords, and you had got them to the middle levels and stopped, and you knew perfectly well what that meant and had never said it out loud to anybody either. What you liked was the thing nobody watched: the bar going down and you catching it; twelve people alive at the end of a fight who would have been eight without you; the specific, private, unshareable satisfaction of a job whose success looked exactly like nothing happening.

Your guild was called Ninth Hour and you had been in it for six years, which in that world was a geological era. There had been a stretch — the second year, mostly, and some of the third — when it had been the best thing in your life without any competition at all, when there had been eighteen of you on voice chat four nights a week and somebody was always awake and somebody was always up for something, and you had known the sound of their houses: a dog, a boiler, a partner asking about dinner, a road outside a window in Halifax and another one outside a window in Tampa. You had never met a single one of them. You had helped one of them move flat by talking to him about it for four hours while he packed.

It had thinned. That was all. Nothing had happened, which was worse than something happening. People got children and jobs with earlier starts, and the ones who stayed got very good, and the ones who got very good got recruited by guilds that raided on a schedule you could not keep, because your schedule was four nights on and four nights off and rotated, and there was no negotiating with it. Six weeks ago they had brought in a second Mender for the progression team. Nobody had said anything to you about it. You had found out from the roster, which was a public document, the way you found out about most things.

The message from the guild leader — a man in Derbyshire called Ste who you would have described, if pressed and drunk, as one of the two or three closest friends you had — had come in at nine that evening and said *no worries mate*, which was a response to a thing you had said, and you had read it eleven times since, and it did not get better.

So at three-fifty in the morning you had the character creation screen open instead.

The new server was going live at midday: a fresh start, everybody at level one, no legacy gear, no eleven years of accumulated advantage, no bank full of things that had once mattered. You had been looking at it on and off for a week. You had the name reserved. You had spun the model around and given him black hair, because you always did, and a jaw you did not have, and you had gone into the class list and looked at the ones with the swords for a while, honestly and seriously, the way a man looks at a holiday brochure for a country he will not visit.

And then you clicked back to Mender, because you were not going to lie to yourself at four in the morning in an empty building.

Here is what you would have said, if there had been anybody there to say it to, about why you had given nine years to a make-believe world with a subscription fee. It was not the dragons. It was that it *told you*. Somewhere in that game there was a number, and the number was true, and if you did the thing the number went up. There was a page you could open that listed, in plain language, precisely what was required of you and precisely what you would get. When you failed there was a log that said why. When you did well, somebody's health bar was full, and that was not an opinion. Nobody in Aetherbourne had ever left you on read. Nobody had ever restructured the department. Nobody had ever put your contribution in a bar chart next to a target set by a man in another building who had never once asked what you actually did.

You wanted rules. You wanted them printed. You wanted, badly and unfashionably and with a hunger you had mostly learned to keep quiet about, to live somewhere that would just *tell you what the rules were*, and you had spent a decade renting that feeling by the month.

The cursor sat over the button that said **BEGIN AGAIN**.

There was four hundred quid of overtime in this month's shifts and a voicemail from your mother you had not returned since Sunday and a man called Tom who had stopped replying eight days ago after three weeks that had, you were fairly sure, been going well, and a hookup a fortnight before that whose name you genuinely could not now retrieve and whose kitchen you could describe in detail. There was a flat with a radiator that worked and a boiler that did not. There was no crisis anywhere. That was the thing. There was no crisis, no illness, no debt, no cruelty, nothing you could point at and say *there, that, that's what's wrong* — there was just a life that had quietly closed over you like water over a dropped coin, and a man of thirty-one waving his arm at a ceiling every eleven minutes so the lights would stay on.

You thought, with your finger on the trackpad: *I would give anything to start over somewhere with a manual.*

Then the on-call phone went, because the depot manager in Rotterdam started early, and you closed the laptop without pressing the button, and you dealt with it, and at ten past five you swiped out of the side door into the rain.

⁂

The business park at that hour was a car park with ambitions. Four buildings the colour of wet cardboard around a retention pond with a sign about newts. The rain had been going since eleven and had settled into the fine, patient, sideways kind that gets into a coat's collar and stays. Your car was a fourteen-year-old estate with a boot full of things you kept meaning to take to the tip, and it started on the second turn, which you took as a good omen because you took everything as an omen at five in the morning.

The road home was seven miles and you could have driven it asleep and had, more or less, on several occasions. Out past the roundabout with the sculpture nobody liked. Along the dual carriageway with the crash barrier that had been mangled since February. Left onto the old road that went under the motorway through a bridge that flooded whenever anybody so much as mentioned rain, and which you took anyway because the alternative added eleven minutes and you were tired.

The radio was doing the five-fifteen headlines. You had the heater on your feet and the blower on the screen and the wipers on intermittent and you were thinking, in the low idling way of a man three-quarters asleep behind a wheel, about whether to make the eggs or just go to bed.

The underpass was flooded. You saw it in the headlights — the sheet of it, black and moving, the way it took the light and gave nothing back — and you did what everybody does, which is slow down about a third as much as you should. The car went in. The bow wave came up over the bonnet, a big soft muscular shove of water, and the wheel went light and vague in your hands.

That was the last thing that happened for a reason.

Because then the road ended, and it did not end the way a road ends.

There was no bang. Nothing hit you. There was no slide, no spin, no barrier, no other vehicle, nothing so straightforward as an accident. The road ahead simply *stopped being included*. It was like watching a word get taken out of a sentence — not crossed out, not obscured, but retrospectively never there, so that the sentence closed smoothly over the gap and read perfectly well without it. The bridge, the streetlight, the wet chevrons on the far wall, the standing water, the noise of the standing water: subtracted, all at once, with no edge and no seam and no sound of any kind.

The absence of sound was the worst part. You would come back to that for years. Not silence — silence is a thing a room does, silence has a texture, you had stood in enough empty buildings at four in the morning to know exactly what silence sounds like. This was the removal of the category. There was no medium left for anything to happen in.

You had time for exactly one thought and the thought was not profound. It was: *I've done something wrong.*

Then everything came back at once, and all of it was different.

⁂

The first thing was pressure — a hard shove of it, front to back, as though you had been pushed into a wall made of the wall itself. Your ears went. Your sinuses went, a bright hot spike behind the eyes. Something enormously loud started immediately and did not stop.

The second thing was *warmth*, and it was obscene. You had been driving through a wet October morning at eleven degrees with your feet in a fan heater, and now there was sun on the back of your neck. Actual sun. Weight of it, the specific pressure of it on skin, and under your palms a paving stone that had been in that sun for hours.

The third thing was smell, and there was far too much of it. Frying. Fat and something hot and spiced, cumin-adjacent, not quite. Smoke of the good kind. Coffee, but wrong, but definitely coffee — roasted, close, four or five different roasts arguing with one another. Wet stone. Cut herbs. A hard clean electric bite underneath it all, like the air near a photocopier or a railway line, and above everything a green wet smell of growing things that had no business being anywhere near a fryer.

And sound. God, the sound. You had come out of an absolute nothing straight into a market at the top of the morning, and your brain, which had been carefully managing an empty office and a dark road, simply put its hands up. Hundreds of voices at conversational volume in an enclosed space with hard surfaces. Water — a lot of water, falling and also, impossibly, not falling. Music from two directions that did not agree. A clatter of pans. Somebody laughing very hard about nine feet away. A deep vibration coming up through the stone into your kneecaps at intervals, and a high whine from above, and a rumble from somewhere south, three separate rhythms of something arriving and leaving and arriving.

You were on your hands and knees on warm pale stone in a soaked coat. Your hands were still at ten and two. You noticed that with a kind of remote clinical horror — that your hands had kept the shape of a steering wheel that was not there, that the muscles had not been told.

You were, you realised, screaming, and had been for some seconds, and could not hear yourself.

⁂

What you saw when you got your head up was a plaza.

It was on a slope, and it was built into a slope, which are different things and it was doing both. A broad shallow staircase went up and away from you in three flights, wide enough to be a place rather than a route, and men were sitting on it in twos and threes and sevens the way people sit on steps in the sun everywhere in the universe. Above and behind the steps the buildings went up — six storeys, eight, twelve — and they were not separate buildings so much as one enormous inhabited cliff, terraced and balconied and hung with green, with bridges running between them at four different heights and walkways that were somehow translucent, so that you could see the shapes of feet crossing overhead.

There were trees. They were the wrong colour. Not autumn-wrong, not amber and rust — these were a deep hot purple shading to copper at the tips, and they were doing it in a way that was unmistakably autumn, unmistakably a deciduous tree in October, and no tree that colour had ever existed on the planet you were born on.

And in the middle of the plaza, ten feet from you, there was a fountain, and the water in it was going *up*.

Not a jet. A jet is fine, a jet is plumbing. This was a broad standing column of water perhaps four metres across, rising in a slow unhurried sheet the way water falls, running upward with visible weight and visible surface tension, breaking at the top into a slow crown that came apart into droplets and then just — stopped. Hung. Turned. Went back down inside itself. It made a sound like a weir and the air around it was cold and smelled of the photocopier smell, and there was a boy of about nine standing at the very edge of it holding his hand flat against nothing, laughing, being splashed by water that was refusing to go anywhere.

You looked at that for a while. Some part of you that had spent four years asking people to describe their fault clearly noted, in a tiny flat voice, that this was not a thing that water did.

You looked past it, and that was worse, because past it the plaza opened into a market, and the market went *down* as well as up. There were stalls, and awnings, and the stalls had stalls under them; there were three floors of frontage that you could see from where you were, and a fourth suggested by light coming up through a gap, and people moving on all of them at once, and there was no wall between inside and outside anywhere — the roofs simply arrived and departed over your head with no ceremony, so that a run of colonnade turned into open sky turned into a covered arcade in the space of a hundred feet.

And there were men.

Hundreds of them. Everywhere. Sitting on the steps and standing at the stalls and leaning on the rail of the level above and walking through with the flat unhurried purpose of people going to work. Big men mostly — and you did clock that, you clocked it early and could not make it mean anything, that the crowd ran tall, a head-and-a-bit above your own five foot ten with a consistency that made you feel like you had wandered into the wrong year group at school.

They were all wearing collars.

That was what your mind fastened on, and it fastened hard. Every single one. A band of metal at the throat, a finger to two fingers thick, open at the front or closed, some plain, some worked, brass and grey and blue-black and one that was a startling green. The nine-year-old at the fountain had one, sized small. The old men arguing at a café table twenty feet away had them. Two lads going past you at a jog with a bag between them had them. There was a man selling something out of a hot cabinet with an apron over his and it caught the light when he turned.

Two hundred people in a public square in the sunshine, buying breakfast, all wearing the same thing around their necks.

Your body did the arithmetic before you did. Your heart went, your vision narrowed to a grey tunnel with the fountain at the end of it, and your breathing came apart into short useless snatches that did nothing at all, and you thought, with total clarity: *I am going to die here and I don't know what here is.*

And then, because you had asked for it, and because a mind will build a wall out of whatever is lying about, the interface arrived.

⁂

It did not announce itself. It came the way a floater comes into your eye, at the edge, and then it was simply there and had, apparently, always been there, laid over the world about eighteen inches in front of your face and moving when you moved.

You made a sound. It was not a word.

You have thought since about what happened in those seconds, and the honest answer is that you were nine years deep in a habit and the habit came for you. A man drowning grabs what floats. Your hands went out flat on the warm stone and your mind, which had absolutely nothing left with which to understand a purple tree or a fountain running backwards or two hundred strangers in identical neckwear, reached back into the only place it had ever been given a world with a legend, and it *built one*, right there, out of nothing but need and muscle memory, and it hung it in front of your eyes where the panic was.

You put your hand up to your face and it came away red, and that steadied you, which tells you something about how bad the alternative was. Blood was a fact. Blood was tractable. You pinched the bridge of your nose the way you had been taught at eleven and tipped your head forward and breathed, and the bar labelled COMPOSURE crept from one segment to two, and you watched it do it, and you knew — you *knew*, you were never once confused about this, not that morning and not later — that nothing was measuring you. There was no scanner. There was no system. There was a man on his knees in a foreign market having the worst minute of his life, and there was his own frightened brain drawing him a picture in the only handwriting it had.

It helped anyway. That was the thing you could never explain to anybody afterwards, including to yourself. It should not have helped and it helped enormously.

You looked up at the nearest sign, over a stall four metres away with a hot cabinet in it and a queue. The letters were letters. That was the horror of it, and you had not expected the horror to come from that direction. They were confidently, obviously letters — an alphabet, with repeating forms and clear word breaks and something that was plainly a price with plainly a number in it — and they did not resolve. You were a literate adult in a public place, surrounded by writing, and you could not extract one single fact from any of it. Not a name. Not a digit. Not a warning. You looked at four signs in a row and got nothing off any of them, and you understood, in a way that arrived like cold water in the small of your back, that you had been deleted from the entire apparatus of being told things.

The two men nearest to you were talking, three feet away, with their backs half turned. It was ordinary talk. It had the exact shape of two colleagues disagreeing mildly about a delivery: a long patient sentence, an interruption, a snort, a short flat run of syllables with a shrug in it. Consonant-heavy, liquid in the middle, with a rise at the end of a clause that you would have staked money was a question. You listened to fifteen seconds of it with the total attention of a man listening for a rescue party and it stayed sound. Not once did it come apart into parts. It was a wall with a very good texture.

Then the interface did the thing that told you exactly what it was.

That was the company's template. Field for field, in order, with the same stupid mandatory sub-category that nobody ever filled in and the same *Escalation path* line that existed because a consultant had put it there in 2019. You had looked at that form perhaps forty thousand times. Your mind had reached for a container to put an unsurvivable fact into, and it had reached past nine years of dragons and grabbed the ticketing system, because the ticketing system was what you actually knew, and because on some level below language you had already decided that this was not an adventure. It was an outage. It was the biggest outage there had ever been and you were the only person on shift.

*Assigned to: you.* You read that line about six times.

⁂

You got up. It took two goes. Nobody had touched you yet, though a fair number of people had looked — you were a man in a soaked black overcoat kneeling in a fountain plaza with a bloody nose, which will get you looked at in any city on any world, and the looks you were getting were curious rather than alarmed, which you did not have the resources to find reassuring.

You went through your pockets, because it was a procedure and procedures were the only currency you had left.

The phone was the one that did it. Not the road, not the fountain, not the collars. You held it up and turned in a slow circle in the middle of that plaza with your arm above your head — and it was the same gesture, wasn't it, the exact same arm-in-the-air of a man asking a ceiling to notice him — and there was no signal, and there was no network to have no signal on, and the little spinning icon went round and round looking for something that was not going to be there in a thousand years.

Your knees went. You sat down on the bottom step, hard, and put the phone face down on the stone beside you, and the interface, unhelpful to the last, produced one more panel, unprompted, and this one you had not asked for at all.

Somebody crouched down in front of you.

He had come out from behind a counter across the plaza — you registered it afterwards, the little six-stool frontage with the cabinet and the two hanging lamps, the shutter half up — and he had come at a fast walk without hurrying, the way people move towards someone they have decided is going to fall over. He got down on his heels in front of you with his forearms across his knees, an arm's length away, not closer, and he did not touch you.

He was about your age, maybe younger. Twenty-eight, twenty-nine. Tall like the rest of them but built narrow with it, so it read as height rather than size. Dark curling hair pushed back and not recently cut. An apron over a plain shirt with the sleeves shoved up, and his forearms and the heels of his hands were dusted with something pale and there was a smear of something orange-red near his elbow, and he smelled overwhelmingly of frying oil and hot spice and, under it, of the same four-way argument of coffee that hung over the whole square. There was a metal band at his throat, plain, unornamented, brass gone dull with wear at the front where a thumb had gone over it ten thousand times.

He had one of those faces that is doing several things at once. Mostly it was doing concern. But there was something else in it — an intent, forward, *interested* quality, entirely inappropriate to the situation, the look of a man who has found something on a shelf that should not be on that shelf.

He spoke.

It was three or four words, gentle, level, pitched low under the noise of the fountain, ending on that rise you had learned to recognise in the last four minutes. A question. Almost certainly the question — the one anybody asks a stranger who is sitting on the ground bleeding, in any language, on any world: *are you all right?* You could hear the shape of it. You could hear the kindness in it, which travels, which is apparently the one thing that travels.

And there was nothing. Not a syllable, not a root, not a cognate, not one handhold anywhere on the whole smooth face of it.

You opened your mouth and said, "I don't know where I am." Your own voice sounded appalling, wrecked and thin and much younger than thirty-one. "I'm sorry. I don't — I don't know where I am. I don't know what's happened. Please."

He went very still.

You saw it land: not the meaning, he did not have the meaning any more than you had his, but *something*. His eyes went sharp and his head came fractionally back and he looked at your mouth, and then at your coat, and then at the sodden useless lanyard hanging out of your fist — and he stopped.

He looked at the lanyard for a long moment. At the laminated card with the company logo and a photograph of you taken four years ago in front of a grey curtain and, printed under it in eleven-point sans serif, your name and your job title and a barcode.

You watched a thing happen to his face that you did not have a category for. It was not fear and it was not pity. It was the expression of a man reading a word he has only ever seen in books.

He said something else. Not to you. He half turned his head and said it to the plaza in general, sharply, and two of the men on the steps got up. Then he turned back and looked at you and put his hand out, palm up, open, in the air between you — not taking, offering — and waited, and did not say anything at all, and let you decide.

The fountain went on running upward behind him. The lights of the walkways went on being three storeys over your head. Two hundred and sixty-one strangers went on with a Tuesday you could not read, in a city you could not name, on a world you had no reason to believe was the one you were born on.

You put your hand in his. His fingers closed round yours, warm and floury and absolutely solid, and you found you were shaking so hard that he had to take your weight with his other hand under your arm to get you up.

And in the corner of your vision, unasked, in the only handwriting your mind had left:

```
  OBJECTIVES

  ▸ Do not die.
  ▸ Learn one word.
```
