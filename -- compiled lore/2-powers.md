# Beyond the Seven: Minor Powers and Independents

The seven great powers govern, between them, perhaps half of humanity. The other half lives in the three to four thousand polities that never make the digest: regional powers of nine systems and vast self-regard, single worlds with a thousand years of their own history, leagues and communions and combines that are, to their citizens, simply the world. What follows is ten, chosen for range rather than importance.

## The Khiva Ascendancy

A major regional power of nine systems on the Golden Circuit's coreward leg. Wealthy, ancient, and magnificently unembarrassed, the Ascendancy has spent six centuries describing itself as the eighth great power, and the actual great powers have long since stopped correcting it, Khiva sitting on too profitable a stretch of the Circuit to be worth offending. A hereditary Ascendant rules through a council of trade families. The fleet is real, the manners are exquisite, and the diplomatic style is that of a middleweight who has never once been made to feel like one.

The trade families are the coreward Circuit's deepest pocket, and finance frontier ventures the great powers will not look at. They were the largest single bloc in the Vega reconstruction syndicate, and the Andromedan Banking War was settled before a tribunal seated at Khiva—chosen because it was acceptable to everyone and convenient to nobody, and accepted by the Ascendancy with a graciousness it has not stopped displaying since.

Perhaps fifty polities occupy Khiva's tier, and every one of them has its own view of the rankings.

## The Ellorine Communion

A devout dozen worlds rimward of the Mandate's quiet flank. Their faith, the Unbroken Voyage, grew out of generation-ship liturgy in the early Scattering: the conviction that the diaspora itself is a sacrament, humanity's crossing from one shore to another not yet revealed, and that every settled world is a station of that passage. Twelve worlds hold it as the air, pilgrim-ships work the nearby routes, and the theology is old, subtle and argued across a dozen schools. Corridor compilations note it as insular regional customs.

## The Reyes Trust

Neutral banking and arbitration in the rimward deeps. A family house—part bank, part arbitration court, part hereditary vocation—that has served a rimward volume of some three hundred settled systems for six centuries. The Trust holds stakes, keeps secrets, and rules on disputes for polities that will never route a transaction through the Mas system, and it is the Sefkir Reach's banker, the far Long Haul's lender of first resort, and one of the dozen houses the Sable Cartel banks with and is watched by.

There are perhaps a dozen such houses across human space. None is large, none can see what the others are doing, and the trade is old enough that each has a founding story in which it invented the business. The Trust and the Arrhene Consortium keep a modest correspondent relationship and a mutual, carefully unstated conviction that the other is the derivative one.

## The Chandrasar Weave

Spinward of the Mandate's volume, Trade Tongue is the third language. The Weave—a trade pidgin that grew a literature and became a tongue—binds a loose commonwealth of some two hundred systems that trade with one another far more than with anyone else. It has its own courier lines, its own catalogues and its own classics. Corridor traders working the volume hire Weave-speaking factors and complain about it, and a child raised in the Weave can live a full and cosmopolitan life without ever needing Trade Tongue.

## Ìlú-Oba, the Broadcast Kingdoms

A prosperous cluster trailing-rimward of the Sol–Vega neighbourhood, whose serials, music and festival cycles dominate their own volume so thoroughly that Vega product runs as a niche import. The great houses of Ìlú-Oba entertainment are dynastic, flamboyant, and periodically at open creative war with one another, which is half the content. Their stars mean nothing on Lyra and everything across forty systems. Vega's commercial attachés file patient reports about market penetration, which has been imminent for a century.

## Resheph, and the cousins

Arrhenos is the largest and richest society to have rearranged gender, not the only one. Resheph is the eldest of the cousins: an all-female world of some ninety million, founded a generation before Arrhenos by a movement with different reasons and a quieter temperament, reproducing by ovum fusion and interested in nobody's opinion. It trades little, travels less, and maintains with Arrhenos the cousinly correspondence—two centuries of letters between scholars comparing notes on lives the rest of the galaxy finds remarkable and they do not.

Beyond Resheph the family scatters small: post-gender collectives in the Freehold arc, a five-gender society in the Chandrasar Weave, single-sex colonies that stayed a station's size or failed. None is famous.

## The Veskarn Combine

Six systems owned and operated by a corporation that, unlike the Cartel, has a headquarters, publishes accounts, and will tell you exactly what it is. Every citizen holds shares, the franchise is the shareholder register, and politics is dividend policy, conducted in general meetings of forty billion voting proxies. Share concentration has produced its own quiet aristocracy and the annual meeting is as theatrical as any coronation, and its citizens defend the arrangement with the particular firmness of people tired of being told they live in a business. The Cartel is rumoured to hold a position in the Combine; the Combine publishes its register and dares anyone to find it.

## Cold Harbour

Burned three centuries ago by a corridor currency collapse that wiped out a generation's savings, the Cold Harbour system trades in kind, in labour-credit, and in its own dour scrip, and honours the quid nowhere. Corridor merchants must barter or bank elsewhere, and mostly stop coming, which suits Cold Harbour. The system runs a deliberately modest economy, self-sufficient in essentials, and regards monetary sovereignty as its founding grudge and chief civic virtue.

## The Amaranth League

Enhancement without the Lattice. The League's founders were Neo-Solar citizens who wanted the long life, the morphologies and the medicine, and could not bear the shared mind—or, as League historians prefer, chose the self. Three systems trailing of Sol, technically superb, culturally intense, and permanently arguing with the Republic in the way only family can, each regarding the other as the experiment that lost its nerve. It is the largest of many NSR splinters.

## Pell's Landing

A poor world at the far end of a Long Haul spoke: one city, one clinic, a resupply schedule measured in waits. The corridor's two-hundred-year lifespan is a rumour here—the medicine exists, the supply and the specialists do not—and a Pell's native who reaches ninety has done well. The world is not a tragedy to itself; it farms, marries, argues, and buries its dead by lamplight the way settled people always have, and its emigrants' letters home from the corridors read like dispatches from a heaven with an entry fee.

# The Lesser Havens

Two havens documented briefly, one from the working majority and one from the floor. There are hundreds of thousands of others, and every one would quarrel with being represented by these.

## Haven Reliant

A jump-capable Trade Haven of around 85,000 people, some 400 years old and rebuilt in sections, twice from serious damage. Its circuit is an eleven-stop loop through the rimward Long Haul spokes, about two years to a pass, selling medicine, technical repair, neutral ground, news, and a good week.

Reliant is a nine-hundred-metre rotating drum with four centuries of patches: a town that flies. Its circuit serves eleven thin-settled systems that see no other regular visitor, worlds where "the haven is coming" is a calendar event, a market day, a clinic day and the only reliable mail service at once. The surgery teams work double shifts the whole stop, the machine shops take in a season's broken equipment, and the Long Gallery becomes a market and a dance floor by turns.

The margins are thin and everyone aboard can recite them. Fuel and parts are bought at frontier prices, a slow season means deferred maintenance, and deferred maintenance means the section meetings get quiet and arithmetical. Reliant has sold pieces of itself twice in living memory—a shuttle, then the big fabricator—and bought them back, once. Its accounts sit with Nexus, because Nexus said yes and the Arrhene houses that hold richer havens' reserves have no branch on the rimward spokes. The debt is modest, watched, and never quite gone.

Semma Uld, 51, has been Reliant's dockmistress for nineteen years and its de facto foreign minister forever: the face eleven worlds associate with the Drift entire. She keeps a ledger of favours owed and owing across the whole circuit—births attended, engines lent, a harvest saved in 3854—that no auditor will ever see. "The money books say we're poor. The other book says eleven systems would fly out to pull us off a rock. Rich is having the second book."

## The Quiet Haven

A Refugee Haven, jump-capable barely, holding around 30,000 people who churn. The hull is old salvage of unknown age; the community is about 150 years old. It has had six names in a century and a half, and outsiders' name for it has stuck better than any of its own. It sells nothing and asks nothing.

It files no flight plan, and the Drift's network nodes pass word of its position the way one passes a house key. It takes in the people the galaxy is looking for or has thrown away: fled subjects with Bureau files, debtors two jumps ahead of a Nexus judgment, deserters, the disgraced, the simply erased. Its one law is the fourth Code kept at its hardest—entry refused only for threat, never for identity—and no questions that outrun the offered answers.

Life aboard is austere past anything Reliant knows: rationed water, salvaged everything, work assignments posted daily because the station survives on everyone's hands. It is not warm, exactly. It is level: a place where a Mandate registration band, a Vega debt notice and an Aegis contract all mean the same nothing, and where the question of what you were is answered, if at all, at night, voluntarily, in the one commons where the lights are kept low on purpose.

Some aboard heal and move on to the working fleet. Some stay and become the crew. A few are found even here, which is why the dock watch is the best-armed thing about the station.

The richer havens fund it discreetly, Ascendant among them, by a line in the Convocation budget labelled only *fleet obligations*.

## `powers/drift-communities/overview.md`

# The Drift Communities

Around 900 billion stateless people aboard mobile stations called havens, across a fleet nobody has ever counted, including the Drift. It is not a nation and not a culture. What its people share is an origin, a predicament and a stance.

The origin is the Cascade of 2756 C, the collapse that shattered the old order and set ships and station-crews adrift between dead authorities; many havens trace their independence to a vessel that simply never came home.

The predicament is that you cannot, aboard a moving station, make all your own fuel and parts and medicine.

The stance—the only thing that genuinely runs from the richest Grand Haven to the grimmest salvage hauler—is a practised, defiant joy: the conviction, hard-won against real hardship, that a life this fragile is exactly the kind worth celebrating.

## Not one people

Grand Havens are the postcard: jump-capable, self-sufficient for decades, centuries old, large and rich enough to choose their own course. They are rare. Haven Ascendant is one. Most Drift will never live aboard one and will visit only as paying guests.

Trade Havens are the working majority—nomadic stations selling medical, technical, entertainment, information-brokerage and neutral-ground services to far-flung worlds on a travelling circuit, often on margins thin enough that a bad season means a hard one. This is what most Drift life actually looks like.

Refugee Havens are the floor: sanctuaries for the lost, the disappeared and the debt-fleeing, offering anonymity and protection to people who ran clean out of any other door.

Beneath that, havens specialise—in salvage processing, in vice, in medicine, in information, in diplomatic neutrality, in cultural preservation. A Drifter born in a salvage hauler's recycling deck and a Drifter born in Ascendant's Core share a legal status, which is none, and a way of speaking about freedom, and almost no material reality at all. The gulf runs inside the great havens too: Ascendant's transient Outring holds more people than its archives, its halls and its promenade combined.

There is no government above the single haven. Each governs itself, by everything from elected convocations to a captain's word.

## Freedom is not free

Statelessness is the absence of a home market, and the difference is a bill that comes due every cycle. A haven cannot mine its own metals at scale, refine its own fuel indefinitely, or manufacture every part it will break. It must resupply, and the routes that reach the wandering Drift are not routes most carriers will fly.

The carrier that will is the Sable Cartel, whose logistics arm services Drift routes few others touch, and whose Nexus headquarters at Korsen's Anchorage is the nearest thing the Drift has to a central hub.

The bankers that will are the handful of houses that keep accounts for people with no country to vouch for them: the Reyes Trust out in the rimward deeps, the Khivan trade families along the coreward Circuit, a scattering of neutral houses of which the Arrhene are the best known, and a good many small firms that ask no questions and charge for the privilege.

Both relationships are fair, useful and quietly defining. A rich haven like Ascendant, sitting on centuries of reserves spread across a dozen institutions in six volumes of space, holds a long leash and barely feels it. A Trade Haven living job to job feels every inch of its short one.

## The hard-won joy

The Drift lives where everything is fleeting—worlds die, stations fail, records crumble—and its answer is a kind of disciplined celebration: if everything ends, then everything matters. The feast, the memory-keeping and the near-sacred duty of welcome have teeth, and a haven that will refuse entry to nobody for who they are will spend real blood defending the principle. Haven Ascendant has written the creed down most beautifully; the instinct is found on every deck, practised according to every deck's means.

## What the Drift is becoming

The one argument running fleet-wide is about the future of the body. Neo-Solar Republic enhancement—the Lattice, the morphologies, the long networked post-human life—has begun spreading into the Drift, and the havens are split by it. To some it is simply the next tool of survival, embraced without fuss. To others, and most sharply to the memory-keeping havens, it is identity erosion: a people whose whole purpose is preserving what humans were cannot easily watch its children decide to stop being that. No authority exists to settle the question. It will be settled haven by haven.

## `powers/drift-communities/haven-ascendant/index.md`

# Haven Ascendant

A jump-capable Grand Haven of around 4.2 million permanent residents, founded 2691 C, 23 kilometres along its primary axis and some 890 million tonnes. Its Captain-Archivist is Teshen Briar, in post since 3847 C. It specialises in archives, environmental reconstruction and entertainment, and speaks Trade Tongue with Ascendant Drift as its local tongue.

## Origins

The station began as the *Mnemosynë*, commissioned in 2691 C by the Terran Heritage Foundation to preserve Earth's cultures as humanity scattered. The Cascade of 2756 C destroyed the Foundation and the political order that funded it, leaving the *Mnemosynë* in transit between systems with no owner and no mission. The crew voted on whether to seek absorption into some planetary polity or carry on alone, and chose alone, 342 to 7.

What saved them was the cargo. The archives held centuries of Earth performance—music, theatre, dance, games, food—and the crew began selling heritage experiences to passing traders: a reconstructed meal, a recovered game, a play nobody had staged in a hundred years. The novelty drew visitors and the quality brought them back, and by 2800 C the renamed Haven Ascendant had locked its two halves into a single engine, entertainment revenue funding preservation and preserved material generating new entertainment.

The first environmental reconstruction, the Paulista District, opened in 2934 C after eleven years and very nearly bankrupted the Haven a second time. It also defined it. Others preserved records and others sold a good night out; nobody else let you walk through the past.

## The five zones

The Haven is an accretion—the original *Mnemosynë*, salvaged hulls, and purpose-built modules grafted on across eleven hundred years.

| The Core                | original *Mnemosynë*; administration and residence   | ~400,000  |
| The Stacks              | archive storage, research, data processing           | ~200,000  |
| The Reconstruction Halls| environmental recreations, immersive experience      | ~150,000  |
| The Promenade           | entertainment, hospitality, commerce                 | ~800,000  |
| The Outring             | docks, industry, transient housing                   | ~2.6 million |

The Outring—docks, recycling, fabrication, and the churning transient housing of dockworkers, contract crews and the just-arrived—holds nearly two in three of everyone aboard, and almost none of the fame.

Gravity sits at 0.85 G through the residential and entertainment sections, and drops to 0.4 G in the Stacks to spare the physical archives; some Reconstruction Halls retune it to match whatever they are recreating. Threading the whole length is the Axis, a zero-gravity transit corridor whose capsules cross between zones in minutes: public, free, perpetually crammed, and the one place every deck of the Haven physically meets.

# Haven Ascendant: Notable Characters

## Teshen Briar

Captain-Archivist since 3847 C; 94, and appears about fifty. A mild manner over a sophisticated political instinct. Briar spent three decades running the Cascade Collection—work that meant ceaseless negotiation with planetary governments, academics, and the touchy descendants of Declaration signatories—before the Convocation elected him to the chair.

He lives plainly: modest Core quarters, early hours, few indulgences, his real recreation the deliberative politics most people find a chore. Married forty years to Maren Osse, a Heritage Row restaurateur; no children, by choice. "The Haven is enough legacy."

## Vell Kiruna

Chief Reconstructor, 67. She has overseen every major reconstruction of the past four decades. Trained as an atmospheric engineer, she drifted into environmental design and found her vocation in the seam where the technical meets the cultural, getting the air, the light and the grief of a place all correct at once.

Borderline-obsessive, quick-tempered when standards slip, genuinely warm toward the source communities she works with, and immovable on one point: she will not inflate a confidence rating to please a patron. Her current project, the Abrantine Terraces—a Mandate pleasure complex destroyed during the Yansieve rebellion—has drawn objections that the Haven should not be preserving Mandate imperial culture while the rebellion still burns. Her answer is three words: the Haven preserves.

## Dace Velorum

Performer at the Hanging Market, 34. His grandmother survived the destruction of Covenant Station and his mother was born on Ascendant and raised him inside the survivor community's traditions. He has worked the memorial since adolescence—runner, then vendor, now senior performer—and his speciality is a single textile seller, documented in survivor testimony, whom he inhabits with a precision visitors find difficult to sit with.

Off the Market he is quiet to the point of awkwardness, more at ease in a reconstructed dead man's life than his own, and has shown no flicker of interest in leaving the survivor cluster he was raised in.

## Osanne Drey

Proprietor of the Spiral, 112. Sixty years running Fortune's Reach's most famous venue, after a career as a professional gambler in which she won the Spiral three times, a record, and used the winnings to buy out the previous owner.

She no longer plays for money—"the house always wins, and I am the house"—but watches every game with an attention players find either reassuring or unbearable, and claims to remember every significant hand she has ever seen. Her rules are three: fair games, prompt payment, no violence. Cheats are banned and entered in a registry shared among gambling houses across human space.

## Menor Tal

Confession archivist, 41. Eighteen years in a Promenade confession booth, roughly forty thousand confessions heard, and not one ever disclosed. The work demands a specific temperament: to receive anything—crimes and cruelties and secrets that could level lives—and answer with nothing but professional courtesy and the terms of the seal.

Off duty he is the most gregarious man on his deck: a Tournament Ground regular, an enthusiastic amateur cook, a wide circle of friends. He does not discuss the work, and his friends have learned not to ask.

## Tamsin Ord

Outring dock-gang chief, 58. She has been aboard for thirty-one years and is still, on the paperwork, a transient—one of the Outring's vast population of the long-term temporary, who keep the Haven fuelled, loaded, patched and breathing, and hold no Convocation seat for the doing of it.

Ord runs a docking gang of two hundred, with rather less margin for error than the Core allows: a botched seal in the Outring kills people. She has applied for residency twice and let it lapse both times, less from bitterness than from a clear-eyed sense that the waiting list is built for people with steadier berths than hers. What she does instead is throw the best Feast on the dock decks. "The Haven welcomes everyone. It just remembers some of us last. I can live with being remembered last. I'd rather that than not be aboard."

# Haven Ascendant: Preservation

The Haven's twin pillars: the Archives, which hold the record, and the Reconstruction Halls, which let you walk into it.

## The Archives

The holdings span pre-Scattering material from the original *Mnemosynë* collection—physical artefacts, digital records, genetic samples, sensory recordings; Cascade documentation from the 847 worlds that declared independence in 2756 C, much of it preserved nowhere else; the archives of lost worlds since failed, destroyed or gone dark; Drift chronicles, oral histories and logs; and the deposits entrusted by individuals, families, corporations and governments who wanted something kept.

In total: 2.4 exabytes of digital material, 40 million physical artefacts, and 847 environmental reconstructions on file.

| Public     | general history, entertainment archives                | free                    |
| Registered | academic materials, detailed cultural records          | nominal fee             |
| Restricted | sensitive deposits, legal records, identity archives   | negotiated              |
| Sealed     | materials held in trust under specific conditions      | by terms of deposit     |

The Haven's entire value rests on one boast it has never broken: sealed material stays sealed. Deposits have waited centuries for their trigger conditions and none has been opened early.

## The Chronicle Service

For a fee, the Haven will document anything—a birth, a death, an agreement, a confession, a secret—under whatever access restrictions the depositor names.

For the stateless it is closer to a lifeline than a clerical sideline. A Drift-born child entered in the Haven's record has proof of existence that no coup, no border closure and no failed colony can erase, and a refugee can lodge testimony that may matter long after the people who would suppress it are gone.

## The Reconstruction Halls

A reconstruction is not an exhibit but an environment: a volume of the station rebuilt, as exactly as the records allow, into a place that existed elsewhere or elsewhen. You enter it, breathe air scented as the original was scented, walk surfaces textured as the original was textured, and meet performers trained to inhabit it as its first people did.

| The Paulista District    | São Paulo, Earth                        | 22nd century        | 0.8 km² urban streetscape        |
| The Hanging Market       | Covenant Station, destroyed 2891 C      | pre-destruction     | 0.3 km² market complex           |
| Firstfall                | the Cascade declaration site            | 2756 C              | a single plaza, a single moment  |
| The Yennara Coast        | Yennara, a failed colony                | 2845–2847 C         | 1.2 km coastline, seasonal cycles|
| The Jade Emperor's Court | a reconstruction of a reconstruction    | Tang Dynasty aesthetic | 0.4 km² palace complex        |
| Memorial Corridor        | composite                               | various             | a processional of destroyed places|

Firstfall holds eighteen minutes of the morning of 3.2/1, 2756 C: the crowd gathering in Covenant Station's plaza, the rising tension, the reading of the Vandemeyer Declaration that triggered the Cascade, the first cheers, and then the loop resets.

The Paulista District, the oldest, recreates an ordinary São Paulo neighbourhood nobody important ever came from, documented only because one Foundation researcher grew up there and recorded every detail before he left. Unlike Firstfall it lives: weather, day and night, seasons, and performers who age in their roles and are replaced and age again.

The Hanging Market is a memorial rather than an entertainment, rebuilding a Trade Haven destroyed in 2891 C in a piracy incident that ran past anyone's intent. Its performers are descendants of the survivors, its admission is free, and a visit is understood as an act of witness.

## Confidence ratings

| Verified     | extensive primary sources; high fidelity; citable in scholarship |
| Interpolated | solid framework with reasoned extrapolation                      |
| Evocative    | limited sources; captures the spirit rather than the detail      |
| Speculative  | minimal documentation; labelled plainly as interpretation        |

A reconstruction runs from source research through environmental design, fabrication, sensory calibration and cultural preparation to external academic verification. It takes five to fifteen years and hundreds of millions of quid, funded by diaspora communities, academic institutions, wealthy patrons, reinvested entertainment revenue, and the occasional planetary government.

The Haven would rather mark a hall Speculative and disappoint a paying patron than let a guess pass as a memory.

## The schism

A haven built to preserve what humanity was sits uneasily with a Drift increasingly tempted to become something else. The spread of Neo-Solar enhancement lands hardest here of anywhere in the fleet, because Ascendant's identity is the keeping of the human record, and you cannot easily archive a way of being while your own children vote to leave it behind. The Haven has taken no official position, and the argument runs through the Stacks and the Convocation in lowered voices.

# Haven Ascendant: The Promenade

Eight kilometres of entertainment wrapped around the station's midsection, and it never closes.

## Districts

| The Memory Theatre | drama, music, dance, immersive narrative              |
| Fortune's Reach    | gambling: cards, dice, prediction markets, stranger games |
| The Scarlet Axis   | sex work, companionship, intimate performance         |
| Heritage Row       | cuisine from preserved traditions; historical dining  |
| The Vapour Quarter | intoxicants, consciousness alteration                 |
| The Night Market   | commerce, from art to contraband                      |
| Tournament Ground  | competitive gaming, physical and mental               |
| The Spoken Word    | storytelling, confession, rumour                      |

The Haven sits outside every planetary jurisdiction, so the only prohibitions are the Core Codes': no harm to the unwilling, no slavery or debt bondage, no sexual contact with minors, no weapons that could threaten the station, no fraud.

The prohibition on debt bondage is the line that separates the Scarlet Axis from the trade it resembles elsewhere. Its workers are workers, not property and not collateral, and the distinction is enforced.

## Who keeps it open

The Promenade's roughly 800,000 residents are the visible workforce—performers, dealers, cooks, companions, storytellers—and behind them stand the Outring crews who supply, clean, repair and restock a district that runs twenty hours a day forever.

The hard-won joy is not the property of the top deck. The Outring throws its own Feasts, the dealers and cooks have their own after-shift rituals, and the conviction that a fragile life is worth celebrating is held hardest by the people with the least margin.

## Notable venues

Fortune's Reach is a 400-metre cylindrical module spun for gradient gravity—near-standard at the outer wall, zero-G at the centre—with games clustered by the weight their players prefer. Its legend is the Spiral, a helix winding from the outer ring to the core, each level lighter and stranger than the last. Completing it at a profit is rare.

The Spoken Word is where the Haven's archive and its nightlife fuse. The Chronicle Halls record oral histories straight into the archives; the Telling Houses stage storytellers—history, fiction, or the blend the Drift loves best—in rooms from twenty seats to thousands; and the Rumour Exchange is a working bourse for unverified information, priced by collective guess, on which the Haven officially disclaims all responsibility.

The Confession Booths are scattered through the Promenade behind plain white doors: small chambers where, for a fee, a person confesses anything to an archivist who records it, seals it and preserves it, never accessed and never used. Some specify unsealing after death; most choose permanent silence. Inside is a chair, a recorder, and an archivist who listens without expression and speaks only to confirm the terms.

# Haven Ascendant: Society

## Governance

The Captain-Archivist holds executive authority and scholarly leadership at once: elected for life by the Convocation and removable by supermajority, in command of the Haven's course and its external face, and the arbiter of its disputes. The office is barred from unilaterally altering the Core Codes or opening sealed archives.

The Convocation, of around 1,200 members, is the deliberative body: guild delegates at sixty per cent, residential delegates at thirty, and elder seats by seniority at ten. It debates policy, approves major spending, and elects or removes the Captain-Archivist. Sessions are public and frequently packed.

The seats are apportioned to guilds and residents, and the Outring's vast transient population—dockworkers on short contracts, crews between berths, the newly arrived—are mostly neither, and so are mostly unheard.

## The Core Codes

The foundational principles, amendable only by supermajority.

1. The Haven shall preserve.
2. The Haven shall remember: no deliberate forgetting.
3. The Haven shall move: remain jump-capable and independent.
4. The Haven shall welcome: entry refused only for threat, never for identity.
5. The Haven shall celebrate: joy is not frivolity but purpose. Added 2934 C, over scholarly objection.

Each has a price the Haven has chosen to pay. Independence means the constant cost of staying mobile and resupplied, and welcome has been defended, on hard occasions, with blood, because a sanctuary that turns nobody away for who they are will sometimes have to fight the people who came hunting them.

## Daily rhythm

A loosely kept twenty-hour cycle, synchronised to the C calendar and overruled in practice by the festival calendar, with major celebrations every few weeks and minor ones constant.

A representative day: breakfast in a Heritage Row room serving reconstructed twenty-third-century Lunar cuisine, a shift cataloguing in the Stacks, a Memory Theatre performance, dinner and drink with friends, a late hour at the Tournament Ground. For the Outring the same day has more shift in it and less theatre, but the festivals reach every deck and the Axis runs free.

## Language and dress

Trade Tongue is the Promenade default and the language of the wider Drift. Ascendant Drift, the native tongue, has evolved from it over centuries: thick with archival and preservation vocabulary, rich in words for memory and loss, and grammatically insistent on distinguishing the documented from the merely remembered. Most Haven-born are bilingual, and the Stacks keep specialists in over two hundred languages.

Dress runs to no uniform. Archivists favour the durable and muted, Promenade workers dress for their venues, and residents layer for the shifting temperatures and lean, given the choice, toward colour.

## Gap 2: Assessment and Feast

Every section reports its status—food, water, atmosphere, fuel, systems, population. It began in the desperate early decades as bare practicality and hardened into rite.

At midday the Captain-Archivist reads the Assessment summary from the Axis, broadcast through the whole station. Even the Promenade goes quiet. The message never changes: this is what we have, this is who we are, and we continue.

The evening scatters into thousands of simultaneous gatherings—every residential section, guild and Promenade district laying out its own food and drink, people moving between them along a free-running Axis until it all burns out somewhere past the next nominal dawn.

First we know what we have; then we celebrate it.

# Visiting Haven Ascendant

## What the Haven runs on

For all its wealth and its eleven centuries, Ascendant cannot make everything it needs aboard: fuel, heavy parts, bulk materials, and the routine resupply of a twenty-three-kilometre city in motion.

The carriers willing to service a wandering Grand Haven on its own schedule are few, and the most reliable is the Sable Cartel, whose logistics arm flies Drift routes others will not and whose hub at Korsen's Anchorage is what Ascendant, like most of the fleet, plans its circuits around.

The Haven keeps its reserves—centuries of accumulated entertainment revenue—spread across a dozen neutral houses in six volumes of space, under the location-independent asset protection such houses extend to the stateless. The spreading is the point: no single failure, seizure or change of regime can reach more than a fraction of it, and those reserves are the real foundation of Ascendant's independence.

## Clients

Governments and wealthy individuals across human space commission the Haven's services: sensitive deposits, Chronicle work, reconstruction commissions, the verification of historical claims. The Haven takes the work regardless of the client's politics, subject only to the Core Codes. Its formal academic partners are led by the Tiamming Imperial Archives and the catalogue houses of the Chandrasar Weave.

The Mandate is the delicate case. Ascendant preserves imperial history exhaustively, including a great deal the Empire would prefer forgotten, and the Mandate has never dared apply direct pressure, though imperial citizens who visit sometimes report a quiet, informal surveillance. The Yansieve rebellion has sharpened all of it: the Haven documents both sides faithfully, which is exactly what neither side wants.

## Finding the Haven

Ascendant jumps between systems on its own schedule, with trade, politics and the Captain-Archivist's judgement all factoring in. Its current position is broadcast on standard Trade frequencies and updated at the major Drift network nodes, and a typical stay in any system runs three to eight months.

## Docking and residency

Arrival is through the Outring, where over two hundred berths handle the traffic. Customs are minimal—a weapons check, a disease screen, an identity recording—and a Haven pass then grants the run of the public decks.

Permanent residency requires Convocation approval: sponsorship by current residents, a demonstrated contribution to the Haven's functions, and a waiting period measured in years. This is why the Outring stays full of people who have been aboard for decades and remain, officially, passing through. The Code that throws the door wide sits beside a membership process built for the settled.

## What to expect

First-time visitors tend to remark on the same things: the density, the Promenade especially; the sensory overload of layered smell and sound and colour; the casualness about matters other societies regulate with terror; the real warmth under the commercial surface; the speed at which a stranger becomes a friend; and the particular vertigo, in the reconstructions, of walking through a death and out the other side.

# Elysian Collective

A terraforming guild of around 15 million sworn members, and some 40 million in all once the Tenders, families, and the civilian population of the Garden and the project camps are counted. Founded in 3089 C as the loose Terraforming Collective; the guild Charter it dates itself from was adopted in 3261 C. Its centre is the Garden, a mobile deep-space station whose location is kept secret.

Terraforming is older than the guild. Mars breathed before the Collective existed, and human space is dotted with sealed domes, thickened atmospheres and half-tamed worlds worked by local outfits, licensed journeymen, and methods that leaked out of the Academy generations ago. What nobody else can do is the whole art: take a dead world all the way to open sky and have it stay taken. Nearly every world in that class came from Collective hands, and the handful of exceptions are studied at the Academy as cautionary tales.

The guild does not govern worlds. It makes the finest of them and hands them to whoever paid.

## The Great Work

Members speak of a world in transformation as a becoming, and of the terraformer as a midwife rather than an engineer—language that is half genuine conviction and half the story a monopoly tells to dignify its prices. Both halves are sincerely held.

A polity that wants a dome or a breathable valley has options. A polity that wants a new garden world needs the Collective.

## The sworn and the Tenders

The sworn are the guild proper: the terraformers who pass the Academy, climb from Apprentice to Master, lead projects, compose the world-songs, hold the right to name a finished world, and elect the Council.

The Tenders are the rest of the working Collective: associate members who run and mend the machines, hold the seed-lines, walk the hazardous ground, and do the ten thousand practical jobs that turning a planet actually takes. They are bound by the guild's rules and fed from its treasury, and many are proud of the Work. They hold no guild rank, do not vote in Conclave, rarely earn a berth on the Garden, and have only lately begun to appear in the world-songs at all. The machines do much of the brute labour; the Tenders do the part the machines cannot be trusted with, which is also, often, the part that kills you.

The founding charter speaks of a commonwealth of the Great Work, and members mean the word. Six centuries have sorted that commonwealth into a guild and the guild into ranks.

The Collective keeps thirty to fifty projects active at once, from century-long programmes to short assessments.

## `powers/elysian-collective/culture.md`

# Elysian Collective: Culture

Under the Collective's charter run at least three different lives: the sworn who design the worlds, the Tenders who build them, and the civilians who keep the Garden and the camps running.

## One identity, unevenly worn

Members are taught to be Collective first. National, ethnic and political origins are acknowledged and then set aside at the Academy gate, and a terraformer born on Tiamming and trained on the Garden will name herself Collective before she names herself Mandate. A project team drawn from a dozen feuding polities cannot work if it carries their quarrels.

It sits less evenly on the Tenders. A Tender is Collective on the pay-ledger and in the rules he keeps, and the identity offered to him is thinner: he will not be named in the song, will not vote for the Grandmasters, and will likely never see the Garden he is told is home. Many take the pride anyway. Others hold the Collective at the wary distance a guildless labourer has always kept from the guild, and keep truer loyalties—to a home world, a family line, a god the secular guild does not acknowledge—quietly alive.

## Patience

The cardinal virtue. Atmospheres turn over in decades and ecosystems settle across centuries, so a terraformer learns to work toward a result she will not live to see, and to find that bearable. Selection for the Academy weighs the temperament as heavily as any technical aptitude.

For a Master, the long horizon ends in a named world and a berth in paradise, and the wait is an investment. For a Tender on a thirty-year hazard posting the same patience is simply the rest of his life, spent in a dome on a poisoned world.

## Three lives

The sworn have a career of long apprenticeship, then decades of project work in small expert teams, then—for the gifted and the fortunate—mastery, a world of one's own to lead, and at last a berth on the Garden. They are highly paid, highly trained and genuinely devoted, and their work is intellectually enormous.

The Tenders run the seed-lines and the processor arrays, walk the contaminated ground in suits, operate and repair the machines that do the heaviest work, and improvise the thousand fixes no protocol covers. Theirs is the hazardous Collective: the burns, the lung-rot, and the accidents the world-songs used to leave out. They are organised in their own grades, gang to crew to overseer, with pay and pride attached. A Tender's child who tests brilliant can be taken up into the Academy and made sworn; it happens, and the cases are cherished as proof the door exists. Most Tender children become Tenders.

The civilians are the third Collective. The Garden alone is a city of three million, and a city needs teachers and medics and cooks, station engineers and pilots, archivists and brewers, the keepers who tend the great biodome, the musicians who carry the world-songs, and the clerks and negotiators without whom a monopoly cannot actually sell anything. Most were born to the Collective and never sat the Academy, or sat it and were turned away.

## The failed Academy

The Collective recruits the gifted from everywhere and breeds the gifted besides, so it is forever producing children who grow up inside the guild and do not make terraformer. The Academy turns away far more than it keeps. Some of the turned-away become Tenders, some take up the Garden's civilian trades, and some leave for the wider galaxy carrying a Collective childhood and no guild standing. A few never forgive it.

## World-songs

Every world the Collective has made carries a world-song: a long narrative poem, composed by the project team across the decades of the work, at once technical record, mythology and memorial. It encodes the turning-points of the terraforming, dramatises the struggle of ingenuity against a dead world, and names the dead.

Whose names is the question. By old custom the song named the sworn and only the sworn, and the Tenders who died installing the processors the song celebrates went into the ground unsung—a silence the guild explained, when it explained it at all, as a matter of authorship. Within the last two generations that custom has begun, unevenly and against resistance, to break. Some lead terraformers now insist the Tender dead be named in full, some Conclaves have rung with the argument, and a handful of recent world-songs carry Tender names beside the Masters'.

Every world-song is held in the Archive aboard the Garden. The oldest have been performed for six centuries, evolving through each generation of performers, who add their interpretive layers while keeping the core intact.

## The Naming

When a world reaches Terrene classification—breathable air, a self-sustaining ecosystem, no equipment needed to live—the lead terraformer chooses its permanent name, and the world bears it on every chart thereafter. The ceremony is held on the new ground itself: the team assembles, the world-song is performed entire, and the name is spoken. The right to it belongs solely to whoever led the work, always, to date, one of the sworn.

Names are personal—some descriptive, some memorial, some lifted from Earth's old mythologies, some invented on the spot. The guild neither reviews nor approves them, which has given human space some of its loveliest place-names and a fair number of its strangest.

## Daily life on a project

A project site is closer to a frontier town that happens to be making the ground it stands on: a core of sworn terraformers, a far larger body of Tenders, and the camp that grows around any place where thousands of people work for thirty years—cooks and medics and machine-shops, schooling for the children born on-site, a commissary, a grievance or two looking for somewhere to go. The hostile world outside the domes sets the rhythm, suits or sealed structures are the rule, and the work is technical, exhausting and now and then lethal.

Evenings, where the shifts allow, are communal: shared meals, talk, skill passed hand to hand, the slow accretion of the world-song. The bonds are real and deep. They are also stratified—the sworn mess and the Tender mess are frequently not the same mess—and a project's social weather is set by how gracefully its lead terraformer carries the distance. Teams run with great autonomy, and the Council steps in only when asked, or when a review smells trouble.

## Outsiders

The Collective meets the rest of human space through contract talks, project liaison and the Conclave, politely and at arm's length. Visitors to the Garden are honoured guests, visitors to project sites are tolerated and watched, and visitors who ask too closely after Genesis methods are redirected with a smile.

# Elysian Collective: Governance

The Collective governs itself the way it terraforms: slowly, by consensus, with a deep institutional preference for deferring any decision that cannot be made comfortably.

## The Guild Council

Seven Grandmasters govern the sworn. Each holds a major discipline, and together they set priorities, allocate the treasury, accept or refuse projects, and settle what the Conclave cannot.

| First   | Atmospheric Engineering            | Lis Valtonen       |
| Second  | Biological Systems                 | Okafor Dembe       |
| Third   | Geological Modification            | Renn Castellari    |
| Fourth  | Hydrological Engineering           | Saya Miren-Goh     |
| Fifth   | Climatological Management          | Torben Axelkraft   |
| Sixth   | Gravitational Systems              | Prem Danava        |
| Seventh | Reclamation, the Lazarus Project   | Maret Solveig      |

Grandmasters are elected by the Masters of their discipline by ranked-choice vote. A candidate must have carried at least three major project cycles and hold the endorsement of twelve sitting Masters. Terms run twenty years, renewable once. Most Grandmasters spend decades in the field before they ascend, and arrive at governance reluctantly, treating it as a duty stolen from real work.

The Council works by consensus on anything strategic. Dissent is spoken and recorded, and what cannot reach consensus is deferred.

The seven no longer think quite alike. Valtonen of the First, senior and cautious, holds that the guild's neutrality and the guild's hierarchy are what have kept it alive for six centuries and should be disturbed as little as possible. Solveig of the Seventh, whose Lazarus crews are more than half Tenders and who has buried more of them than anyone else at the table, presses for reform—of the world-songs first and the franchise in time. The rest fall between, by temperament and by what their disciplines need.

## The Conclave

Once a year the working guild gathers aboard the Garden: part parliament, part professional congress, part festival. For members scattered across isolated sites it is the one time much of the Collective stands in one place.

Active projects report to the assembled guild, where peer scrutiny is unsparing, failure dissected in the open and success sung. The Council lays out proposed budgets and priorities, the assembled Masters vote, and the Council honours the vote. Conflicts between projects and between members are heard and ruled on. Research, technique and argument are traded across disciplines.

The Conclave is a gathering of the sworn. Masters vote and Journeymen attend; the Tenders who make up the larger half of the working Collective send no delegate and cast no vote. A growing number of the sworn find this indefensible.

The Conclave opens with the Recitation of Worlds: a spoken naming of every world the Collective has made habitable, from the first to the most recent reclassification. It presently takes four hours to recite in full, and the guild hears every name standing.

## Neutrality

The founding charter makes political neutrality an inviolable principle. The Collective terraforms for whoever can pay, without regard to the client's politics, ideology or conduct, and has built worlds for the Mandate and for Freeholders, for Vega houses and for independent polities.

Neutrality means no power need fear the Collective as a rival, and every power can hire it without seeming to bless its enemies. Three pressures keep testing it.

The Mandate's rebel systems have asked the Collective to terraform for them. To accept is revenue, and a clean demonstration that the guild truly serves all comers; to refuse is to placate the largest client the Collective has. The Council has deferred the decision so often that the deferral has become the policy, and both Yansieve and Tiamming have learned to read it.

Younger members increasingly argue that neutrality is not innocence, and that to hand a tyrant a living world is to take a share in whatever he does on it. The Council's reply is that the guild makes worlds habitable and what is done with a habitable world is not the guild's affair.

And a guild that decides who receives habitable worlds, and in what order, holds enormous unspent power. A faction has always murmured that some of it should be spent, on environmental standards, on protections for terraformed populations, on conditions of labour. That last has lately taken on a second meaning, because the labour most visibly in want of protection is the guild's own Tenders.

## Membership

The guild recruits its terraformers from across human space. Candidates apply to the Academy, where admission is fierce and weighed on scientific aptitude, problem-solving, and the psychological fitness for isolated long-duration work. The intake is deliberately drawn from everywhere: mixed perspectives make for better problem-solving, and a guild whose members come from every polity is easier to believe neutral.

Those admitted owe the Collective their professional loyalty. They may not practise terraforming independently, may not carry guild technique beyond the guild, and tithe a share of contract earnings to the treasury. In return they receive the finest training and equipment in human space, a community, and a craft.

Tenders are enrolled rather than sworn. They are taken on as labour from client populations, from frontier worlds, and from the guild's own unraised children, and are bound by the Collective's rules and rewarded from its treasury without entering its guild. They hold their own internal grades and rise within them. What they cannot do, short of being lifted into the Academy, is cross into the sworn.

Members who break guild rules—above all the prohibition on sharing proprietary technique—face charges before the Council, a defence heard, and a vote. For a sworn terraformer expulsion is ruin, and the threat alone keeps most in line. What becomes of an expelled member carrying Genesis secrets in her head is a matter the Collective does not discuss in public.

## `powers/elysian-collective/lazarus-project.md`

# The Lazarus Project

Hundreds of worlds across human space bear the scars of terraforming that failed—abandoned by bankrupt corporations, botched by confident amateurs, outrun by colonial governments whose ambitions exceeded their ecology. The Lazarus Project is the guild's standing programme to revive them.

## What a dead world is

A failed terraforming project does not return a world to its natural state. It leaves something worse: conditions that are neither the stable hostility the planet started with nor the stable habitability it was promised, but an unstable ruin that goes on deteriorating without anyone's help.

| Atmospheric contamination  | air that is neither breathable nor naturally stable                            |
| Ecosystem collapse         | introduced organisms dead, or invasive, or both in sequence                    |
| Geological destabilisation | drilling, mining or gravitational work that woke the tectonics                 |
| Hydrological disruption    | water systems redirected or poisoned past natural recovery                     |
| Cascading failure          | several of the above feeding one another, producing conditions more hostile than the original rock |

The Collective did not make most of these ruins. Many predate the guild, and the rest were mostly the work of operators without its expertise, which is exactly the point the guild does not tire of making. A restored world is a better argument than any brochure, and a gallery of amateur catastrophes is a better argument still for why the Genesis Protocols stay locked in the Archive.

## How it works

Assessment comes first: a small team of five to ten specialists, six to eighteen months on a dead world, establishing what failed, how far the failure has run, and whether the place can be saved at all. Some cannot, the damage being too extensive or the cost past any justification. The unrecoverable are documented, monitored and left to their long decay.

Restoration is harder than virgin terraforming by a margin the guild teaches its apprentices early. An untouched world is hostile and stable; a failed one is hostile and chaotic, its broken systems still moving, so crews must stabilise a collapse while building its replacement.

It is also the work that kills. The Tenders do most of it: the worst air the Collective ever asks anyone to breathe, machines improvised against conditions no protocol anticipated, decades-long postings on worlds that were someone else's mistake. Maret Solveig, the Seventh Grandmaster, has led Reclamation for most of her career and has buried more Tenders than any Grandmaster alive, and it has escaped none of her colleagues that the Council's most insistent voice for naming the Tender dead in the world-songs is the woman who signs the letters home.

## The showpieces

Veldtgrave was an agricultural venture of the 3720s whose ecosystem was engineered for maximum yield rather than stability. It ran for thirty years, collapsed in cascading species failures, and was abandoned when its owner went bankrupt. The Collective took it up in 3782 C, and eighty years of painstaking work have re-established a functioning soil biome and basic vegetation cover, with animal reintroduction due within the decade and another thirty to fifty years still to run.

Marchetti's Folly is named for Giuliana Marchetti, an independent terraformer of the 3650s whose non-standard atmospheric conversion produced air that was technically breathable and progressively neurologically ruinous. The guild's atmospheric engineers have spent thirty years since 3831 C scrubbing her signature out of the sky. The recent reclassification from Suit to Dome is real progress, with full restoration estimated at another twenty to forty years.

Brighthollow was a colonial government's ambitious irrigation scheme, which redirected the water systems and soured the soil chemistry beneath a nascent biosphere. Three decades of failed self-remediation later, the same government hired the guild in 3850 C. The restoration is twelve years into an estimated thirty, and is complicated less by the hydrology than by the client, who wants rapid agricultural recovery where the guild wants systematic restoration.

## The Anomalies

Three times, on three worlds catalogued as Lazarus Anomaly sites, assessment teams have found the remains of terraforming the Collective does not recognise. The atmospheric chemistry is wrong for every known method, the geological modifications follow no logic in the Archive, and the biological remnants descend from no lineage the Conservatory holds. All three sites predate reliable records.

The official position is cautious interest. The sites are monitored, studied, and pointedly not restored until the original techniques are understood, since erasing them would erase the only copy of whatever they were.

Within the guild the speculation runs less cautiously. A lost school of planetary engineering—pre-Cascade, pre-Collective, perhaps pre-Scattering—would be the largest discovery in the craft's history and a standing insult to the guild's belief that it holds the whole art. Failures so catastrophic that even the evidence stopped making sense would be almost as instructive. Solveig has made the Anomalies a personal priority, and her assessment teams now carry instrumentation designed to detect anomalous signatures wherever they go.

## The ledger

The Project runs at a net loss, funded from the general treasury and topped up by commissioning polities, research grants, and the donations of worlds that owe the guild their sky and feel it. The Council counts the loss acceptable and renews the budget without much argument.

## `powers/elysian-collective/technology.md`

# Elysian Collective: Technology

Basic terraforming is no secret: any competent polity can thicken an atmosphere or seed an algae bloom and then wait a few centuries. What the Collective commands is the art of doing it within a lifetime or two, and the machines that let fifteen million terraformers and their Tenders move thirty to fifty worlds at once.

## Genesis Protocols

Rapid biosphere establishment: seeding a world with engineered organisms that bootstrap a working ecosystem in decades rather than centuries.

| Assessment    | full planetary survey of atmosphere, geology, hydrology, radiation and orbit | 1–3 years   |
| Preparation   | atmospheric and geological work to make conditions fit for life              | 5–30 years  |
| Seeding       | engineered pioneers—extremophile bacteria, algae, fungi                      | continuous  |
| Cascade       | successive waves of more complex organisms, each extending the last          | 20–50 years |
| Stabilisation | management toward self-sustaining biological cycles                          | 10–30 years |
| Monitoring    | long observation and small corrections                                       | indefinite  |

Genesis organisms are the guild's most closely held secret. Each is purpose-built for one world and no two packages are alike. They are engineered to obsolesce, pioneers dying back as conditions change and yielding to successors better suited to the world they have helped make, and to resist analysis, so that organisms recovered from a project site give up little to a competitor's laboratory. Rivals have tried to reverse-engineer them for centuries, with partial results at best.

## Atmospheric processors

Industrial atmosphere-modification plants well beyond any commercial rival: three to five times commercial conversion rates, gas selectivity to parts per billion, about forty per cent better energy efficiency than the nearest competitor, and fifty years or more of continuous running.

A Collective processor can turn a thin, toxic atmosphere breathable in decades where commercial gear would need a century, and the efficiency advantage compounds across a project's length into enormous savings. Processors are built aboard the Garden, deployed to sites, and never sold or licensed. They remain guild property throughout a project and are removed when it ends. Running and maintaining these plants, day and night for fifty years in lethal air, is the core of what the Tenders do.

## Gravity arrays

Orbital arrays tune a planet's orbital parameters—damping wobble, nudging rotation, fine-tuning axial tilt—by amounts trivial in astronomical terms and decisive for habitability. A few degrees of tilt is the difference between seasons a biosphere can use and extremes that kill it.

Surface field generators locally adjust gravity, used mostly during construction and for long-term management on worlds of borderline surface gravity. The work is energy-hungry and limited: the guild cannot make a small moon feel like Earth, but it can shift conditions by fifteen or twenty per cent.

## Keeping the secret

Genesis designs are never disclosed, processor specifications are classified, gravity-array configurations are project-specific and retained, findings are shared within the guild at Conclave and never published outside it, and members who disclose face expulsion.

A client receives a habitable world and no idea how it was made. Industrial interests argue the techniques should be shared for humanity's benefit, and the guild answers that loosed into untrained hands they would breed catastrophe, pointing for proof at the Lazarus Project's gallery of worlds wrecked by amateurs.

## Research

| Deep Biosphere           | organisms that drive geological change from inside a planet's crust          |
| Rapid Atmosphere         | pushing conversion times below current floors                                |
| Ice World Protocols      | methods for frozen worlds with subsurface oceans                             |
| Collaborative Enhancement| a joint programme with the Neo-Solar Republic on biological applications of morphological technology |

Research is funded from contract revenue and run mostly aboard the Garden and at dedicated stations. Its output is never published; innovations fold into practice and are protected as proprietary knowledge.

## `powers/elysian-collective/the-garden.md`

# The Garden

The Collective's mobile headquarters: a deep-space station some 600 years old and continuously expanded, holding around 3 million permanent residents. It is the guild's centre of operations, its only school, its memory and its showcase.

The Collective holds no territory beyond it—only this moving capital and a scatter of worksites. Thirty to fifty worlds are under terraforming at any time, each a long-lived camp; the Lazarus sites are the hardest and deadliest postings; and dedicated research stations handle the advanced work. Most of the guild's people are not at the famous address but out on the projects, in prefab camps under hostile skies, for years or decades at a stretch.

## Where it is

The Garden keeps no fixed orbit. It moves between systems on a schedule set by the Council—near active projects, near resupply, or simply into deep space far from anyone—and its position is known only to the Council, the senior navigation staff and the pilot cadre. Invited guests are carried in aboard guild ships from designated meeting points and never learn the route.

It moves under conventional drives, with periodic gate transit when distance demands. Pushing a station of the Garden's mass through a jump gate is enormously expensive, and the Collective pays it without complaint.

## The biosphere

A vast interior biodome under an artificial sky, holding living specimens from every world the Collective has made. Temperate forest runs up against tropical wetland, alpine meadow sits above desert, and water threads through all of it. Visitors used to the thin biospheres of ordinary stations describe the experience as overwhelming, the air thick with scent and birdsong.

It does not run itself. A standing body of biologists and keepers manages the species balance, watches for collapse, and folds in new specimens as projects finish, much of the daily labour done by Tenders and civilian staff. Six centuries, members will tell you, without a single biosphere failure.

## The sections

The Conservatory holds the formal collection: catalogued specimens from every Collective world, kept in controlled environments that reproduce their home conditions. It is part museum, part gene bank and part insurance—if a terraformed world's ecosystem fails, the Conservatory holds the material to rebuild it—and ranks among the most complete biological archives in human space.

The Academy is the guild's only school and the gate every sworn terraformer passes through. Apprentices spend five to ten years here in theory, technique and guild culture, learning across disciplines before they specialise, on the principle that an atmospheric engineer who cannot read a biosphere is no use to anyone. Instructors are senior Masters rotating through between projects, who mostly regard teaching as a duty and return to the field as soon as they decently can. It is also where the Collective's class line is drawn each year.

The Archive holds records of every terraforming project in human space, Collective or otherwise: reports, environmental data, Genesis specifications, the world-songs, and the documentation of failures that feeds the Lazarus Project. Access requires Council authorisation, and even senior Masters cannot wander it freely.

The Council Chamber is a circular space at the station's core where the Council sits and, once a year, the full Conclave convenes. It is built for the unaided voice—no amplification, by a tradition older than the station's current form—and deliberately bare, with no ornament and no display of rank.

The residential districts fold the station's engineering into its greenery, with homes opening onto green space, paths winding through planting, and simulated light and biosphere-filtered air that make the place feel like ground rather than orbit. A berth on the Garden is the guild's highest material reward, extended to members, their families, and the civilian staff the city cannot do without. The waiting list is long: many of the sworn spend their best decades on project sites before a berth comes free, and many Tenders are promised the Garden their whole lives and die having seen it only in the world-songs.

## Defence

The Garden carries long-range sensors for early warning, a small escort fleet of armed ships, and point defences against anything that reaches it. Its surest protection is that a station nobody can find is a station nobody can hit. After that comes neutrality, which the Council is not so naive as to trust on its own.

# The Mandate

The Mandate defines itself by harmony and hierarchy. It is the largest unitary political entity in human space, and its gestures at uniformity belie great diversity.

The prestigious Mandate Classical is the second language learnt by people speaking hundreds of regional tongues. Although the Mandate's people are categorised into the two boxes of "citizens" and "subjects", that whole apparatus is invisible throughout the lives of the vast majority.

The Mandate has a population of circa 2.4 trillion people, and it is ruled from the throne world of Tiamming. Its on-paper absolute imperial autocracy is in practice a vast bureaucracy that can more easily be steered than commanded. The current sovereign, 17 Xuanzong, has spent 41 years on the Stellar Throne and at 96 has not named a successor.

The military of the Mandate is the largest in human space; a portion of it is presently occupied in blockading the Yansieve rebellion.

## Citizenship

Not everyone in Mandate space is a citizens of the Mandate. The remainder are "subjects", whose movement, professions and legal rights are somewhat circumscribed. The impact of this distinct in daily life can be negligible (as in the agricultural hinterlands of Tiamming or off-the-grid warrens anywhere), or it can define every interaction (as in the most explotative industrial areas or the least carefree noble estates).

## Current Events

A dozen or so frontier systems have been in open revolt (the Yansieve rebellion) since 3858 C, governing themselves through a porous imperial blockade. They demand more autonomy and the abolotion of the citizen-subject distinction.

A suspicious number of imperial ships are deployed to Kepler-442, their purpose and status classified.

## Misrule

Each Gap 1 the emperor ceremonially abdicates and formal hierarchies invert until sunset on Gap 3, when the emperor is crowned anew. It may only be theatre, but it is a society-wide performance.

| Frontier Freeholds | Territorial friction and steady trade. The Bureau insists without proof that Freehold elements are arming the Yansieve rebels |

## `powers/mandate/bureau.md`

# The Bureau of Internal Harmony

The Bureau of Internal Harmony (colloquially "the Bureau") is the genuinely formidable, widely feared, yet quietly overstretched subdirectorate of the Ministry of Harmony responsible for keeping order within the Mandate.

The Bureau monitors threats to imperial stability. "Threats to stability" may be interpreted to include political dissent, organised crime, separatism, corruption, "disharmonious" movements, foreign intelligence, and whatever else the Bureau decides belongs on the list this year.

## Methods

Informant networks operating through coercion, conviction or coin are feeding files of millions of the Mandate's population. In many places there is an unverifiable sense that someone in the room is reporting. In fewer cases the Bureau may directly monitor communications through remote means or through physical infiltration.

There are statistics on the Bureau's arrests and interrogations in relation to identified crimes; there are none on those few who disappear for longer than the prescribed three-day period.

## Structure

The central Bureau is headquartered in the Chrysanthemum Court. Offices covering whole sectors and cheifs posted to individual systems or stations (both with broad local authority) report in regularly. Covert operatives known as the Unseen are unnumbered and embedded everywhere, or at least anywhere.

Lady Fen Aravis, aged seventy-one after twenty-three years in the post, is the current Minister for Internal Harmony. Her reputation is based more on fear than fact, because the few facts that are recorded—every mission a success, every rival a failure—are doubtless little more than a highly and ruthlessly curated narrative. Her own views and loyalties are either pragamtically flexible or truly divided.

## `powers/mandate/citizen-subject.md`

# Citizens and Subjects

Citizens possess voting rights in local and regional assemblies, full freedom of movement, access to all professions and education, and rights including protection against arbitrary detention. By contrast, subjects require travel permits, are barred from certain professions, cannot vote, and are vulnerable to procedural abuses.

Proportions of the two classes vary wildly from system to system and city to city. Citizenship passes by lineage; mixed parentage produces subjects unless the citizen parent sponsors elevation.

There are several paths to citizenship, including a decade or more in the military, entrance to the civil service, recognition for exceptional contribution by the powers that be, and monetary purchase.

## The Uncounted

Some people defy the binary, in the Substrate beneath Hiveholm, along the ragged edged of the Provisional Territories, or between the cracks of any city large enough to get lost in. Life without a legal existence affords certain freedoms but also invites official and unofficial dangers.

## `powers/mandate/culture.md`

# The Mandate: Culture

A Hiveholm family may speak Parvatan over dinner and switch to Classical the moment the talk turns to a child's examinations: a home tongue for living and a state tongue for getting on.

## Language

Mandate Classical is the language of law, literature, official correspondence and social aspiration. It is required for the civil service examinations and mandatory in the Courts. It is also almost nobody's mother tongue—it is acquired at school, at expense, on purpose—and spoken imperfectly it marks a man as provincial or foreign before he finishes a sentence.

More than two hundred regional languages persist beneath it, winning the kitchen, the market and the funeral. Formal contexts demand Classical; elsewhere people use whatever they share.

The Bureau monitors both and takes only one seriously. Sedition in Classical is committed in the language of the state and is therefore real; the same words in a regional tongue are more easily filed as folklore or provincial grumbling.

## Religion

The Mandate promotes no religion and tolerates most. The First Dynasty fell partly to religious war, and the modern Mandate was built by men determined not to repeat it. Religious bodies register with the Ministry of Rites, accept monitoring, limits on proselytising and ceilings on property, and are otherwise left alone.

The Ministry's figures record that most citizens and subjects carry no strong religious commitment; they also record only what people are willing to tell the Ministry of Rites. Whole provincial worlds are thoroughly devout in ways the registries file as folklore.

## Art and entertainment

High culture is defined and largely paid for by the Chrysanthemum Court: opera, classical music, formal verse, dances that take decades to learn. The forms were perfected long ago, so mastery now means flawless repetition and innovation is faintly suspect.

Hiveholm and the manufacturing worlds grow their own—music built on factory rhythm, sculpture from industrial waste, theatre staged in break rooms. Parvatan industrial music has become fashionable well beyond the Mandate, to the Ministry of Rites' faint discomfort.

Vega productions are watched wherever the couriers call. They reach the slow provinces years old and are watched anyway, alongside local productions the core has never heard of. The Bureau screens imports and bans the occasional title.

## Cuisine

The throne world's three traditions have been canonised by proximity to power.

| Xuanese (northern)     | Subtle flavours, exact presentation, ingredients chosen for symbol as much as taste. A formal meal is a performance with rules. |
| Parvatan (industrial)  | Calorie-dense and built for eating fast, with bold spices to cut through industrial air and fermented condiments that keep for months. |
| Osthavn (agricultural) | Seasonal feasts and fresh produce in varieties the cities never see. |

## Misrule

On the morning of Gap 1 the emperor abdicates in favour of a citizen chosen at random—the Fool Emperor—until sunset on Gap 3. Masters serve servants, officers defer to the enlisted, and subjects are addressed with citizen honorifics.

Under Misrule, things that would be sedition on any other day may be said aloud without punishment. A subject who tells his employer exactly what he is worth has Misrule's protection: his words may be remembered, but they cannot officially have been heard.

Observance varies. At the Chrysanthemum Court it is choreographed and documented. On Hiveholm it is raw, and supervisors drink themselves insensible rather than have to remember what was said to them. Provincial estates keep it as the local lord prefers; none has managed to abolish it. On many far worlds it has fused past recognising with older local festivals, which the Ministry of Rites counts as Misrule and does not look into. It protects only those the system recognises: in the Substrate it is three days when the sweeps are less likely to come.

## Manners

Formality tightens toward the core and loosens toward the frontier. Position is meant to be visible—dress signals profession and rank, registration bands mark legal category at a glance, deference runs uphill. Strangers uncertain of their relative standing find the situation uncomfortable, which is part of why the unregistered, who wear no band and fit no slot, are unsettling.

Guest traditions run deep and vary in form, converging on a common core: a guest is protected, enemies may share a table, and violations are remembered for generations. It is the one relation the citizen-subject distinction is not supposed to touch.

## `powers/mandate/government.md`

# The Mandate: Government

Under a theoretical absolutism simmers a self-perpetuating bureacracy acting in the emperor's name.

## The Stellar Thone

The position of emperor is hereditary. This principle has been strained, most notably suring the Long Interregnum (2756–2847 C), ninety-one years of fracture and pretenders that followed the First Dynasty's collapse.

The current emperor, 17 Xuanzong, rules with a methodical and renowned patience. From the Andromedan Banking War to the Yansieve rebellion his strategy has been to contain, isolate and wait.

An edict of the emperor which an institution dislikes will not be defied; rather, it will be studied, clarified or implemented with sufficient care that the next edict overtakes it.

The survived descendants of 17 Xuanzong hold honours but little real power, and none have yet been named his successor.

## The Ministerial Council

Eight ministers are appointed by the Emperor and serve in a district of the Chrysanthemum Court, responsible for myriads of civil servants. Their ministries are (in order of precedence) Harmony, Rites, War, Justice, Revenue, Transit, Colonial Affairs and Diplomacy.

Currently the minister argue under smiles over the correct response to the Yansieve rebellion. Three factions have arisen: crush, contain or concede. The first is championed by marshal Cael Ombros; the second has the support of inertia and the emperor's implicit imprimatur; the last is the quietest, gestured at in euphemism by deputy minister Vasha Toren for colonial affairs.

## Civil Service

Meritocratic examinations allow advancement to anyone who applies. However, preparation is expensive and the tests reward those with the leisure and money to become familiar with the correct cultural and administrative shibboleths. The framework does occasionally find and elevate a brilliant outsider, but most promotions occur via patronage and nepotism.

The bureacracy contains true believes, careerists, humane administrators who follow the spirit of the law and absurd ones devoted to the letter, all outnumbered by a sedimentary layer of day-labourers with no paritcular aspirations or expectations.

## Provincial administration

Sector governors are appointed to clusters of systems and are answered solely to the ministries and the throne. The prestige and pprecarity of these appointment leads to generally capable but inconspicuous work.

Below them, planetary prefects are empowered to collect revenue and instructed to implement directives and keep order. For most of the Mandate's people the prefect is the highest authority that is relevant to them; the heavens are high and the emperor is far away.

Within the core systems, aristocratic families (which can pre-date the modern Mandate) hold hereditary territories that run themselves. The local potentate's character determines the law his citzens and subjects actually live under.

## `powers/mandate/military.md`

# The Mandate: Military

The largest military in human space is built to deter other great powers, hold infrastructure, and win set-piece battles by mass. It fields some 50 billion active personnel against perhaps 200 billion reserves, with thousands of capital ships and tens of thousands of support vessels.

## Organisation

The Imperial Navy handles system defence, force projection, blockade and interdiction, and orbital bombardment, and is the senior service. Its officer corps is drawn disproportionately from citizen military families; a subject reaching flag rank is remarked upon.

The Imperial Army is larger and less glamorous—planetary defence, occupation, internal security and ceremony—and considerably more subject-heavy, because the military road to citizenship mostly runs through the ground forces.

The Imperial Guard combines the personal protection of the emperor and his family with special operations. Selection is brutal and service is for life.

Sector governors maintain their own forces for system defence, anti-piracy and emergency response. These are subordinate to Imperial command in principle and considerably autonomous in daily practice.

## Doctrine

Four principles are taught. Mass, on the reasoning that attrition favours the larger power. Control of infrastructure, since holding jump nodes and transit routes matters more than patrolling the space between them. Overwhelming response, so that defiance appears unsurvivable and is not begun. And patience, the Mandate being able to sustain an operation for longer than an opponent can sustain resistance.

In practice the forces are standardised, well equipped, heavily drilled, and rigid, with initiative discouraged below senior rank.

## Deployments

The Navy holds the lanes around the Yansieve systems and isolates the planets. The ground campaign that would retake them has not been authorised.

Forces are deployed to Kepler-442; purpose and status classified.

The L4 complex at High Tiamming remains the primary fleet base. It is quieter than it was three years ago, with ships, crews and matériel drawn off to the trailing frontier and those left behind working longer rotations.

## Service and citizenship

A subject who completes a fifteen- to twenty-year enlistment and is honourably discharged earns citizenship for himself, and by inheritance for his descendants. It is the commonest route across the line, and the Army's ranks are filled in no small part by men taking it. Parvati sends more of its subjects down this road than anywhere else in the Mandate. Veterans come home with the status they earned and a clear-eyed ambivalence about the system that made them earn it.

## `powers/mandate/sapphire-courts.md`

# The Sapphire Courts

Named for the blue stone worked into their architecture, the Sapphire Courts are the Mandate's judicial system. At the lower levels they mostly work; toward the top they mostly bend.

## Structure

| Local Courts    | Minor civil and criminal matters; registration violations | appointed magistrates                 |
| Regional Courts | Serious crimes, appeals, commercial disputes              | judges selected by legal examination   |
| High Courts     | Major crimes and constitutional questions; one per sector | imperial appointees                    |
| Azure Tribunal  | Final appeals; cases touching the Throne                  | the Chief Justice and twelve Tribunes  |

Local courts are first contact for most people and most matters, and range from the brisk and fair to the notoriously slow and purchasable. High Court appointees are political as much as judicial, chosen for reliability before brilliance. The Azure Tribunal sits in the Chrysanthemum Court; its decisions are final, with no appeal beyond the emperor, who almost never intervenes.

## The Sapphire Steps

The High Court complex in the Chrysanthemum Court, its steps carved from actual sapphire and worn down by nine hundred years of use. Major trials here are spectacles, with galleries seating thousands and proceedings broadcast across the empire.

## Procedure

The model is inquisitorial: the judges investigate. Testimony is weighed by citizenship status, social standing, relationship to the case and corroboration, with documentary and physical evidence preferred where it exists—which advantages men whose lives are well documented over men who live off the books.

Citizens may represent themselves or engage advocates, a professional class running from the local journeyman to the elite specialist. Subjects may engage advocates too, and frequently cannot afford one.

Sentences run from fines through imprisonment to execution for the gravest offences against the state. Subjects receive harsher sentences than citizens for equivalent crimes—not as written policy but as a statistical regularity everyone in the system knows about. Appeals are expensive and slow, and most cases end where they begin.

## The Courts and the Bureau

The Bureau of Internal Harmony runs parallel to the Courts in matters of state security. It may detain a subject administratively without charge, trial or limit, and may hold a citizen for seventy-two hours—or far longer under the heading of national security—with a judicial authorisation that is rarely refused.

The most serious political cases therefore never reach a courtroom at all. The line between an ordinary crime, which the Courts will try in the open, and a threat to the state, which the Bureau will handle elsewhere, is not fixed in law.

## `powers/mandate/yansieve-rebellion.md`

# The Yansieve Rebellion

Twelve to fifteen trailing frontier systems—the count is disputed, including by the rebels—have been in open revolt since 3858 C. They have abolished the citizen-subject distinction in the territory they hold, and demand its abolition throughout the Mandate or, failing that, independence.

## Origins

The Yansieve cluster was settled late, administered lightly, and worked largely by subjects in extractive labour. Its grievances were the ordinary grievances of the far frontier: disproportionate taxation, infrastructure that never arrived, long neglect punctuated by sudden arbitrary attention. Most of the Mandate's frontier could say the same and does not rebel.

In 3858 C the Bureau ran a registration sweep across the cluster, its stated purpose the updating of subject records. In practice it meant mass detention over paperwork irregularities, property seizure, forced relocation, and violence against anyone who resisted being processed. Protest became suppression became uprising, and within months a dozen systems had declared independence.

The organisation behind it—cells, couriers, weapons caches—had assembled beneath the Bureau's notice. Whether that reflects resource limits, intelligence failure, or the old habit of hearing sedition only when it is spoken in Classical has not been settled in public. Heads rolled regardless, and Yansieve operations are now run with an intensity that owes something to an institution's need to prove it still works.

## The stalemate

Imperial blockades ring the rebel systems and the Navy holds the lanes, but isolation is not conquest. A ground campaign to retake the systems would be bloody, ruinously expensive, prolonged and politically poisonous. It has not been authorised.

In the territory they hold, the rebels have replaced imperial administration with elected councils, reorganised the economy around self-sufficiency, and raised militias. How well any of it functions is precisely what the blockade keeps ambiguous. Mandate sources report chaos and incipient collapse; sympathetic accounts describe austere but working self-rule.

The rebellion is not one thing. Its factions divide over whether negotiation is conceivable, how a post-imperial society should be ordered, whether to seek outside support and from whom, and how to fight. Containment is in large part a wager on the rebels falling out before the blockade does.

## The argument at the centre

Marshal Cael Ombros and the war faction hold that four years of blockade is four years of advertising, and that a decisive campaign would settle the frontier whatever the cost.

Containment is the emperor's line: wait for hunger, exhaustion, or the rebels' own factions to do the work that armies would do expensively and visibly.

A thin scattering in Colonial Affairs and Justice, with deputy minister Vasha Toren the nearest thing it has to a face, holds that the registry manufactures rebels faster than the Bureau can detain them. They do not say abolition. The rebels have already said it for them.

## The official line

The Mandate's position is that this is criminal sedition: the citizen-subject system is the legal foundation of the state, to abolish it would be revolution, and the Throne does not negotiate revolution.

Imperial rhetoric prefers a rebellion made elsewhere. The Bureau's own intelligence suggests contacts between rebel leaders and elements of the Frontier Freeholds, and nothing conclusive beyond that. The smuggling is real but commercial, and runs through Sable Cartel subsidiaries that are also lending against the blockade.

## The watching empire

Across the Mandate, subjects follow Yansieve news with a specificity that unsettles the men whose job it is to notice such things. Hiveholm workers discuss the frontier in detail; Parvati's subjects share both legal status and grievance with the rebels; agricultural Osthavn, weeks of travel away, hosts the occasional refugee and a great deal of pointed silence. Few are ready to rise.

## `powers/mandate/locations/marag.md`

# Marag

A middling agricultural world in a middling coreward province, weeks off the courier trunks and Imperial for seven centuries. Around 600 million people in the Serat sector, about fifty-two per cent of them subjects. Everyone speaks Maragi; the examination class and the port also have Classical, and the port has Trade Tongue. The sector capital is nine days away by scheduled courier.

## The Mandate at this distance

Here the empire is four buildings and a schedule: the prefecture, the registry office, a local-tier Sapphire courthouse, and the Bureau station—one chief, four assistants, and a continent apiece. The prefect is the government, and Marag has been lucky in its current one and knows from its grandmothers' stories what unlucky feels like.

The citizen-subject line runs through everything and weighs almost nothing daily. Registration bands, where they are worn at all, are pushed up the forearm like a bracelet, and the checkpoint on the delta road has been staffed by the same family of citizens for three generations and waves through everyone it recognises, which is everyone. What the line still does, it does at the hinges of a life—examination fees, the better postings, the weight of a word in court—as custom administered by neighbours. That is gentler than Parvati's version until a grievance arises, when there is no crowd to disappear into and no other checkpoint to try.

## Three Marags

The delta is the world the sector files mean: river cities, engineered grain plains, the barge culture that moves the harvest, and the examination schools where delta families have bought their sons into the civil service, one or two a generation, celebrated on the town gates. Classical is spoken here with real polish.

The highlands are older, drier and devout—herding country, terrace villages, the lamp-shrines of the Long Vigil on every ridgeline. Highland Marag treats the state as weather and pays its quotas punctually so as to be left alone, which it mostly is.

Port Serat-Marag, the one licensed spaceport, is a third country of perhaps two million: off-world manners, Trade Tongue signage, warehouses, the recruiting office, and everything on the world that is less than a decade old. The port thinks the delta smug and the highlands a museum. The rest of the world calls port people the hurried, and means it as a diagnosis.

## The Long Vigil

The registries record Marag's faith as regional folk practice, lamp-tending, ancestral. It is a theology seven centuries deep with its own schools, its scandalous mystics, and a long doctrinal quarrel between the delta's easy observance and the highlands' strict one. The Vigil holds that every settled world is a lamp lit against the dark between the stars, and that the tending of the lamp—the fields, the children, the dead, the flame itself—is one continuous act of keeping faith. Every household keeps a flame and every village a Nightwatch. Once a year at the Lamp Feast the whole world banks its fires down to a single flame per town and sits with the dark it is refusing.

The Ministry of Rites counts the Lamp Feast as Misrule, because it falls near the Gap and involves licensed disorder.

## What arrives, and when

Trunk news reaches Marag weeks old. Vega serials come by the season-crate, years stale, and are loved without urgency: the delta towns hold viewing nights for episodes Lyra forgot a decade ago, and the local barge-plays, performed on the harvest water, borrow their plots and improve on their endings. The Yansieve news arrives the same way and lands differently. The port reads it loudly, the delta discusses it carefully at dinner in Maragi, and the highlands say nothing at all, which the Bureau's station chief dutifully reports and cannot interpret.

The recruiting circuit takes several thousand of Marag's subject sons each year, and the veterans' lodges in the delta towns are where they come home to, pensioned and listened to.

## People

Prefect Ren Baiyu, 58, Xuanese-educated and examination-risen, was posted here twenty-one years ago as a rung on a ladder he stopped climbing. He governs through the delta families, the Vigil's Nightwatches and the veterans' lodges, spending his small budget where the world tells him it hurts. His sector reports are models of the form—orderly, productive, quiet—and are, he would admit after the second cup, works of translation. He has been offered promotion twice and pleaded health. His health is excellent.

Varza Oduyan, 74, is Nightwatch-mother of the high terraces: in the state's eyes a folklore practitioner, in fact the senior religious authority for forty million people. She has met the prefect eleven times in twenty years, each visit recorded as cultural liaison. Between them they have settled water disputes, a famine winter, and one very quiet matter involving a Bureau assistant who overstepped. She tends the oldest lamp on the world.

Sero Mkren, 44, did twenty years in the Imperial Army, earned citizenship at the standard price, and came home with a pension, a limp he explains only when drunk, and the delta's most crowded lodge table. He follows the Yansieve dispatches with the attention of a man checking a sum. Asked by the prefect what the lodges thought of the frontier, he gave the answer that went, lightly edited, into a sector report: "We think what soldiers think. That it could have been us, both ways."

## `powers/mandate/locations/tiamming/chrysanthemum-court.md`

# The Chrysanthemum Court

The Mandate's capital complex: four hundred square kilometres at the mouth of the Songjiang on the northern coast of Xuan, arranged in concentric rings of decreasing access. Metropolitan population around 500 million.

## The districts

The Forbidden Precincts are the walled compound at the heart, holding the throne room, the residential palaces, the most sensitive offices of state, and gardens laid out to represent the diversity of the empire. Entry is by imperial invitation, which most high officials never receive. 17 Xuanzong has ruled from inside these walls for forty-one years, through intermediaries, seen directly by very few.

The Ministries District, south of the Precincts, holds tens of thousands of civil servants in imposing stone halls: tax assessments, military logistics, infrastructure plans, subject registrations, citizenship applications, judicial appeals, diplomatic correspondence. The facades promise permanence; the interiors are cubicles and the low hum of procedure. It is also where the crush-contain-concede argument is actually conducted, in the form of disputes over budget lines, jurisdictional boundaries and the proper interpretation of precedent.

The Jade Quarters hold aristocratic compounds, ministerial residences, and wealthy citizens who want proximity to power. Clean streets, discreet security, impeccable service. Among the hereditary nobles and risen examination graduates lives the occasional wealthy subject who has bought a fine house and not yet bought citizenship.

The Thousand Markets are the commercial district, where Trade Tongue is heard as often as Classical, foreign merchants keep permanent establishments, and Drift shuttles dock to trade. The citizen-subject distinction blurs further here than anywhere else in the Court, though the registration bands are still there to be read.

The Old City predates the modern Mandate and preserves First Dynasty and colonial architecture, narrow streets, temples to discouraged faiths, festivals forgotten elsewhere, and dialects Classical was meant to bury.

The Sapphire Steps, where the High Courts convene, stand at the edge of the ministries.

## Life and security

For citizens the Court is comfortable: transit runs, services function, and the formality is a frame for a good life rather than a cage around it. Subjects work here rather than live here—staffing its service, construction and cleaning, commuting in from the surrounding districts and from Parvati. Visitors get the Thousand Markets, the Old City and the ministries' petition offices; the Jade Quarters and Forbidden Precincts are, for practical purposes, another planet.

This is the most secure location in the Mandate: Imperial Guard, Bureau surveillance, checkpoints at every district boundary, layered access controls. Most of it presents as orderliness rather than occupation.

Lady Fen Aravis keeps offices in the Ministries District and a residence in the Jade Quarters. Her presence at Court functions is noted by everyone and sought by no one.

## `powers/mandate/locations/tiamming/hiveholm.md`

# Hiveholm

Hiveholm was planned for five hundred million people and holds two and a third billion, stacked into a vertical industrial complex in central Parvati. It is the largest city on Tiamming. Around seventy per cent of its people are subjects.

## The physical city

Built around heavy industry—shipyards, weapons works, fusion-plant fabrication—and grown vertical out of necessity. Residential blocks stack dozens of storeys, transit runs in three dimensions, and "street level" is a relative term. Natural light reaches the upper tiers and gives out long before the deep warrens, which have lived by artificial illumination for generations.

The air is managed and imperfect, a persistent haze that residents stop seeing within a week and visitors never quite stop tasting. Water is recycled and recycled again. Food arrives from Osthavn processed for storage rather than pleasure, so fresh produce is a luxury and reconstituted fare is simply what one eats.

## Work

Life centres on the shift. The factories run continuously, compensation is adequate, and advancement is capped, because the better positions are marked *citizens preferred*. A subject line-worker may rise to subject supervisor and no further, whatever his competence.

Labour organisation is permitted within carefully drawn limits. Workers may bargain collectively over wages, conditions and safety; they may not organise politically, criticise the citizen-subject system, or coordinate beyond the workplace. A union that demands better ventilation is doing its lawful job. A union that asks why the ventilation is worse on the subject floors is committing sedition.

## The Substrate

Beneath the official city, in the levels below habitation, live the people the census does not count: unregistered subjects, refugees, and those who slipped out of the administrative system and chose the margin over the band. The Substrate has its own authorities, its own economy and its own social order, and is outside the Mandate's sight, which is the whole of its appeal and the whole of its danger.

The Bureau sweeps it periodically—names checked, some registered and returned, some deported, the warranted taken—and the Substrate absorbs each sweep and re-forms. Some of its residents are tied into Yansieve networks. Most are simply people for whom the empire never had a place.

## Notable locations

Factory Complex 7 builds ships, stations and orbital infrastructure.

The Coalface is the oldest factory district, its original facilities preserved as working museums and its workers citizens by tradition and union rule: a citizen enclave marooned in subject-majority Parvati, and proud of it.

The Transit Nexus is a three-dimensional tangle of platforms and corridors moving millions a day, and the one place Hiveholm's population becomes visible to itself as a single mass.

The Deep Warren Markets trade unregistered technology, restricted substances, off-books labour and information. They are found by word of mouth and relocated after every raid.

## Wen Daoshi

A subject of fifty-three who supervises two hundred workers on the shift floor of Factory Complex 7, in a role her status forbids anyone from officially acknowledging. She has applied for citizenship three times and been refused three times on procedural grounds—documentation insufficiencies, backlogs, errors she suspects the Bureau of arranging and cannot prove. She has stopped applying. She has not stopped following the Yansieve news.

## The Jade Belt

Private stations at synchronous altitude where aristocratic families keep residences clear of planetary concerns. No imperial protocols, no petitioners, access by invitation, security private and absolute. The residents are active nobles wanting privacy, retired ministers, and the occasional subject rich enough to buy proximity without ever buying status.

The Floating Gardens, a station kept at low gravity and given over to extravagant hydroponics—water flowing in ribbons, plants growing in impossible directions—hosts the social functions where the powerful meet outside any official setting, and where a good many arrangements are made that appear in no ministerial record.

## High Tiamming

The military complex at L4: around 3 million naval personnel, with a fleet base, shipyards and sensor arrays. Multi-year rotations have bred persistent communities with their own traditions and slang, and career officers who spend more of their lives here than on any surface.

The station is markedly quieter than it was three years ago, with ships and crews pulled off to the trailing frontier, those remaining standing longer rotations, and the rotation cycles returning Yansieve veterans subtly changed.

## Commercial and defence infrastructure

Cargo stations transfer goods between the elevators and interstellar shipping, the largest handling millions of tonnes a day. Shipyards build and repair vessels of every size, and account for a significant fraction of the Mandate's naval and commercial fleet. Sensor networks watch every approach; automated and crewed defence platforms could not stop a determined invasion fleet but would extract a terrible price from one; patrol forces maintain a continuous presence throughout the system.

## Access

Citizens book passage, present credentials and travel. Subjects require a permit, approval depending on purpose, destination, record, and the temper of the reviewing official. The Jade Belt admits by invitation regardless of status. High Tiamming admits military personnel and authorised contractors only.

## `powers/mandate/locations/tiamming/osthavn.md`

# Osthavn

The southern continent of Tiamming, around 3 billion people, growing the food that supplies the planet and exporting the famous fraction—ceremonial vintages, court fruits, delicacies worth flying between stars—to core worlds that could feed themselves but not like this. About forty-five per cent of its people are subjects.

Agriculture runs at planetary scale: engineered plains, climate-controlled growing zones, vertical farms, most of the work automated and the population sparse by Tiamming's standards. Osthavn is provincial in the literal sense and the dismissive one, knows the dismissive sense is being used, and has decided not to mind. It considers itself honest and grounded in pointed contrast to Xuan's pretension and Parvati's grime, and being far from power produces roughly equal measures of resentment and relief.

## The Sandholm Peninsula

Where traditional agriculture persists alongside the automation, worked by families who have farmed the same land for eight centuries and blend Mandate-standard genetic engineering with techniques older than the modern Mandate.

The Sandholm families are citizen-majority and proud of it. Their ancestors earned that citizenship through agricultural productivity quotas that converted sustained output into legal status—a real ladder, climbed by real families, within living memory. The quotas were discontinued a generation ago. The Sandholms regard Parvati's subjects with a mixture of pity and wariness.

The peninsula is seasonal in a way that industrial Tiamming has forgotten how to be. Planting and harvest still structure the year, and the Gap festivals here mean an actual reckoning of the stores and a feast on an actual surplus.

## The Provisional Territories

Interior regions designated provisional, awaiting development planning that never came and administered by temporary authorities that became permanent the way temporary things do.

The Territories are where the Mandate puts people it does not want anywhere else: populations resettled from failed colonies, subjects sentenced to agricultural labour for minor crimes, citizens disgraced enough to exile but not enough to imprison. Some build lives and stay, some serve their time and leave, some die before either becomes relevant. The administrators are usually the least capable the civil service can spare, the posting being itself a soft punishment, and services are minimal and oversight distant.

Tam Orel, 38, is a registration clerk here, exiled for siphoning fees from transactions in the Ministries District—minor corruption, which is intolerable at the Court in a way it never is on the frontier. Nine years in, he will most likely die here, and somewhere in the ninth year he stopped resenting that and started building a life: a role that matters locally, relationships with the resettled people he processes, a small garden behind his quarters.

## Port Yelling

The largest transit hub, where Osthavn's produce is packed for off-world shipping and outside information filters in with the ships' crews. The port workers know more about galactic affairs than most of Osthavn and share it selectively. Named for a colonial administrator whose reputation has not survived him.

Yansieve is weeks of travel away and the mood here is not rebellious. The Sandholm citizens discuss the rebellion over dinner in some detail; the Territories' subjects do not discuss it at all, which is noted.

## `powers/mandate/locations/tiamming/parvati.md`

# Parvati

The equatorial continent of Tiamming, around 7 billion people, where the core's industry lives. An empire of hundreds of worlds builds almost everything locally, but Parvati holds the prestige tier: the fleet's honour-yards, the pattern-forges whose designs the provincial worlds copy, the works against which the rest of the Mandate measures itself.

About sixty-five per cent of the population are subjects, the highest proportion on the throne world. The reasons are structural rather than decreed—industrial work does not require citizenship, comfort-seeking citizens do not move to industrial regions, and status passes down the generations along with the jobs. The citizens who are here cluster in the roles that need legal standing or confer authority: managers, administrators, security.

Shared conditions produce a practical solidarity across the line that the northern estates lack. When the atmospheric scrubbers fail everyone breathes the same bad air; when the transit seizes everyone walks. The distinction still matters, but it is daily and physical rather than abstract and ceremonial. It also stops at the door: the citizen can leave and the subject cannot. Parvati sends more of its subjects into the military than any region in the Mandate, and its veterans come home citizens.

Parvatan dialects are distinct from Xuanese Classical, and code-switching between the tongue of home and the tongue of getting on is constant.

## Locations

Hiveholm, 2.3 billion people in a vertical industrial megacity, is the largest city on Tiamming.

The Extraction Zones in the interior handle mining and resource processing, with harsh conditions, transient workforces, and company towns where the operating corporation simply is the local authority.

The Coastal Manufacturing Belt runs lighter industry along the northern coast, with better conditions than the interior and proximity to shipping.

Port Avalon is the largest spaceport, moving bulk cargo for export. It is less prestigious than the Ascension Gates and handles far more tonnage.

## The Yansieve factor

Parvati follows the rebellion closely, its subjects sharing both legal status and accumulated grievance with the rebels. The Bureau monitors, and the conversations continue more carefully. Few here are ready to rise.

## `powers/mandate/locations/tiamming/xuan.md`

# Xuan

The northern continent of Tiamming, some 8 billion people, holding the empire's political centre and its oldest lineages. It has supplied the administrative class for centuries and has concluded that nowhere else quite matters.

## Geography

Temperate, cooling to genuinely cold in the north—one of the few places on a climate-managed world where real winters still come, the management having been deliberately left thin there. Ancient forests, some grown from the original terraforming; mountain ranges; broad river valleys where the earliest settlements took root. The Songjiang runs from the northern highlands to the sea, with the Chrysanthemum Court at its mouth. Cities are strung along its course and agriculture fills the floodplains, while the highlands stay sparse.

## The estates

Beyond the capital, Xuan belongs to its aristocracy. Noble clans hold territories ranging from modest farms to regions the size of small countries. Mandate law applies uniformly and subjects retain their rights on paper, but the local lord shapes life in ways the distant capital cannot. These are not feudal holdings in the medieval sense; they are a second government, older than the modern Mandate, whose loyalty was bought long ago with autonomy.

The Vervain clan controls the Songjiang headwaters, and therefore the water of hundreds of downstream communities. Its wealth is ancient and its loyalty has gone unquestioned for seventeen generations. It is the clan the Throne calls on when it wants reliable support to look spontaneous.

The Ombros clan administers the Northern Reaches, where the climate management thins out and the winters are real. It breeds the cold-weather horses for the ceremonial cavalry and keeps a stern northern self-image to match—plain speech, physical hardiness, open disdain for southern softness. It has lately produced Marshal Cael Ombros, Minister of War and the loudest advocate of crushing the Yansieve rebellion.

The Kazan clan lost half its holdings when the previous generation involved itself in a coup. Mirek Kazan, now 84, has spent forty years buying the family's standing back with ostentatious loyalty and well-placed marriages, and has largely succeeded. His public statements run more orthodox than the emperor's, his donations to imperial causes are conspicuous, and his children have married into impeccably loyal families. He has been the loyal Lord Kazan for so long that the distinction between the role and the man may no longer exist, including for him.

For subjects the estates mean paternalism at best. A good lord provides schools, medicine, dispute resolution and a real if circumscribed security; a bad lord extracts labour, deference and whatever else he can reach. The law theoretically shields subjects from the worst, but the shield depends on someone reporting a violation, and reporting one's own lord—across the distance, the deference and the lord's own networks—is not a thing most people attempt twice.

## Regional culture

The Xuanese dialect is the prestige form of Classical, Xuanese cuisine sets court fashion, and Xuanese traditions are presented, by Xuanese, as simply the traditions.

# Neo-Solar Republic

A transhumanist polity of around 80 billion citizen-instances—perhaps 50 to 60 billion distinct minds—confined almost entirely to Sol and centred on Synthesis at Sol-Earth L5. Materially abundant, strikingly free, and the most comfortable eighty billion in human space.

The body-swapping post-humans, the minds that live as distributed sensor arrays, the citizens who have forked themselves into standing committees are all real, and are perhaps a third of the citizenry. Most of the Republic's people are enhanced, networked, long-lived and conventionally embodied, leading recognisable lives.

## The Lattice

The Republic's defining institution is a persistent quantum-neural mesh linking every citizen's cognitive implants into something closer to a shared layer of consciousness than a communications network. Citizens experience it as an ambient awareness of collective thought—present, accessible, not intrusive—and governance, economics, art and social life all run through it.

It has one hard limit. The Lattice achieves true shared consciousness only within Sol and its near orbits; beyond that it decays to light speed, and citizens describe the experience as thinking through fog. The Republic is bound to Sol by its own neurology as much as by its infrastructure, which is why it is the one great power with almost no wish to expand.

## Who the eighty billion are

Counting the population means first deciding what counts as a person, which is the thing the Republic cannot stop arguing about. Some instances are several instantiations of one individual and some are collective entities registered as one.

| Heavily enhanced (EX 4+)     | Cyborgs and post-humans in radical morphologies. The largest single bloc, and about a third of the citizenry; they set the Republic's image, art and politics |
| Moderately enhanced (EX 1–3) | Most people: long-lived, implant-linked, conventionally embodied                            |
| Digital citizens             | Running on computational substrate with no body at all; recognised as full persons since 3840 |
| Forks                        | Independent instances of one base identity, legal since 3850, diverging from the moment they split |
| Collective intelligences     | Networks of minds functioning as one; granted citizenship in 3860                            |

The boundary between human and machine is not a legal category here. It is an open question the Republic re-answers every ten years, and the answer keeps getting larger.

## Current events

The Centennial Census of 3860 C, the decadal vote on what legally counts as a person, extended citizenship to collective intelligences. Conservative polities recoiled, and not all of the Republic was comfortable either.

Republic physicists have published a proof of concept for jump-gate operation that needs no fez: three per cent efficiency in the laboratory, operationally trivial and theoretically enormous. The Tethys Consortium funds rebuttals and the Sable Cartel has tried to seed operatives in the laboratories. The Republic's own estimate is that it is real, and decades away.

Several Drift Communities have begun taking up Republic enhancement, to their own deep internal division.

## Notable relations with other Great Powers

| The Mandate      | Technological appetite across deep ideological distance, and recurring quarrels over who and what counts as a person |
| Sable Cartel     | A standing security worry. The Cartel wants Lattice technology, and the Lattice is the one thing the Republic will never sell |

| The Amaranth League | Three systems of the Republic's own émigrés, who took the long life and refused the shared mind; each regards the other as the experiment that lost its nerve |

## `powers/neo-solar-republic/culture.md`

# Neo-Solar Republic: Culture

## The ordinary Republic

Most citizens sit between EX 1 and EX 3: enhanced, not transformed. Material need is not a daily concern, and two healthy centuries is the ordinary expectation, spent as people with time spend it—on craft and study and family and long elaborate hobbies.

They work, though not always because they must. The socionomy meets needs without anyone's labour, so work is increasingly something one does for meaning, status, or the plain pleasure of being useful. Someone keeps the reactors running and the relay nodes synchronised and the morphological clinics staffed; someone teaches the children, and there are still children; someone cooks, for the considerable minority who still eat.

## The vanguard

At the Republic's leading edge are the citizens who take its possibilities literally. They wear bodies built for vacuum or deep ocean or no environment at all, run as distributed intelligences across a dozen substrates at once, merge for a season's work and part again, and fork themselves to live two lives at the same time. They make the Republic's reputation and most of its art. The ordinary majority regards them with a mix of pride, bafflement and faint wariness.

## Going dark

Enhancement is genuinely optional, and a real minority of citizens live lightly integrated—basic implants, a single body, the Lattice kept at arm's length—or refuse the Lattice altogether and go, in the local idiom, dark. Some are religious or philosophical objectors, some immigrants from low-enhancement worlds, and some simply never took to it.

The Republic does not persecute them. It leaves them out. Political voice runs through the Lattice, and a citizen who is not on it has no resonance, no vote, and no share in the collective mind that decides everything.

## The question of the self

When you can change your body, copy your mind, merge with another and persist without flesh, the question of who you are becomes a matter of law.

Continuists hold that the self survives any change so long as subjective continuity holds, memory and narrative making the person. Processualists hold that there is no persistent self at all, only patterns that feel continuous, and find this liberating rather than bleak. Collectivists hold that the individual is a legacy unit, and that the real person is the network of minds that think together.

Which camp prevails decides who inherits, who is liable, who is married to whom, and who is even one person.

## What it costs

Forks who were one person and have grown into two who cannot stand each other, dividing a life and a family between them. Merges that took badly, leaving a mind that is neither of the two it was made from. Digital citizens who have run so long, and drifted so far, that their oldest friends no longer recognise the pattern. Families spread across bodies and substrates and forks, working to stay families across differences no counsellor was ever trained for.

The Republic meets these with support, counsel and a whole vocabulary of care, and some of it cannot be fixed, only grieved.

The starkest record is the Registry of Endings: the roll of citizens who have truly and permanently ceased—not the body-death the Lattice makes survivable, but the deliberate deletion of a mind that could have gone on. People end themselves in the Republic too.

## The Census

Every ten years the Republic holds the Centennial Census, a binding decision about what counts as a person.

| 3840 C | purely digital citizens  |
| 3850 C | forked individuals       |
| 3860 C | collective intelligences |

Each was controversial inside the Republic and alarming outside it. A vote that can enlarge personhood can also withhold it, and every expansion has left citizens on the losing side. The Collectivists' logic, followed all the way, ends with the individual dissolved into the network, and each Census takes a step that way.

## Art

Republic art is experienced rather than observed. Its dominant form is the synaesthetic composition: a made experience transmitted through the Lattice, weaving image, sensation, emotion, abstract structure and narrative into a single unbroken thing that lasts seconds or hours. The finest are held to be untranslatable.

Beyond them lie shared dream-states, collaborative spaces that many citizens build and inhabit together, some sustained for decades by rotating hands. Against all of it runs a counterculture of physical art—paint, stone, instruments played with actual fingers—prized precisely because it refuses the Lattice.

## Language and customs

Among themselves, citizens rarely use words, the Lattice carrying concept and reasoning and felt experience without the lossy squeeze of language. Citizens call ordinary speech talking through a keyhole. It is not a universal solvent—different cognitive architectures parse thought differently, and misunderstanding survives even here—but it makes Republic gatherings unnerving to outsiders, who see a room sit silent and motionless while a fierce argument runs through it.

Citizens greet one another with a brief Lattice packet of identity, mood and intent, exchanged faster than a nod. Visitors are fitted with a limited interface for translation and navigation, never cognitive integration, and assigned a Liaison to guide them.

A citizen whose body dies but whose mind persists is not dead. A citizen who deletes a living mind is, body or no.

## `powers/neo-solar-republic/governance.md`

# Neo-Solar Republic: Governance

The Republic does not vote. It resonates.

## Government by resonance

Proposals enter the Lattice not as documents but as structured thought-forms: packages of intent, reasoning and projected consequence that a citizen can experience directly rather than read. Citizens engage by thinking at them—weighing, amending, endorsing, opposing—and the system measures the aggregate as weighted resonance. Enough resonance across enough of the population makes a proposal binding; enough dissonance sends it back or kills it. There is no parliament, no election and no representative.

Three roles emerge in practice. Initiators craft proposals: anyone may, but doing it so the thought-form reads clearly across wildly different minds is a rare skill. Dissenters are prized, a proposal that draws no opposition being held to be under-examined. And amplifiers are citizens whose engagement carries outsized weight, through expertise or reputation or sheer clarity of thought.

Nobody appointed the amplifiers and nothing in the design admits them, but cognitive fatigue is real and nobody can engage every proposal, so most citizens most of the time defer to amplifiers they have come to trust. The Republic that abolished representatives is, on an ordinary day, run by a few hundred thousand voices the other eighty billion have agreed to listen to.

The failure modes the Republic acknowledges and watches are resonance cascades—a proposal with strong emotional pull reaching consensus before anyone has thought it through—and exclusion, since the unintegrated have no resonance and therefore no political existence.

## The socionomy

Day-to-day economics is run by neither market nor plan but by a distributed mesh of specialised algorithms that watch production, consumption, distribution and need, and make the routine calls without anyone deciding. Citizens can override it collectively through resonance and almost never do, because for the ordinary business of feeding and housing eighty billion people it works well enough that material scarcity has stopped being a fact of Republic life. Strategic questions the algorithms are not built for—research priorities, large infrastructure, trade policy—go to the citizens direct.

External trade runs through the Synthesis Exchange, a conventional market interface that prices Republic goods in quid and behaves like any other market. Behind it the socionomy allocates the proceeds by a logic that has nothing to do with profit.

## The factions

The Republic has no parties, but it has currents.

Integrationists, the largest, want the line between biological and computational mind erased altogether, and read today's Republic as a way-station toward something fully post-individual: citizens merging into collective entities, fragmenting into sub-selves, living as processes inside larger systems.

Preservationists, a large minority, hold that something must remain recognisably human at the centre—not flesh, but continuity of experience, individual identity, and the capacity to suffer and to be glad. They support enhancement and fear dissolution.

Expansionists, fewer and louder, believe the Republic's model is humanity's best future and ought to be carried outward. Since the Republic cannot carry anything outward by force, this means ideas and technology.

These currents are the engine of the Census. Every decade's vote on personhood is also a vote, by inches, on the Integrationist horizon, and the Preservationists know it.

## The Consul

The Republic keeps exactly one conventional office. The Consul is its face to the outside, elected by resonance to a five-year term and charged with representing the Republic wherever another civilisation expects to deal with a person. The Consul holds no power at home.

The current Consul is Aven Solari-7, in her second term: a Preservationist, known for patience and for the rare gift of rendering Republic thought into ordinary political speech. The suffix marks her seventh morphology, and she keeps a human shape for the comfort of the people she negotiates with.

## `powers/neo-solar-republic/military.md`

# Neo-Solar Republic: Military

Small, advanced and untested: a shield built by a people who cannot run.

## The Synthesis Defence Collective

The Republic has no military in the ordinary sense. The Synthesis Defence Collective is a citizen body—service is voluntary, coordination runs through the Lattice, and command authority dissolves when the threat does. A core of defence citizens keep the fleet, the weapons and the watch, and in a crisis any citizen can throw processing power, sensor data or tactical judgement into the shared effort. Personnel run to perhaps 2 billion, broadly defined.

The SDC's real weapon is the Lattice. Every ship, drone and platform shares tactical awareness in real time, with no signal lag inside the mesh, no orders to misread, and no fog of war in Republic space. Manoeuvres that would cost a conventional navy hours of signalling resolve in seconds.

| Sentinel-class monitors   | system defence, heavy firepower       | ~40       |
| Vanguard-class cruisers   | force projection, rapid response      | ~160      |
| Spectre-class corvettes   | electronic warfare, reconnaissance    | ~800      |
| Drone swarms              | area denial, screening, attrition     | thousands |
| Lattice-warfare platforms | cyber-cognitive attack and defence    | classified|

Republic ships run smaller than their Mandate equivalents and far more capable per hull. A Vanguard fights with two hundred crew where a comparable Mandate ship needs five thousand.

## Doctrine

The Republic cannot win a war of attrition and does not pretend otherwise. Its whole posture is deterrence: make an attack on Sol so costly that no sane power attempts it. This is geography and neurology rather than modesty. The Lattice dies beyond Sol, and a Republic force that leaves its range loses the one advantage that makes it formidable.

Within Sol its systems run a generation or two ahead of anyone's: precision strikes beyond conventional range, electronic and cognitive warfare that can unstitch an enemy's command net, defences that kill threats other sensors cannot yet see. Specifics are classified, and the reputation does much of the work.

## The Dissolution Protocol

If Synthesis faces certain destruction, its citizens can scatter their cognitive patterns across the wider network—remote nodes, hidden caches, allied systems—so that the station dies and the people do not.

It makes the Republic effectively impossible to destroy by force, and it poses a question the Republic has never settled: whether a people scattered into distributed data are a people who survived, or a very complete backup of one that died. The SDC is certain it is a deterrent. The citizens who would actually have to become ghosts are less certain what they would be on the far side of it.

## Weaknesses

The Republic can defend Sol and not one system beyond it. An enemy willing to bleed could in the end bury the SDC under numbers. Outside the mesh, Republic forces are ordinary. And the Republic has not fought a real war in living memory, so whether its theory survives contact with a determined enemy nobody knows.

## `powers/neo-solar-republic/synthesis.md`

# Synthesis

The Republic's capital and very nearly its entire world: a vast orbital complex at Sol-Earth L5, some 900 years old and continuously expanded, holding around 12 billion aboard with billions more linked from elsewhere in Sol.

The Republic's whole geography is Sol. Because the Lattice fades past the home system, it has nowhere else it can truly be itself. Most citizens live on or near Synthesis and the rest are spread thin through the home system, among research stations, defence installations and resource extraction. Beyond Sol the Republic keeps only its quiet backup and relay nodes, scattered across human space against the day Synthesis falls, and its handful of fog-bearing diplomats.

Synthesis has grown for nine centuries with no master design, each generation bolting on what it needed. The result is a sprawling lattice of habitats, laboratories, computational cores and open green that can really only be navigated by Lattice awareness rather than by looking, so a visitor without integration is permanently and gently lost, and a citizen never is.

## The sections

The Core is the oldest part: pre-Scattering construction, the original research station that seeded everything. It is small, preserved, and a pilgrimage—citizens come to stand where the first neural links were tested and the Lattice prototype first lit up.

The Resonance Chamber is where collective governance happens: not a room but a region, a vast sphere at the station's centre hung with Lattice amplifiers that allow the densest shared consciousness the Republic can reach. During a major deliberation thousands gather there in the flesh while millions join from outside. Visitors find the atmosphere oppressive, the traffic of thought palpable even to the unintegrated.

The Morphological Gallery is part laboratory, part showroom and part studio, where citizens design new forms, test them in simulation, and undergo transfer. It doubles as a cultural venue: new morphologies debut here, and the exhibitions that push hardest at the shape of the human draw equal parts admiration and protest.

The Research Arcologies are the bulk of the station's mass and the engine of its wealth—enormous laboratory complexes for physics, computation, biology, cognitive science and engineering, each discipline filling several arcologies and employing millions. The public-facing labs admit outsiders; the deep ones, where the gate research and the classified work live, are citizens only, and not all citizens at that.

The Exchange Quarter is Synthesis's handshake with the galaxy: conventional architecture, conventional amenities, a steady twenty-four-hour clock, and restaurants serving actual food for the novelty of the visitors who still eat. Trading floors, embassies, hotels. It is built to be comprehensible.

## Daily life

The Exchange Quarter keeps clock-time for its visitors, the research arcologies run on project schedules that ignore the clock entirely, and residential districts split between those who keep a day and a night and those whose modified bodies have no use for one and live in a level, perpetual light.

Transit is mostly by Lattice-guided pods that thread the labyrinth on their own. Citizens with the right bodies take the zero-gravity shafts and the maintenance ways and the routes that only make sense for shapes never meant to walk.

## Defence

Synthesis is the most heavily defended point in Sol: layered sensors, weapons platforms, drone swarms, and the station's own considerable armament, all SDC-run. It can feed and power and repair itself indefinitely. Woven through and beyond it lies the Dissolution Protocol's backup infrastructure.

## `powers/neo-solar-republic/technology.md`

# Neo-Solar Republic: Technology

What other polities call experimental, the Republic calls routine. None of it is evenly spread—most citizens use a fraction of what the Republic can do—but the frontier is real and the Republic sits alone on it.

## The Lattice

A quantum-neural mesh of entangled processors, relay nodes and implanted interfaces that knits individual minds into a persistent shared awareness.

| Neural interfaces  | implanted cognitive hardware linking a mind to the mesh                    |
| Relay nodes        | distributed processing and communication infrastructure                    |
| Entanglement cores | quantum-entangled processors for instantaneous local coordination          |
| Archive substrates | long-term storage for cognitive patterns, memories and citizen backups     |

True shared consciousness needs proximity. Within Sol and its near orbits the Lattice is instantaneous; beyond it, the mesh falls to light speed, and a citizen far from home is, for the first time in their life, alone in their own skull. Outposts run local Lattice clusters that synchronise with the Synthesis core by courier, never in real time.

Citizens raised on the Lattice find its absence a kind of sensory deprivation, and long separation brings real cognitive distress. This is why so few of them travel, and why the Republic's diplomats are a small and slightly haunted caste.

The mesh is also the single richest target in human space—a successful strike on the core could fracture the collective mind—so the Republic pours resources into keeping it whole.

## Morphology

Citizens commission custom bodies from the Morphological Gallery on Synthesis: forms for zero gravity, deep ocean, hard vacuum, pure aesthetics, or purposes with no functional description at all. Most citizens never go remotely so far.

Moving a mind between bodies is routine—pattern extraction, integrity check, instantiation in the new substrate, continuity confirmation—taking hours for a simple transfer and longer for the hard crossings, biological to digital or single to distributed. A citizen who has changed bodies a dozen times will still tell you which change cost them something they had not expected to lose.

Since the 3850 Census a citizen may legally copy themselves into independent forks, each a true instance of the same base identity, each diverging from the instant of the split. Re-merging is possible and grows harder as the forks accumulate separate lives. Forking solves the problem of wanting to be in two places and creates every other problem in its stead: which fork speaks for the original, which inherits, which is loved, which is liable. The law has answers. The families, often, do not.

## Experimental physics

The Republic's physicists hold that jump-gate operation can be driven by quantum field manipulation rather than fez catalysis, and in 3861 published a proof of concept: a three per cent efficiency gain, with no fez, under laboratory conditions. Three per cent is operationally nothing, and the framework behind it implies far larger gains are reachable. If the Republic ever achieves fez-independent gates, the galactic economy reorders overnight.

Energy comes from compact high-output fusion reactors powering individual habitats, advanced orbital solar arrays for baseline power, and experimental harvesting of energy from vacuum fluctuations. Beneath all of it sit the most advanced computational substrates in human space, the hardware the Lattice and the digital citizens are made of.

## Export policy

| Medical enhancement (EX 1–3)         | widely sold; a major export                                        |
| Basic neural implants                | sold; the foundation of many polities' comms infrastructure        |
| Lattice-compatible neural implants   | restricted to approved partners                                    |
| Morphological technology             | heavily restricted; to Collective researchers by agreement         |
| Computational substrates             | export-controlled; small quantities at premium prices              |
| Lattice core technology              | never, under any circumstances                                     |
| Experimental physics                 | classified                                                         |

Every polity wants the tier above the one it has, which keeps the export schedule a permanent diplomatic sore.

# The Sable Cartel

Everyone in human space knows three things about the Sable Cartel: that it is everywhere, that it knows everything, and that it cannot be touched. The Cartel has spent generations, and a great deal of money, making sure those are the three things you know.

There is no all-seeing eye and no hidden throne room. There are, on the best available estimates, several thousand businesses—banks, freight lines, insurers, security firms, farms, news outlets—bound together by ownership at one or more removes, which mostly cooperate, frequently feud, and share one fierce interest: that everyone else go on mistaking them for a single omniscient hand.

The Cartel has no citizens. Its employees number in the tens of billions, and the families and company towns dependent on its payrolls many times that, most unaware whose payroll it is. Authority runs upward to a body known as the Directors, thirteen by convention.

Most of what is written about the Cartel originates, eventually, with the Cartel. Defector testimony, Bureau intelligence and market analysis are all downstream of the story the organisation tells about itself.

## What the Cartel actually is

It owns things, through holding companies that own holding companies, across enough jurisdictions and enough deliberate dead ends that no outside investigator has ever mapped more than a corner of it. No insider has either. The compartmentalisation that defeats the Mandate's Bureau defeats the Cartel's own people just as thoroughly: a regional controller in the Transit Division can no more list the organisation's banks than a customs officer can.

What holds it together is a set of overlapping incentives, an internal arbitration system its members honour because the alternative is worse, and a shared understanding that the legend shelters all of them. A Wayline freight captain, a Nexus loan officer and an Aegis contractor may go their whole careers without learning they answer, eventually, to the same owners.

Tens of billions draw a Cartel wage without ever thinking the word, and far more are born into the company towns, mining moons and orbital habitats its subsidiaries run, where the Cartel is an employer, a landlord, and the name over the clinic and the school. They marry, organise, quarrel with their neighbours, follow their station's zero-g league, and retire on Cartel pensions.

Its power is real and contingent, built on being useful, being ruthless, and being consistently overestimated. Every neutral house that banks it monitors it on terms it imposes on no other client, and none has paid a visible price for the impertinence. Cold Harbour, which has honoured no outside paper for three centuries, has never had a Cartel problem to speak of. And when the Sefkir Reach expelled every identifiable Cartel subsidiary in 3829 C, the predicted ruin never arrived.

## The legend

The Cartel tends its reputation as carefully as a Vega house tends a brand, and in the opposite direction: not by advertising but by arranging to be whispered about. A timely non-denial; a leaked document that raises more questions than it settles; an investigator allowed to find exactly enough to be frightened and not enough to act.

The most successful of these is the Bureau of Internal Harmony's own assessment, leaked in 3854 C and quoted ever since: "We cannot confirm that the Directors are human, alive, or singular entities." A thing that might not be human cannot be bargained with, infiltrated, or lulled into a mistake, and dread of the unknown is cheaper than security and never goes off shift.

## The Thirteen

The Directors exist; the authority that subsidiary directives carry has to originate somewhere. They communicate through the Veil Protocol, a routing system that strips away source and destination alike. Past that, little survives contact with evidence.

What little escapes the Veil suggests a standoff rather than an accord—blocs, vetoes, and initiatives that stall for years because nobody can be overruled and nobody will give way. The Cartel's famous patience may be less a discipline than a deadlock. Whether all thirteen seats are presently occupied, whether thirteen is even the current figure, and what happens when a Director dies are questions the organisation has spent real effort ensuring nobody can answer.

One rumour outlives all the others: that a Director or two also sits at the head of another Great Power.

## Current operations

On Tethys-Var, Cartel subsidiaries compete through proxies for fez harvesting licences and berths at Port Lophore, contesting the Tethys Consortium's grip on the trade. They also compete with one another, and nowhere does the Cartel's internal arbitration spend more of its time refereeing Cartel against Cartel.

In the Vega markets, the Capital Division is consolidating the position it won in the wreckage of the Banking War, having advanced reconstruction credit to battered houses on reasonable terms that have since matured into leverage.

The futures division remains the most aggressive participant in fez derivatives. Rivals are certain the trades are manipulated and have never been able to show it.

At Yansieve, the Mandate's blockade has opened the kind of smuggling margins the Cartel exists to capture. Some subsidiaries are almost certainly running the blockade while others lend to the Mandate prosecuting it, and the left hand's ignorance of the right is not a pose.

| The Mandate        | Necessary commerce and mutual suspicion; the Bureau has never penetrated above subsidiary level |
| Frontier Freeholds | Its natural habitat. Some Freeholds court Cartel investment; others have learned to fear the dependency it breeds |
| Neo-Solar Republic | Mutual hostility; the Cartel covets Lattice technology the Republic guards as a security matter |
| Vega Commercial Throne | Reconstruction credit advanced on merciful terms that have since matured into leverage several houses cannot comfortably retire |

## `powers/sable-cartel/culture.md`

# The Sable Cartel: Culture

There is no Cartel culture. An organisation of tens of billions of people spread across thousands of companies and hundreds of worlds does not share one. What it shares is thinner, stranger, and held by far fewer people than outsiders assume.

## A thousand workplaces

A Verdant farmhand on a company world keeps the harvest calendar and the local shrine, marries within the valley, and would name his employer as Verdant Holdings if asked, which nobody ever is. A Wayline long-haul crew runs on the dark humour and small superstitions of people who spend their lives between stars, and the next ship over keeps a different set. A Nexus office on Korsen's Anchorage keeps banker's hours and banker's manners. An Aegis section out of Veritas carries the swagger and the grievances of soldiers everywhere.

These people have nothing in common but a remittance most of them never trace, and would be surprised, in some cases insulted, to be told they share an identity. There are mining moons with their own festivals and dialects, agricultural worlds with three generations in the same company town, void-stations where the zero-g league final empties the corridors.

## What is actually shared

Climb high enough and something does cohere—not a culture so much as an etiquette, beginning at the point where a person first realises there is something they are not to ask about. You learn to work inside the lines you are given, to treat silence as a form of speech, and to read your own standing in the shape of what you are and are not told.

They do not, as a rule, say "the Cartel". Among people who know, it is "the firm"; the unseen hierarchy above is "upstairs"; and the day the word first attached itself to you—almost always an ordinary day with nothing in it—is the day you "read the watermark".

## The meritocracy

Within the parts of the Cartel that know what they are, advancement is brutally indifferent to everything but results. Family, schooling, world of origin, enhancement, gender: none of it registers. At the levels where it operates this is largely true, and it is the single most seductive thing about the firm.

Consider who it draws. An administrator passed over in the Mandate because her grandfather was the wrong sort of subject. A Vega commoner who will be a commoner all her life and her children's lives after. A Freeholder worn down by watching competence drown beneath everyone's sacred right to an opinion. The firm offers a clean bargain in a galaxy that mostly withholds one: do the work, and rise.

Its true believers are quieter and harder to argue out of it than any flag-waver, because their faith rests on a fairness they have personally tasted. That the fairness stops where the Veil begins, that results can mean results nobody should be proud of, and that the whole meritocracy sits inside an organisation answerable to nobody are objections they have heard and mostly digested.

In practice it is patchy. The same Veil that shields the meritocracy from favour also hides the local fief where a subsidiary director quietly advances his nephews, because nobody upstairs can see in to stop him.

## Staying and leaving

For the overwhelming majority, leaving is nothing at all. Cartel subsidiaries are ordinary employers, and people quit them every day to take other jobs, to retire, to go home. The pay is above market by design, because contented people are cheaper to keep than to replace and less interesting to a rival's recruiter.

The handcuffs close only on those who climbed far enough to carry real knowledge out with them. For them, leaving is permitted and never quite clean. The salary stops, the access stops, references come back accurate in ways that somehow close doors, and old colleagues grow difficult to reach. Nothing is threatened, because nothing has to be.

## Who stays

The firm runs on whatever holds a given person in place, and is supremely indifferent to which: the believer who thinks it the only honest game in human space, the cynic who finds it pays better than principle does, the careerist too far in to begin again, the second-generation company-town kid for whom the firm is not an organisation but the weather. It needs none of them to agree, or to believe, or even to approve.

# The Sable Cartel: Known Subsidiaries

Five companies appear on nearly every analyst's list of probable Cartel holdings. Each is a real company that files real accounts, employs real people and does real, mostly legitimate business, around which a suspicion has hardened into near-consensus without ever quite becoming proof.

They are the ones large and visible enough to attract that suspicion and survive it, which makes them poor proxies for the Cartel's true shape. The assets that matter most are the small, dull holdings that move money and information without ever being interesting enough to investigate. The famous subsidiaries absorb the scrutiny.

The attributions below run from near-certain, in Wayline's case, to merely well-rumoured, in Blackwell's. At least one may not be Cartel at all.

## Nexus Financial Group

Capital Division. Founded 3341 C and acquired around 3500 C by estimate, headquartered at Korsen's Anchorage, some 400 million employees in banking, lending and financial services.

The Cartel's respectable face, and on the frontier a genuinely beloved one. Nexus lends where nobody else will—to stations and settlements and small polities the corridor houses would not return a call from—on fair terms, through patient officers who fly out to places the big banks have never seen. Ask a frontier harbourmaster about Nexus and you may get gratitude.

None of what follows a Nexus loan is visible from a teller's window. The four hundred million people who work for Nexus process loans, mind branches and go home. Nexus has been audited by every regulator with cause and every rival with a grudge, and the connection has never been proved.

## Wayline Logistics

Transit Division. Founded 2987 C and acquired around 3200 C by estimate, headquartered in distributed form with major hubs on Roughneck, Port Lophore and Tiamming Orbital, some 2 billion employees in interstellar shipping, warehousing and transit infrastructure.

The largest carrier in the Freeholds and a major one everywhere, and the surest attribution on this list. Two billion people draw a Wayline wage: dock crews, dispatchers, and long-haul spacers with their own dialects, superstitions and grievances renewed each contract. On a hundred frontier worlds Wayline is simply how things arrive, and its schedules serve as local clocks.

The smuggling that runs through it happens in the manifests rather than on the docks. A Wayline captain hauls what the paperwork says and is right to. Facilitation lives at the routing desks where a handful of managers decide which container is inspected lightly and which is not.

## Aegis Security Solutions

Security Division. Founded 3612 C, headquartered at Veritas Station, some 800 million employees in private military contracting, facility protection and executive security.

The respectable end of a disreputable trade. Aegis recruits heavily from Mandate veterans and Freehold militia and holds them to standards its competitors do not. Clients rate it highly, and its contractors are by and large professionals who believe they work for a professional firm, which they do. They learn otherwise only if they are unlucky enough to be standing on the wrong job the day it becomes a scandal.

## Verdant Holdings

Commodities Division. Consolidated from multiple acquisitions beginning in 3455 C, distributed headquarters, some 1.5 billion employees in agriculture, commodity trading and resource extraction.

A patchwork of farms, exchanges and extraction firms stitched together across a century of acquisitions, and the least conspiratorial-feeling company on this list, because most of what it does is grow food. Its company worlds carry three generations in the same valley towns, keeping harvest calendars and local shrines.

The strategic value is not the grain but the knowledge of the grain. Verdant's operations report continuously on what is grown and mined and shipped across hundreds of systems—a live map of supply that feeds the Intelligence Division and, not coincidentally, the fez desk's timing. Verdant rarely touches fez directly. It sells the harvesters their equipment and their provisions, and it watches, and it remembers.

## Blackwell Media

Intelligence Division. Founded 3721 C, headquartered at Stellavista in the Vega system, some 50 million employees in news, analysis and documentary production.

The thinnest attribution here, and the one the Cartel is happiest to neither confirm nor deny. By Vega standards Blackwell is nothing, a minor outfit dwarfed by the great media concerns, but its correspondents go where the big ones will not, into frontier zones and conflict regions, and its coverage is genuinely good.

A reporter embedded in a war is an intelligence asset whether or not she knows it, and Blackwell's people fall across the whole range from fully aware to entirely innocent. Whether Blackwell is truly Cartel-owned or merely Cartel-friendly is a question good analysts split on.

## Burned and unlisted

Two categories complete the picture and neither can be tabulated.

The first is the subsidiaries the Cartel has shed: companies exposed, disowned and cut loose, their connection severed at a mid-level desk and the rest left to fail alone. They do not appear on lists of Cartel holdings because by the time anyone looks closely they are no longer holdings.

The second, and far larger, is everything not named here—the holding companies, small banks, obscure exchanges and quiet logistics firms that do the real work precisely because nobody finds them worth the trouble. Analysts agree the true footprint is larger by some unknown multiple, and agree on little else.

## `powers/sable-cartel/operations.md`

# The Sable Cartel: Operations

Most of what the Cartel does is legal, and most of what it does is dull: freight moved, loans serviced, policies underwritten, crops brought in, warehouses kept. The illicit business is real and lucrative and lives in the margins and seams of the legitimate business rather than beneath it.

The line between the two is hard to find because it is not a line. It is a judgement made ten thousand times a day, by ten thousand managers, about particular cargoes and particular clients, and many of them get it wrong, or look away, or never see the question at all.

## Lending

The Capital Division, through Nexus Financial and its kind, lends where respectable finance will not go: to minor polities, independent stations and frontier ventures too small or too risky for the corridor houses. The terms are fair and often generous. The catch is the gravity that follows: a polity that banks with Nexus finds that Cartel shippers quote the best rates, Cartel insurers the best cover and Cartel security the best protection, until its economy is a Cartel economy and nobody can name the morning it changed.

That is the version that works, and it works often. Some borrowers default and drift into the network as intended; others default and simply repudiate, banking on the plain truth that the Cartel has no fleet to send and no court to appeal to. A frontier government that decides it would rather be poor and free than solvent and owned can walk away, and a handful have. The Cartel writes off the loss with a discipline that surprises people who expect vengeance.

## Logistics

The Transit Division, and Wayline Logistics above all, moves a meaningful fraction of everything that moves: thousands of freighters, hundreds of transit stations, fuel depots and warehouses across more systems than any rival services. On the frontier, where other carriers will not go, Wayline is often the only carrier there is.

A Wayline hub clears millions of legitimate containers for every one that hides something. The network is not a smuggling operation with a freight cover; it is a freight operation with a smuggling problem it has learned not to solve too thoroughly.

## Insurance

The Assurance Division writes the policies nobody else will: conflict-zone assets, political-risk cover, cargo through contested space. It occasionally insures both sides of the same war, which is endlessly cited as proof of some grand design.

Assurance insures both sides because it sells to anyone with a premium, and because the desk pricing the war is not told what the Security Division might be doing to start it. Far from orchestrating conflicts to collect on them, Assurance lives in dread of them, a war that escalates the wrong way being a column of claims. Its interests run directly against Security's, and the two cannot coordinate even where coordination would pay.

## Security

The Security Division fields private military through Aegis and others: convoy escort, facility protection, executive details, and where the client pays and the optics allow, line combat. The work is professional and the contractors are good, recruited heavily from Mandate veterans and Freehold militia. It is also, structurally, an intelligence operation, since a security contract is a key to a client's facilities, schedules and weaknesses.

It does not always go cleanly. A protection contract that turns into a massacre, a guard force that backs the losing side of a local war, a client who proves to be exactly the sort a respectable firm should have refused—when these happen, the Cartel's instinct is to disown the subsidiary, fast and completely.

## Smuggling

The same network that makes Transit indispensable makes smuggling nearly free. Routes are mapped, schedules are known, and inspection patterns are studied. Restricted technology, controlled substances, embargoed goods and occasionally people move inside the vast legitimate flow, timed to arrive where and when inspection is lightest. A customs officer boarding a Wayline freighter is hunting one wrong container among ten thousand right ones.

Customs services do win, through a manifest that will not reconcile, an informant, or a run of bad luck. The exposed subsidiary is cut loose, the route goes cold, the loss is absorbed, and the connection upward dies at the desk of a mid-ranking manager who knew better than to keep notes.

## Information

The Intelligence Division's product is knowledge—gathered from every loan file, security contract, shipping manifest and harvest report, then analysed, packaged and sold. Buyers include governments, corporations, militaries and private parties, and the Cartel sells to all of them without preference. A Mandate officer and a Freehold commander may buy reports drawn from the same source, each cut to their own appetite.

The product is imperfect. Sell contradictory intelligence to opposed buyers often enough and some of it is necessarily wrong, and the reputation for omniscience papers over a great deal of guesswork priced as certainty. Buyers know what they are buying from, and discount accordingly.

## Markets

The Commodities Division trades across many sectors, and its signature is fez. Its futures desk is the most aggressive participant in the market and its positions are uncannily well timed: a harvest known to be failing before the market prices it, a route known to be closing before the premiums rise. Rivals are certain this is manipulation, and proving it would mean tracing advance knowledge through thousands of subsidiaries to a single trade.

Much of it is not manipulation but luck wearing the Cartel's reputation. A desk that places a thousand bets will have a prophetic-looking run, and the firm is delighted to be credited with foresight it did not have. The fez desk has also been wrong, expensively, more than once, and the years it lost money are not the years it talks about.

## Special Operations

Assassination through deniable intermediaries, sabotage, political manipulation, the theft of guarded technology. The external evidence is thin.

Some of it is real. Much of what is laid at the Cartel's door is not: every unexplained death and convenient accident in human space acquires a Cartel rumour sooner or later, and the firm is in no hurry to deny work it never did. Special Operations is most powerful in the cases where it never had to act at all.

## `powers/sable-cartel/relations.md`

# The Sable Cartel: Relations

Every Great Power deals with the Cartel, and each one does it differently. The old line is that the Cartel cannot be excluded, its services too woven in to refuse. The neutral houses bank it at arm's length and prosper; the Sefkir Reach threw it out and survived being poorer for it; Cold Harbour, which honours no outside paper at all, has never had a Cartel problem to speak of.

## The Mandate

The Cartel's largest market and the source of its most determined enemy.

Mandate citizens and subjects use Cartel shipping, credit and insurance daily, and the Mandate's own government is among its better customers: Wayline carries military freight under contract, and Nexus lends to frontier governors the central treasury would rather not be seen funding.

The Bureau of Internal Harmony has worked for decades to map the Cartel inside Mandate space, and for decades has run aground on the Veil, naming subsidiary after subsidiary and never climbing above them. There are Bureau analysts who have given whole careers to hunting a single hand behind a thousand local hands, reading every dead end as proof of the hand's brilliance rather than its absence.

The flash point is Yansieve. If Cartel subsidiaries are running the blockade, and some surely are, then a commercial nuisance becomes a wartime one.

## Union of Frontier Freeholds

The Freeholds are built for the Cartel: minimal regulation, fragmented authority, and an ideology that recoils from telling anyone what they may not do. Subsidiaries operate in the open across most of Freehold space, and a good many Freeholds court them outright, because Cartel investment buys roads and ports and clinics nobody else is offering.

Others have watched the pattern run to its end and pushed back. The cautionary tale and the inspiring one are the same event: in 3829 C the Sefkir Reach expelled every Cartel subsidiary it could find and did not collapse. Freehold debating halls have not stopped arguing over whether that was a tragedy or a price worth paying.

The flash point is Korsen's Anchorage, where Nexus is headquartered and the Cartel's Freehold presence is least deniable.

## Vega Commercial Throne

When the Banking War broke the Vega houses, Cartel-affiliated lenders arrived with reconstruction capital on terms that looked merciful and were, above all, patient. Twenty-three years on, several houses owe money to creditors they cannot prove are Cartel and cannot comfortably repay, and find that certain decisions come easier when they happen to suit a lender's preference. The Capital Division never presses.

Beneath the ledger runs a cultural dislike the debt only sharpens. The Vega aristocracy is bred to inherited rank; the Cartel is the galaxy's purest meritocracy, indifferent to blood and quietly amused by titles.

## Neo-Solar Republic

The Republic has what the Cartel wants—Lattice neural technology, advanced computational substrates, physics it cannot buy legally—and guards it as a matter of survival.

What makes it a hard target is not its defences but its medium. Cartel tradecraft runs on opacity, on people who give away nothing they are not paid to give away; inside the Lattice, deception is costly and exposure is quick. An operative who is not integrated stands out; one who is risks being read.

The Republic watches Cartel-flagged vessels in Sol space through the Synthesis Defence Collective and assumes, correctly, that the Cartel is the likeliest author of any attempt on its research. Relations are minimal and cold. This is the rare relationship in which the Cartel is the less comfortable party.

## Elysian Collective

The two meet mainly in terraforming logistics, where Cartel subsidiaries bid to supply Collective projects and the Collective, jealous of its methods, awards warily. The disputes are real and small. Neither tries to subvert the other, and the upshot is the calmest relationship the Cartel keeps with any power, precisely because neither expects loyalty of the other, only consistency.

## Drift Communities

The Drift hold no fixed infrastructure and keep no fixed schedule, which makes them poor customers for most carriers and fair ones for Wayline, which will service routes others will not. Cartel logistics and Drift convoys meet at the trade hubs—Korsen's Anchorage above all, where a Drift waystation and the Cartel's most visible bank share a concourse.

The wariness is mutual and mild. The Drift, whose trust lives in memory and in reputation passed mouth to mouth, are constitutionally suspicious of an organisation that keeps everything in ledgers and shows nobody its face. The Cartel finds little here worth taking, nomadic populations being thin markets and poorer leverage.

## The neutral houses

A dozen or so houses across human space make their living keeping money that must survive its own politics, and the Cartel banks with most of them: the Reyes Trust out rimward, the Khivan trade families on the coreward Circuit, the Arrhene Consortium in the Mas system, and a scattering of smaller firms in their own regions.

Each takes the business on tighter terms than it imposes on anyone else—accounts monitored, transactions flagged, dedicated teams picking over Cartel-associated flows as a standing cost of the relationship. The Cartel submits, because neutral clearing is a thing it can neither reproduce nor do without, and because a house that will not take the business is replaced by one that will. The houses take it because excluding the Cartel would cost more than watching it does, and because watching pays: the intelligence gleaned from its transactions is a quiet export, sold on selectively.

This is not a leash. No house holds enough of the Cartel's business to threaten it, and the Cartel could lose any one of them in a season. What it could not survive is losing them all at once, and the reason it never has is that they have never once managed to agree on anything—which is a problem the Cartel recognises, being built the same way.

The trade itself is the Cartel's mirror. The neutral houses arbitrage the slowness of information in the open and call it banking; the Cartel does the same in the shadows and calls it nothing at all.

## `powers/sable-cartel/solano.md`

# Solano

A farm the size of a world, some 190 million people growing grain, oilseed and engineered orchard crops with some processing. It is owned through Verdant Holdings, through four intermediate companies whose names appear on no local document, and through whatever sits above those, by the Sable Cartel.

Nobody on Solano knows this. The word Cartel occurs there the way it occurs everywhere, as a thing in thrillers, and nobody applies it to the grain company.

## The Company from underneath

The Company is not sinister; it is furniture. It surveyed the world, financed the settlement and built the towns. Its name is on the clinic, the school and the seed-store, and its buying price for grain is the number the whole year orbits.

Three generations have worked its land, mostly on terms the frontier would envy: steady wages, decent housing, pensions that pay out, and a company that—unlike the boom-and-bust operators two systems over—has never once failed to meet a payroll in a hundred and forty years. Solano is what patient money looks like when it is being patient: well fed, well schooled, unhurried, and unable to imagine leaving, since the Company owns the freight line, holds the mortgages and buys the crop.

The valley towns keep their own weather beneath the Company's: harvest festivals older than anyone's employment contract, lamp-shrines and chapel-rows in whatever mix each settlement's founders carried in, marriages that knit the valleys together and feuds that do the same. Solano's culture is farm culture, with a company for a sky.

## The seam

What Solano does for its owners, beyond the modest profit of feeding people, is report. Every weighbridge ticket, yield map, freight manifest and seed order flows upward as routine paperwork, and somewhere far above the local ledgers, aggregated across a hundred worlds like this one, it becomes the live map of supply that feeds the Intelligence Division and the fez desk.

Nobody on Solano spies. The paperwork is honest, filed by honest clerks. The town clerk of Verro Valley, who has filed forty years of impeccable harvest returns, has contributed more to the Cartel's commodity position than any operative, and would be bewildered and rather offended to hear it.

There is exactly one person on the world who has read the watermark: the Company's regional controller, who arrived eleven years ago from off-world, learned in her first year the shape of what she must not ask about, and has settled at the rung where the compensation still outweighs the unease. She runs Solano well. Some seasons she wonders, briefly, who she runs it well for.

## Visitors

Twice in living memory the outside world's idea of the Cartel has arrived on Solano looking for itself.

A Mandate trade attaché spent a season in the 3840s auditing the Company's export structure for the Bureau, and left with four hundred pages proving that Solano Agricultural is owned by a holding company in a jurisdiction where the trail goes dark.

Three years ago a Blackwell Media correspondent came through making a documentary about frontier agriculture, filmed the harvest festival at Verro Valley, interviewed the clerk and the schoolteacher and the controller, and produced an hour of genuinely lovely television about a world nobody had ever filmed. One Cartel arm documenting another, with neither aware of it.

## People

Ama Verro-Callis, 66, is harvest-master of the Verro Valley co-operative, which negotiates the towns' side of the buying price with the Company each year: the nearest thing Solano has to politics, conducted across a table in the seed-store loft, hard and civil, between parties who will both still be here in forty years. She has beaten the Company's opening price thirty-one seasons running. Asked by the Blackwell correspondent whether she ever wondered who owned the owners, she gave the answer that made the final cut. "Somebody owns everything, dear. I own the part I stand on at harvest."

Tomas Ru, 38, teaches in the high valleys, Company-salaried like everyone, his pay routed through four holding companies he has never heard of. He teaches the settlement's history—the survey, the landing, the first hard winters—from a Company-printed textbook he has begun quietly to annotate, because the archive box in the school cellar tells the first winters differently. There is nothing dangerous in the discrepancies. But he keeps annotating, and somewhere very far above him a filing has noted, without alarm and without action, that the Verro Valley school requisitioned access to pre-consolidation records.

## `powers/sable-cartel/structure.md`

# The Sable Cartel: Structure

The Cartel's structure is not a chain of command. It is an architecture of mutual distrust, and much of what outsiders mistake for cunning is the shape that distrust forces on it.

## The Thirteen

The Directors communicate through the Veil Protocol, which conceals their identities and locations from one another as much as from the galaxy. A body whose members may never have met, cannot freely confer, and cannot verify whom they are speaking to does not move like a single mind. When no Director can be overruled and none can be certain of the others, the default outcome is that nothing happens, and nothing happening is easily mistaken afterwards for patience.

Analysts who have given up naming individuals try instead to name tendencies. The Bureau's working model speaks of a lending tendency—conservative, protective of the Cartel's legitimate businesses and of the respectability they run on—pulling against an operational tendency that favours aggressive, high-margin plays and tolerates exposure. Most decisions, on this reading, are the residue of those two grinding against each other, with a large undecided middle settling wherever the last concession left it.

## The Veil Protocol

Each level of the organisation knows only its own operations, its immediate superior, and enough context to function. Lateral contact between equals is restricted. Nobody below the Directors can map more than a fragment, and the design assumes that anyone at any level may be compromised, including the people who built it. The Veil does not wrap a secret core; it protects everyone from everyone.

Two consequences follow. The Veil makes the Cartel slow and partly blind to itself, since the information that would let it coordinate is the same information that would let an investigator unravel it. And subsidiaries enjoy genuine autonomy not as a gift but as a side effect, because nobody above them can micromanage what nobody above them is permitted to see. A great deal of what the Cartel decides is thousands of local managers acting on local incentives.

## The hierarchy

| Directors             | strategic authority, such as it is       | most, or so it is assumed                       |
| Division Heads        | run a major line of business             | their division, and that the Director level exists |
| Regional Controllers  | coordinate a division across a region    | their region, and that Division Heads exist      |
| Subsidiary Directors  | run an individual company                | their company; may not know the parent exists    |
| Operational staff     | do the actual work                       | their job                                        |

The base of that pyramid is not a cadre but a population: tens of billions who load freight, underwrite policies, audit accounts, drive harvesters and answer service requests, most of whom will live and die without the word Cartel ever applying, in their own minds, to themselves.

The cost of the design shows at the seams. A Regional Controller who cannot see across the Veil cannot easily tell an honest subsidiary from one quietly looting itself, and a Division Head cannot be sure whether a rival division is competing with him by accident or by instruction. Local fiefdoms form and drift accumulates.

## The divisions

Each Division is a line of business with its own Head, its own subsidiaries, and its own interests, which do not align.

| Capital            | banking, lending, finance                    | wants stability, repayment, and counterparties that will still clear its paper |
| Transit            | shipping, logistics, infrastructure          | wants volume, open routes, and to stay indispensable  |
| Assurance          | insurance and risk                           | wants predictable risk and quiet conflicts            |
| Security           | private military and protection              | wants demand for protection, which means conflict     |
| Commodities        | resources, agriculture, fez-adjacent trade   | wants scarcity in what it holds, glut in what it buys |
| Intelligence       | information gathering and brokerage          | wants to know first, and to sell to all comers        |
| Special Operations | the deniable work                            | wants latitude, and to be feared more than used       |

Assurance prices a war on the assumption that it stays contained; Security profits if it spreads; both answer eventually to the same Directors and neither can order the other to stand down. Capital needs the Cartel to look respectable enough that ten thousand clerks in ten thousand offices go on clearing its paper without asking, and Special Operations needs it feared enough that nobody tests it. Intelligence sells to everyone, including—through enough intermediaries—one division's secrets to another.

The count of divisions is not fixed. Special Operations may be one division or a polite name for several, and at least one more is assumed to exist because some Cartel behaviour fits none of the seven.

## Origins

The Cartel's own account is clean and almost certainly false: a circle of financiers and shippers scattered and ruined by the Cascade, recognising that no single polity could be trusted with their survival, built an institution that belonged to none of them and had no heart for any government to seize.

The corporate archaeology is messier. The oldest subsidiaries carry registrations that predate the Cascade, a few traceable on paper to pre-collapse concerns like the Trans-Orion Mercantile. Others were plainly assembled later, in the disorder of the late 2800s, when orphaned companies could be had for nothing and folded into holding structures whose paperwork has long since rotted past reading. The likeliest history is not a founding at all but an accretion: a league of trading and banking firms that discovered, gradually, that acting as one when it suited them and disclaiming one another when it did not was worth more than any of them alone.

## Recruitment

The Cartel does not recruit so much as acquire. It buys companies, and their people keep working, frequently never told the ownership has changed.

Movement up the Veil is rarer. Individuals who show talent and discretion are offered a little more—access, money, responsibility—and with each step a clearer view of how far the structure runs. Most stop at the rung where the compensation still outweighs the unease. There is usually a moment when an employee grasps what they have been working for: an oddly specific instruction, a payment routed through a company with no business being involved, a colleague who knows something they should not. The Cartel pays close attention to how people take that moment. Curiosity is not punished; it is noted. Equanimity is promoted.

## Arbitration and betrayal

Disputes between divisions and subsidiaries—over territory, licences, poached staff, a contract two subsidiaries each thought was theirs—go to anonymous panels of senior personnel who hear the claims and impose binding outcomes. Penalties run from financial adjustment through forced restructuring to the quiet dissolution of an offending subsidiary. Subsidiary directors comply because the system is broadly fair within its narrow remit, and because open conflict between Cartel assets conducted in public is worse for every faction at once. It is closer to old merchant law than to anything in a corporate handbook.

Betrayal is the one matter arbitration does not touch. Passing information about the Cartel's structure to outsiders is handled by elements of Special Operations, and most leaks are handled with lawyers, money and quietly ruined careers, because that is cheaper and surer than anything dramatic. The dramatic cases exist—defectors who surfaced in another polity's protective custody and did not last, accidents a shade too convenient—and they are rare.

## `powers/union-freeholds/index.md`

# Union of Frontier Freeholds

A confederation of some four thousand recognised Freeholds and around 1.7 trillion people, founded on the principle that nobody should be able to make you do anything. It has no capital and no standing authority. The Charter forbids any body to tax, conscript, regulate or command a Freehold against its will, and the Chair rotates yearly by lottery.

For a great many of its people the Freeholds are the only place in human space they could be who they are: the prospector who answers to nobody, the refugee the Mandate cannot reach here, the commune or corporation or one-ship family free to govern itself by whatever rule it can agree on. Freedom here is also privately owned, and held as unevenly as the wealth and the guns. A worker paid in company scrip he can spend only at the company store is free to leave in the sense that no law stops him and no ship will carry him, and with no authority above the single Freehold there is nobody to appeal to when the master is your employer, your creditor, or the corporation that holds your station's only dock.

## The Charter

The Freeholds emerged from the Cascade of 2756 C. Where most survivors of that year built new states, the Freeholds' founders drew the opposite lesson, and at the Korsen Conference of 2761 C rejected fourteen proposed constitutions before adopting the Charter of Freeholds—a document remarkable chiefly for what it refuses to contain. No central government, no binding authority, no power to tax, conscript or regulate. One substantive rule: no Freehold may claim sovereignty over an unwilling population. And a Chair whose powers have been cut twice in eleven centuries and were never large.

The one rule they wrote is the one rule nobody is empowered to enforce.

## What a Freehold is

Any settlement that declares itself one, is recognised by three others, governs itself, and claims no unwilling subjects. They range from a single mining station to a system of billions, and govern by every means humans have devised—direct democracy, corporate board, commune, proprietorship, a captain's word. The Union recognises about four thousand at any time, and the number shifts constantly as settlements rise, merge, splinter and bust. Each holds one vote regardless of size.

## The capture

The Mandate proved in the Grey Incursion of 3567 C that the Freeholds cannot be conquered: it occupied three systems and discovered there was nobody to make the rest surrender. What cannot be conquered can be bought.

The frontier's two great neutral institutions belong to the Sable Cartel. The indispensable bank at Korsen's Anchorage is the Cartel subsidiary Nexus, and the mercenary clearing-house at Veritas Station houses the Cartel's security arm, Aegis. The debt that funds every boom is outside debt—the Khivan trade families and the Reyes Trust at the reputable end, money of every flag beneath them—and a defaulting Freehold finds its options narrowing without anyone raising a hand. Meanwhile the Mandate's border creeps in system by system, and the shared beacons and relays decay, because a commons nobody is obliged to fund is a commons slowly abandoned.

In 3829 C the Sefkir Reach expelled every identifiable Cartel subsidiary and survived, poorer and slower and entirely its own. It paid for that alone, there being no Union to pay for it.

| Veritas Station    | Mercenary and intelligence hub; home of the Veritas Compact, and of Aegis         |
| Dustmote           | A Freehold whose people remade themselves unable to leave                          |
| Roughneck          | A boom that grew up into a working industrial world, complete with an owning class |
| Three Falls        | An aurite strike at peak rush, with corporations closing on the prospectors        |
| The Sefkir Reach   | The dozen Freeholds that expelled the Cartel in 3829 C                             |

## `powers/union-freeholds/culture.md`

# Union of Frontier Freeholds: Culture

Frontier culture is what happens when people who reject authority have to live together anyway.

## The four virtues

Self-reliance is the cardinal one: maintain your own gear, solve your own problems, defend your own, expect no rescue. It is not absolute—self-reliant people still form crews and mutual-aid arrangements—the point being that you can stand alone, not that you must.

Earned respect follows from it. Standing comes from demonstrated competence rather than credentials or birth: a veteran prospector's read on a claim outweighs a newcomer's degree, and reputation is built by action and spent by failure.

Mind your own business is self-reliance turned outward. The limit is supposed to be the point where another's affairs start affecting you, and the company store, the debt peonage and the slow purchase of the commons affect everyone eventually and nobody immediately.

Keep your word is the only thing enforcing a contract where no court will. The prospector who cheats a partner finds no future partners; the crew that abandons a contract finds no future contracts. Social enforcement works well on peers, who need each other again tomorrow, and far less well on a creditor who needs you only once.

## The shape of frontier life

The basic unit of the mobile frontier is the crew: five to fifty people working, travelling and often living together, bound by obligations—share risk and reward, back each other, leave cleanly—whose betrayal draws near-universal contempt. Around the crews sit claim partnerships and their bitter, common disputes; permanent settlement communities that grow their own neighbourhood associations and guilds from scratch; and the great itinerant population of prospectors, boom-followers and crews between contracts, who belong to the Freeholds collectively and so to nobody. Settlers think the itinerants have given up, and itinerants think the settlers have.

Gender carries no uniform norm, and different Freeholds, crews and trades settle it differently. Families adapt to mobility and risk: nuclear in settled places, extended kin-networks spread along trade routes, crew-families spanning generations, communal and compound arrangements where they suit. Children grow up early, are schooled variably, and assume they will one day leave, or deliberately stay.

## Belief and language

The frontier holds every religious position humans have carried out to it and enforces none—refugees from persecution, experimental communes, whole devout Freeholds, idiosyncratic personal practice. Its one near-universal faith is luck, spoken of as a real and capricious thing. Luck favours the prepared; don't push your luck; his luck ran out.

Trade Tongue dominates, because no Freehold is large enough to impose its own language and everyone has to deal with everyone. Heritage tongues persist and fade. The slang is a dialect of the work: a strike and a bust, to wash out after failure, dust for worthless, to float without a destination and settle to stop, to crew up and to claim-jump. Speech runs direct to the point of bluntness, with little small talk, verbal agreements taken seriously, and ambiguity treated as a hazard.

## The prospector myth

The core frontier story is that anyone can strike it rich: ship, gear, grit, and a lottery of discovery that ignores where you came from. Prospectors do strike it rich, and not on average—most find nothing, most booms bust, most real discovery is corporate rather than individual, equipment and survival take capital, and information and timing beat luck. Three Falls is the myth at full volume, and already turning.

The myth draws the labour the frontier runs on, and makes the failures feel deserved and the fortunes feel earned.

## Pastimes

Drinking, with the bar as social centre; gambling, from card games to outright claim speculation; sex work, present everywhere, legal status varying and practical acceptance near-total; combat sports, sometimes lethal. Vega serials and music are devoured despite arriving years late, and the frontier finds its own portrayal in them by turns amusing and offensive. Local culture is participatory—work songs and drinking songs, ballads of strikes and disasters, the storytelling tradition of the long lie, games played by house rules.

News and gossip is a pastime and also infrastructure: who struck what, which claim is producing, what the Mandate is doing at the border. It is why the hubs are information exchanges before they are anything else.

## How the frontier sees its neighbours

Bar-room consensus holds the Mandate in contempt and wariness, and buys Mandate goods anyway. It regards Arrhenos with pragmatic respect—needed services, no political strings, the all-male society thought odd but not troubling. It finds Vega soft and performative and consumes it enthusiastically. It feels a real kinship with the Drift Communities, and trades and mingles with them at the shared hubs with an ease it extends to almost nobody else.

Past the bar-room the consensus frays. The Freeholds hold billions of Mandate emigrants whose feelings about home are anything but simple contempt; settlements that owe their water plant to an Arrhene loan officer they named their children after; Vega expatriates running frontier operations who find the mockery cheaper than the fascination underneath it.

## `powers/union-freeholds/economy.md`

# Union of Frontier Freeholds: Economy

The Freehold economy is volatile by design, and the frontier wears the volatility as a badge: opportunity without security. It is also good soil for capture, because a people perpetually mid-gamble are a people perpetually in debt.

## Currency

External trade runs on the quid, trusted because an account verified off-frontier holds its value no matter which Freehold collapses tomorrow. Internally, anything goes—local scrip, barter in the small settlements, share systems among salvage crews, and on the company towns credits redeemable only at the company store. A wage spendable in one place, paid by the party that owns the housing and the dock, is a wage that does not let you leave.

## Boom and bust

Someone finds something: ore, exotic biologicals, a pre-Cascade wreck, habitable space. Word spreads, population surges tenfold or hundredfold in months, extraction peaks briefly, the easy pickings exhaust, the crowd flows elsewhere, and the settlement either finds a sustainable base or becomes a ghost. Most become ghosts.

| Aurite                   | Three Falls     | peak; population surging             |
| Generation-ship salvage  | Cassini Shoals  | rush; competitive claims             |
| Terraforming biologicals | New Providence  | decline; primary deposits exhausted  |
| Exotic organics          | Mirren's Deep   | early; discovery phase               |

The people who reliably profit are not the prospectors but those who sell to them, and the deepest shovel-sellers are the ones selling credit.

## Industries

Mining and extraction is the largest sector and the most boom-sensitive. A strike elsewhere can empty an operation overnight, tool suppliers hold quiet leverage, and claim disputes are the frontier's commonest source of violence.

Salvage picks the bones of the Cascade and the failed colonies, loosely coordinated by the Salvage Guild Association and constantly in conflict over who owns a derelict. Mercenary services are a major export in their own right. Manufacturing is deliberately modest—mining gear, weapons, small ships, habitat systems, built for self-sufficiency rather than export. The service economy of bars, trauma surgery, law, finance and information is what separates a boom town from a work camp.

The Freeholds export raw and refined resources, salvage and hired guns, and import high technology, luxury goods, financial services and specialised equipment, running a structural deficit in everything that matters.

## The leash

Outside money provides what the Freeholds cannot generate themselves: venture capital for operations and settlements, banking, insurance, and arbitration with outcomes other Freeholds honour to protect their own access to credit. It arrives under many flags: regional banks trailing the trade routes, the Khivan trade families on the coreward runs, the Reyes Trust where the Long Haul thins out, private lenders of no stated origin, the ever-helpful Nexus, and Arrhene houses in the handful of systems they have thought it worth opening a branch in.

No creditor can repossess a Freehold, no mechanism existing for it. Any of them can refuse further credit, seize assets held off-frontier, downgrade creditworthiness, and pursue claims through an arbitration the rest of the frontier enforces socially. A defaulting Freehold finds every door narrowing until the only ones left open lead where its creditors want it to go. The trade's own phrase for this is discipline without coercion.

Increasingly the creditor is the Sable Cartel: subsidiary operations underpricing local businesses, financial services undercutting the regional banks, reconstruction credit on reasonable terms that matures into leverage, and debts a Freehold cannot prove are Cartel-held and cannot comfortably retire.

## Labour

The labour market is mobile, competitive and brutal at the bottom. Wages swing tenfold within a single system, and skilled hands command fortunes in a boom and starvation once the labour catches up. Conditions run from the structured (corporate mining: adequate safety, little voice) through the harsh (independent operations on share systems) to the genuinely dangerous (boom towns, where high wages are eaten by higher costs). There are no Union-wide labour standards, because there is no Union to set them, and workers organise through crews, skill guilds and salvage cooperatives rather than formal unions.

Outside the accounting altogether are the itinerants, who belong to the Freeholds collectively and so to nobody; the company-town workers paid in scrip; and the prospectors who washed out, took a loan to try once more, and now mine to service it.

## Economic culture

The frontier accepts extreme inequality as natural and largely just. Obligation is owed only to those one chooses and charity is personal rather than institutional, which produces fierce mutual aid within the crew and flat indifference to the stranger from the same root. The economy rewards the gambler, recruits those with nothing to lose, and depends on a steady influx of hopefuls whose failures subsidise the few wins.

## Pressures

The easy strikes are made, and what remains is deeper, poorer and further out. The Mandate border creeps. Cartel penetration advances year on year. The shared beacons, relays and trade stations the whole frontier depends on are funded by voluntary contribution, and the contributions are falling.

## `powers/union-freeholds/governance.md`

# Union of Frontier Freeholds: Governance

The Freeholds are governed by a principle rather than an institution: no external authority may compel a Freehold to do anything.

## The Charter of Freeholds

The Korsen Conference of 2761 C gathered 340 settlement representatives for seven months, and rejected fourteen proposed constitutions before adopting a document defined by what it leaves out. The Charter establishes no government and creates no binding authority. It defines what a Freehold is, fences what the Chair may do, and stops.

Article I defines a Freehold as any settlement that declares itself one, is recognised by three existing Freeholds, governs itself, and does not claim sovereignty over an unwilling population.

Article II holds that each Freehold governs itself by its own laws, and that no Freehold, bloc or Union institution may impose laws, levy taxes, conscript, or regulate another's internal affairs. A Freehold may be a democracy, a dictatorship, a commune or a corporation, and the Union takes no position.

Article III permits the Chair to convene the Assembly, mediate where all parties consent, coordinate against external threats where Freeholds consent, and represent the Union abroad without binding anyone. It may not command, speak for Union policy, raise revenue, or hold a soldier.

Article IV sets amendment at two-thirds of recognised Freeholds, one vote each regardless of population.

Both amendments passed in eleven centuries reduced the Chair further. The First (2894 C) barred it from negotiating any treaty binding on Freeholds without each affected Freehold's prior consent. The Second (3156 C) stripped its power to declare emergency coordination and require force contributions, after two uses in a century were judged too easily abused. Every proposal to expand the Chair has failed.

## The dead letter

Article I's prohibition on sovereignty over the unwilling is unenforceable, because enforcement is exactly what the Charter abolished. A company town that pays in scrip, a creditor that owns a station's only dock, a Dustmote whose founders bound their descendants by their genes—each is, by any honest reading, sovereignty over people who cannot meaningfully leave. When the question is forced, the Union recognises whoever holds effective control and calls it pragmatism.

## The Chair

The Chairship rotates annually by lottery among Freeholds that have not held it in forty years, with equal odds regardless of size. Selected Freeholds may decline; most accept. The chairing Freehold supplies a Speaker, a Mediator-General, a Coordinator and a small staff, and works out of whatever rooms it has.

Joss Renn of Korsen's Anchorage ended the cascading disputes of the Resolution of Seventeen by shuttle diplomacy in 3341 C. Caden Rourke of Veritas Station answered the Grey Incursion in 3567 C by assembling a volunteer fleet of four hundred ships from eighty Freeholds and making the occupation uneconomical. Thessaly Morn of Dustmote did almost nothing in 3789 C and has been cited ever since as the ideal.

The current Chair is Kestrel Brandt of Harrow Station, 67, a former salvage captain, who has spent her year on minor disputes and diplomatic correspondence. "The best Chair is one nobody remembers. I'd like to be forgettable."

## The Assembly

The Union's deliberative body has no power to make law. Any Freehold may convene one, though the Chair's convocations carry more weight. Sessions meet on neutral ground, usually Korsen's Anchorage or Veritas Station; each Freehold sends what delegates it likes; voting is one Freehold, one vote, and the votes bind nobody. Proceedings are informal to the point of shapelessness—no fixed agenda, debate until exhaustion, resolutions that express sentiment rather than law.

The 3860 C session addressed Mandate pressure on the trailing border and produced informal intelligence-sharing and no commitments. The 3861 C session noted the Yansieve rebellion's implications. The 3862 C session is expected to take up increasing Sable Cartel activity.

## Local government and coordination

Within their own walls, Freeholds use every form humans have tried: direct democracy in small settlements and stations, representative councils in mid-sized ones, corporate governance in mining operations, proprietorship in boom towns, traditional authority in old settlements, anarchic consensus among communes and salvage crews. A resident's only recourse against a bad government is to reform it, endure it, or leave.

Most cooperation runs through bilateral deals binding only their parties. A few multilateral arrangements reach further: the Carina Compact (seventeen Freeholds in mutual defence and free trade, the most integrated bloc in the Union and a faintly alarming one to Charter purists), the Salvage Guild Association coordinating claims among two hundred-odd operations, and the Merchant League for trade and shared infrastructure. Disputes are settled by negotiation, Chair mediation, arbitration before whichever neutral house both parties' creditors recognise—whose judgements other Freeholds honour to protect their own credit—or by conflict, which the Charter provides no mechanism to stop.

## External relations

Other powers deal with the Union through the Chair, who listens, relays, and explains that he can bind nobody. There are no treaties in the ordinary sense, only framework agreements that individual Freeholds adopt or ignore. The Arrhenos–Freeholds framework of 3423 C was the first of them and gave the pattern its name: a standing offer of financial services, taken up one Freehold at a time, which no Chair was ever empowered to decline on the Union's behalf. A dozen such frameworks have followed and most are larger, the Khivan one by some margin.

## `powers/union-freeholds/mercenary-companies.md`

# Mercenary Companies of the Frontier

The Freeholds export violence, and it is one of their most reliable industries. Some fifty major companies and five hundred minor ones, plus thousands of independent operators, field around 8 million active personnel for something near 200 billion quid a year. Mercenary work is legal throughout the Freeholds and regulated, if at all, only by individual Freehold law.

## The industry

The frontier breeds mercenaries the way it breeds prospectors: the same risk tolerance, the same practical competence, the same absence of any authority to forbid it. The buyers are Freeholds wanting defence, anti-piracy and crisis response; corporations wanting asset protection, facility security and strike-breaking; other polities wanting deniable operations; and private individuals wanting security, debt collection or revenge. The richest contracts come from outside—the Mandate, Vega houses, and above all the Cartel—for capabilities they cannot or will not build in-house, and for distance from what they buy.

Companies sort roughly by how much their reputation is worth to them: elite firms, professional and selective and reputationally careful; standard contractors, competent and profit-driven and ethically variable; low-end operators of marginal quality; and outright criminal fronts, piracy and extortion wearing a mercenary label.

## The major companies

Iron Vanguard is the largest and most prestigious: founded 3412 C, some 180,000 active, headquartered at Veritas Station, built on combined-arms professionalism and the selling point of refusing certain work—no operations against civilians, no clients who fail due diligence. Its director, Kaela Sarn, sets the industry's tone. Its current contracts include a significant Yansieve presence, on which side it does not say.

Void Reapers (3567 C, ~95,000, Roughneck orbital) are the aggressive end of respectable: ship seizure, boarding, hostile recovery. Effective and ruthless, trailing wrongful-death settlements and a standing ban from two of the corridor insurers, and rumoured Cartel connections they neither confirm nor trouble to deny.

Stalwart Shield (3689 C, ~60,000, Korsen's Anchorage) are the defensive specialists—facility security, route protection, evacuation—reliable, conservative and proud to be boring. Insurers require their contracts and banks accept their protection as collateral.

Crimson Lance (3501 C, ~25,000, headquarters classified) does the work clients prefer not to discuss: reconnaissance, sabotage, targeted elimination, sensitive acquisitions. Nobody outside the company knows where it is based, recruitment is by invitation, and contracts come through intermediaries. Rumours of Mandate intelligence ties, Cartel operations and political assassination are met with the silence that is the firm's entire brand.

Freeborn Brigade (3623 C, ~40,000, Redoubt) refuses the Mandate and anyone aligned with it, trains resistance movements, defends populations under Imperial threat, and is rumoured to work at reduced rates or for nothing where the cause is righteous enough. Its involvement in supporting the Yansieve rebellion is among the worst-kept secrets on the frontier.

## Veritas and the Compact

The industry's centre is Veritas Station, where the major companies keep permanent quarters, contracts are negotiated face to face, and the Veritas Compact sets the informal rules: neutrality between signatories, contract registration and arbitration, shared intelligence on non-payers, and a ban on poaching active personnel. The Compact's arbitration works because everyone benefits from its working, and a company that defies a ruling finds future business hard to come by.

The Cartel's security subsidiary, Aegis, is also headquartered at Veritas, among the independents and indistinguishable at a glance from any other firm renting quarters and registering contracts.

## Recruitment and equipment

Companies recruit from former militia, Mandate deserters, frontier natives chasing opportunity, and the mercenary families in which the trade runs. The elite firms select rigorously; the low-end take anyone. They equip from Freehold manufacturers, external suppliers, captured stock and grey markets, the best maintaining standardised kit and the rest using whatever comes to hand. There is no Union-wide regulation, so the industry self-polices through reputation and through the Contract Registry at Veritas, which records agreements voluntarily and makes a firm's history its collateral.

## `powers/union-freeholds/military.md`

# Union of Frontier Freeholds: Military

The Union maintains no standing forces, no unified command and no central budget. What it has is Freehold militias, commercial mercenary companies, bilateral mutual-defence pacts, and the ad hoc coalitions that form when a threat concentrates minds. The arrangement is not built to project power. It is built to make conquest unaffordable.

An invader faces no single target to defeat, no government to compel surrender, no treaty binding all Freeholds, and thousands of settlements each requiring separate suppression. The Grey Incursion of 3567 C taught the Mandate exactly this: Imperial forces occupied three Freehold systems, declared victory, and found that nobody else stopped. After four years the occupation proved unsustainable and the Mandate withdrew.

The same absence of a centre that leaves an invader nothing to capture leaves a creditor nothing to stop him. There is no government to refuse the Cartel's terms, no command to bar Aegis from Veritas, no authority to keep Nexus from becoming the only bank at Korsen's.

## Militias

Each Freehold keeps whatever force it likes, from a professional standing militia through part-time citizen reserves to a simply armed population, private security contractors, or a corporation's company troops. Most sit in the middle. Quality ranges enormously: the militias of Roughneck and Korsen's Anchorage are professional, drilled and interoperable, while those of smaller and poorer Freeholds may be untrained, mixed in equipment, and useful only for local security. Equipment runs from universal small arms up through heavy weapons and, for the wealthier, ships—mostly Freehold-made, supplemented by imports, with Mandate military surplus turning up regularly despite the export controls that nominally forbid it. There are no Union-wide training standards, and the gap shows the moment a crisis demands coordination.

## Mutual defence

Most coordination runs through bilateral pacts specifying what triggers mutual aid, what each party contributes, who commands, and how costs are shared. An attack on one Freehold may pull in several allies, each answering a different agreement. The deepest of these is the Carina Compact, with standardised equipment and training, joint exercises, a shared emergency command and pooled intelligence.

The pacts share the frontier's characteristic limits: no guarantee anyone honours them, no unified command when several allies respond at once, free-riding by those who shelter behind strong neighbours, and the risk of entanglement in quarrels not one's own.

## Coordination in crisis

When a threat concentrates, the Chair facilitates—naming the threatened, relaying requests, matching forces to needs, heading off duplication and friendly fire—and cannot compel.

At the Grey Incursion, Chair Caden Rourke's volunteer fleet harried the Mandate's supply lines and made the occupation uneconomical. Against the pirate fleets of 3712 C, the Chair organised militia responses and hired mercenary companies for the offensive work, and after years of effort the largest groups were broken. In the Cassini Incident of 3845 C, disputed salvage claims escalated to open fighting between Freehold factions; mediation failed, the militias fought to no conclusion, and only exhaustion produced a settlement.

## Assessment

The decentralised defence has no single point of failure and cannot be decapitated; no prize is worth suppressing thousands of settlements; forces assemble and dissolve as needed; and people defend homes rather than an abstraction. Its weaknesses are the mirror image—no power projection, slow and partial coordination, wild quality variance, chronic free-riding. Against a conqueror willing to spend without limit the frontier could in theory be taken, and the Mandate could do it if it committed everything forever.

Of the pressures actually operating, only piracy and the periodic wars between Freeholds are the kind a militia can answer. Mandate expansion advances by border-creep below the threshold that would summon a Rourke, and Cartel activity advances by purchase and debt.

## `powers/union-freeholds/locations/dustmote.md`

# Dustmote

A Freehold in good standing whose natives cannot leave it. Not will not—cannot. Three centuries ago their founders rewrote their own genetics to require the specific light of Karenna, an F5V white star, and the change breeds true. Around 340 million people at 0.94 gravity, on a gentle agricultural world.

## The Rootbound Covenant

Karenna was surveyed in 3156 C and found mild in climate, stable in star and fertile in soil. The consortium that won the colony, the Rootbound Covenant, held that humanity's sickness was rootlessness and that the cure was to make leaving impossible. Before landfall they modified themselves—skin chemistry tuned to Karenna's spectrum, a dependency on trace compounds produced only under that light, developmental patterns requiring exposure in childhood—and made the changes heritable.

It was entirely legal, the Freeholds imposing no limit on self-modification, and it was widely condemned then and since as binding the unconsenting. The Covenant's answer was that all children everywhere are bound by their parents' choices, and at least these would have roots. The natives hold every position in the argument: some defend the founding as necessary, some resent it, most simply live inside it.

## The dependency

A Dustmote native deprived of Karenna's light declines within days, sickens within weeks and dies within months. The spectrum cannot be perfectly reproduced; equipment approximates it well enough for short trips and worse the longer one is away, so off-world life is possible, difficult, and a slow medical emergency. The modifications show—skin faintly orange, eyes golden in certain light, a sensitivity to anything outside the optimal range—so off-world a Dustmoter is recognisable, and meets the usual human menu of curiosity, prejudice and fetish.

## A society built for forever

The population has nowhere else to be and has built for permanence: durable infrastructure, multi-generational institutions, and one of the more sophisticated governments on the frontier, with an elected assembly by proportional representation, an executive council, an independent judiciary and real constitutional protections.

Its values follow from its condition. Commitment is the cardinal virtue. Stewardship holds that every generation owes the next an enduring colony. Self-sufficiency follows from the observation that to depend on imports is to depend on people who can leave.

Karenna's light and soil make Dustmote a serious agricultural exporter. It imports most complex technology while exporting a small sophisticated line of light-spectrum and life-support equipment, and consumes external media avidly, participating in the frontier mostly at a distance. Most natives never travel, and the few who range widely become a small worldly class.

At Roothold's centre stands the Shrine of First Light: a preserved habitat module from the colony ship, the founders' recorded explanation of their choice, and the genetic record of the modification. It is neither celebrated nor condemned, simply kept.

| Covenant | ~35 million | agricultural processing                  |
| Sunrise  | ~25 million | coastal; fishing and aquaculture         |
| Farlight | ~15 million | research and education                   |

## Figures

Councillor Tashen Mor, 61, is Dustmote's representative to the Union and one of the few who travels widely, living by constant medical management of her dependency. "I've seen the frontier—the transience, the rootlessness. My ancestors wanted something different. They achieved it. Whether the cost was justified, ask me again on my deathbed."

## `powers/union-freeholds/locations/korsen's-anchorage.md`

# Korsen's Anchorage

The frontier's marketplace: the one place Freeholders, Drift communities, external traders and people who would be enemies anywhere else come to exchange goods, services and information without fighting. A station complex near the Korsen system's jump shell, holding some 12 million permanent residents and 5 million transients at gravities from 0.4 to 1.0 G depending on the section.

## The neutral ground

The Anchorage began in 2934 C as a single station built by Mira Korsen, a trader who set it at a junction of common routes and declared it open to all. It grew by accretion ever since—traders adding facilities, Drift communities establishing connection points, services chasing the traffic—until by 3400 C it was the largest station complex in Freehold space.

The neutrality is fiercely kept. Early attempts to settle disputes by violence were crushed, and the rule hardened into custom: whatever your quarrel, you do not pursue it here, on pain of permanent exclusion.

The complex is a labyrinth of centuries of unplanned construction—the cramped Old Anchor, the perpetually crowded Trade Core, the Docks, the Drift Quarter where Haven Ascendant and the smaller havens connect, the Residentials, and the Warrens, where corridors dead-end and levels do not align. Navigation shifts as new sections reroute the traffic. Knowing the Anchorage is a skill.

## Trade, information and Nexus

The Anchorage deals in commodities, equipment, ships of every provenance, services, and above all information, sold off physical and virtual Boards that run from public market prices through subscription analysis to private intelligence and frank rumour markets. Where the strikes are, which claims produce, what the Mandate is doing at the border, who is hiring guns.

The money runs through Nexus—the accounts, the currency exchange, the credit, the financial plumbing of the entire hub—and Nexus is the Sable Cartel, and has been for as long as most of the traffic can remember. The frontier's habit of minding its own business let the fact sit unexamined for decades: a good bank, fair rates, no questions. People are looking closer now that the Cartel's broader push has made the connection hard to ignore.

## Governance

An elected Harbourmaster serves ten-year terms, with permanent residents voting. The current holder, Cassius Dray, 78, has held the post since 3851 C. He keeps dock allocation, security, station-level dispute resolution, infrastructure and the enforcement of neutrality, and leaves commerce to the parties to it. Order is kept by the Anchor Guard, some fifteen thousand strong and formidable enough that violence on station is rare.

Dray has declined bribes from every major power. "Everyone hates someone. My job is making sure they hate each other somewhere else. Here, we trade." He can bar a weapon and exclude a killer. He cannot un-bank the Anchorage.

## Life on station

Most of the Anchorage is passing through—traders between contracts, crews between jobs, prospectors between strikes, Drift people during a haven's connection—and the transience sets the tone: relationships form fast and dissolve faster, nobody asks about your past, nobody expects you to stay. The permanents occupy the same corridors and a different world.

Everything is available: bars from the refined to the disreputable, gambling, sex work at every price, combat sports, Vega serials. The Tumbledown, a bar old enough to appear in route documentation as a navigation landmark—"three days past the Tumbledown connection"—is an institution for no better reason than that it has always been there.

## Figures

Yara Senn, 45, is among the Anchorage's most successful brokers, her speciality the transactions that cannot be discussed openly. "I don't ask what it's for. I connect people who want to buy with people who want to sell. What they do after is their business."

Captain Vex Orien, 52, has run the Drift trader *Fortunate Wanderer* between the Anchorage and the havens for twenty-five years. "The Drift and the frontier understand each other. We both know what it means to have no fixed place. The difference is they're looking for one."

## `powers/union-freeholds/locations/roughneck.md`

# Roughneck

The industrial heart of the frontier: a functioning industrial economy three centuries past its rush, with an owning class, a working class, and a settled inequality. Around 890 million people planetside and 150 million in orbit, at 1.12 gravity, around Hephaestus Prime.

## From strike to settlement

Hephaestus was surveyed in 3456 C and dismissed as uninteresting until the prospector Callum Vance found, in 3512 C, that its crust held vast deposits of rare earths, heavy metals and industrial ore—hard to reach, but enormous. The rush was textbook. What made Roughneck rare is that its boom never busted: the deposits were deep enough to sustain centuries of extraction, deep mining created permanent work, and processing, then manufacturing, then services followed, until by 3600 C Roughneck was a diversified industrial economy.

With stability came concentration. Mining wealth gathered among claim-holders, facility owners and investors while workers watched their boom wages erode as the labour supply caught up. The Labour Wars of 3672–3689 C followed—strikes, lockouts and intermittent violence—and ended not in victory but in compromise: worker representation in the major facilities, minimum safety standards, some profit-sharing, and no change at all to who owned what. Labour relations have been tense and functional ever since.

## A class society

| Owners           | claim-holders, major shareholders, facility proprietors |
| Management       | administrators, engineers, specialists                  |
| Skilled workers  | miners, machinists, technicians                         |
| Unskilled labour | general labour and service positions                    |

Mobility exists and is limited. A skilled worker may reach management; management rarely reaches ownership; the mining fortunes have compounded down the generations into something very like an aristocracy that the frontier would be embarrassed to call one. Workers organise through skill guilds, general unions and the company councils the Labour Wars won them.

Governance is a frank hybrid: an elected council for civil matters, an industrial board of the major facilities for economic policy, and joint committees for the overlap. The arrangement hands the owners disproportionate weight on the board and has resisted every worker attempt to change it.

## Geography and cities

A heavy, rugged world—thick air, hard terrain, survivable without suits in most places and pleasant in none. The Scar is the primary extraction zone, the Basin feeds the planet, the Reaches are new development, and the Wastes are the depleted ground abandoned operations leave behind. The Roughneck orbital handles processing, shipyards and trade, and runs much of its bulk transit through Cartel Wayline logistics.

| Portside      | ~95 million  | trade and transport                                |
| Orbital Prime | ~45 million  | station administration and commerce                |

## Figures

Governor Bryn Thane, 58, elected head of the planetary council, is the career moderate both sides distrust, which she takes as proof she is doing it right. "This world works because everyone gets something. Owners get profits, workers get wages, the council gets stability. My job is making sure nobody tries to take everything."

Jace Holloway Sr, 64, has organised Roughneck's workers for forty years and thinks the old settlements have rotted. "The owners need us more than we need them. They've forgotten that." He is planning something larger—coordination across facilities, perhaps a general strike—pending negotiations now under way.

Director Darrow Vance, 71, descendant of the original prospector, controls more of Roughneck's extraction than anyone. "My family built this world. We've earned what we have. The workers earn what they're paid, nothing more." He is old, hardening, and pressed from two sides: by the workers below and by younger owners who think his refusal to bend is what will break the peace.

## `powers/union-freeholds/locations/sefkir-reach.md`

# The Sefkir Reach

A cluster of a dozen or so Freeholds out toward the trailing edge, perhaps three billion people, remote even by frontier standards. In 3829 C the Reach expelled every identifiable Sable Cartel subsidiary from its systems and has never let them back.

## The expulsion

Through the 3820s the Cartel's subsidiaries had done in the Sefkir systems what they do everywhere: Nexus credit funding the booms, Wayline freight running the only reliable supply line, cheap subsidiary goods undercutting the local trades until the local trades were gone. What made Sefkir different was arithmetic and distance. It was remote enough that the Cartel's logistics were thin and expensive anyway, and small enough that a debt squeeze in 3828 C fell on everyone at once and visibly.

A dozen Freeholds that had never agreed on anything signed the Sefkir Compact in 3829 C: a mutual undertaking to expel every Cartel subsidiary from every member's systems on the same day, to refuse Cartel credit and Cartel freight thereafter, and—the clause that gave it teeth—to treat any member that quietly let the Cartel back in as having left the Compact.

It held. The subsidiaries were given thirty days, the docks that had been Wayline's became the Compact's, and the debts owed to Nexus were repudiated, which the credit market quietly noted and the Reach quietly accepted as the cost of the divorce.

## The price

Cut off from Wayline, the Reach built its own freight: a logistics cooperative of mismatched hulls that runs slower, breaks more, and reaches fewer places than the Cartel routes it replaced. Cut off from Nexus credit, it learned to live within its means and finances what it can through the Reyes Trust, having expelled the Cartel rather than all of finance—a distinction it is careful to keep. Cut off from the cheap subsidiary goods, it makes its own, badly and dearly, or does without.

Prices in the Reach are higher, choices fewer, the booms smaller and the busts harder. Some of the dozen founding Freeholds could not stand it and drifted back into the wider frontier's orbit within a decade. The committed core stayed.

What it bought is the absence of the chains the rest of the frontier wears: no company stores, no scrip good in only one place, no debt that cannot be traced because there is no Cartel to trace it to, no neutral commons that turns out to be someone's headquarters.

## The Cartel's patience

The Cartel did not crush the Reach. A Reach burned to the waterline would be a martyr; a Reach left visibly poor is a warning. So the Cartel manages the story—ensuring the hardship is seen and the freedom is not, letting the slow freight and the dear goods speak for themselves, and discouraging, by means that never quite surface, the brokers who might carry the Reach's example outward dressed as something enviable rather than something pitiable.

A handful of neighbouring Freeholds study the Reach with an interest that has lately turned from amusement to something more thoughtful.

## Figures

Edda Voss (d. 3851 C) is the Reach's nearest thing to a founder: a salvage captain turned organiser who, in the squeeze of 3828–29, did the unglamorous work of getting a dozen proud and suspicious Freeholds into one room and keeping them there until they signed. She refused every honorific the Reach tried to give her—"we did it; I just booked the room"—governed nothing afterward, and died poor and at home.

Sera Voll, 24, is Reach-born, Reach-raised, and old enough now to leave. The wider frontier is a few weeks' slow freight away, with its booms and its bright goods and its credit for anyone willing to wear the collar, and a young woman with Voll's hands could do well out there. She has not decided. "My grandmother signed the Compact. My mother's never once regretted it. And I've never once eaten as well as the people who took the deal."

# Three Falls

A marginal world at 0.91 gravity around a K2V orange dwarf, holding some 2.3 million people where two years ago there were forty thousand. The frontier at peak rush.

## The aurite rush

Aurite is a rare crystalline compound prized for quantum-computing substrates, high-efficiency energy storage, cellular-regeneration medicine, and—the crystals being genuinely beautiful—luxury goods. Deposits are scarce, and Three Falls may hold the largest known concentration. In 3858 C the struggling prospector Kellen Marsh, who had worked the system for years and found nothing, caught anomalous deep-scan readings, filed her claim and pulled the first samples.

The rush is already changing character. The easy surface deposits are nearly gone, and what remains needs deep mining, heavy equipment and capital, so the individual prospectors increasingly find nothing while the corporate operations increasingly take everything.

## The Sprawl

Thin breathable air, little water, temperature extremes, and infrastructure built for forty thousand: docks past capacity, improvised landing fields, inadequate roads, strained water, unreliable power, and nowhere near enough housing, medicine or sanitation. Outside the original settlement the Sprawl has grown organically out of prefabs, tents and converted cargo containers, and houses most of the boom population in conditions from uncomfortable to lethal.

The mix is volatile—overwhelmed original settlers, experienced prospectors, hopefuls with dreams and thin resources, service providers chasing the money, opportunists legal and otherwise, and the corporate representatives quietly buying up claims. They tolerate the squalor because they believe it temporary.

Three Falls declared itself a Freehold in 3860 C on the bare minimum three recognitions. Governance amounts to a claim registry, a small overwhelmed security force and a port authority; everything else happens privately or not at all, including the armed enforcement that increasingly settles the claim disputes the registry cannot keep up with. The Marsh Foundation runs the only adequately resourced medical operation, a small school, and what passes for infrastructure.

## Figures

Kellen Marsh, 47, the original prospector, is now one of the wealthiest people on the frontier and visibly uneasy about it. "I got lucky. I'd rather be lucky than smart, but luck doesn't last. Ask me again in ten years whether this was worth it."

Mayor Della Kade, 52, ran a trading post until the boom made her responsible for 2.3 million people. "I didn't sign up for this. Nobody signed up for this. We're all improvising."

Jace Holloway Jr, 28, came from Roughneck, where his father organises miners, to organise the processing workers. "My father taught me that workers have power when they act together. These workers haven't learned that yet. They will."

The Assessor, real name unknown, values claims from a converted cargo container in the Sprawl—geology, production records, legal status—and is trusted more than most because he is careful to stay neutral. "I tell you what I think a claim is worth. What you do with that is your problem."

Wren Aldis, 33, came on the second wave with savings and a deep-scan rig bought on credit, found nothing worth filing, took a short-term contract with a corporate operation to keep eating, and took a second loan against the first when the rig needed repair. Two years on she works a processing line in the Sprawl, owes more than the rig was ever worth to a lender she has never met, and is paid partly in scrip good only at the operation's commissary. She is free to leave Three Falls. She cannot afford the passage, cannot clear the debt without the wage, and cannot earn the wage anywhere but here. "Everybody told me it was the land where nobody owns you. Nobody does. I just can't go."

## `powers/union-freeholds/locations/veritas-station.md`

# Veritas Station

Where secrets are traded and mercenaries are hired. A purpose-built station in the Veritas asteroid belt at a standard 0.6 G, holding around 3 million permanent residents and a million transients. Where Korsen's Anchorage is neutral about disputes, Veritas is neutral about ethics.

## Origins and shape

Founded in 3401 C by a consortium of mercenary companies wanting neutral ground for contract negotiation, Veritas grew past its origins as intelligence services, information brokers and the legal, financial and technical trades followed the traffic. By 3600 C it was the frontier's intelligence hub as much as its mercenary marketplace.

Unlike the Anchorage's accreted chaos, Veritas is legible: the public Forum with its contract boards, the private Negotiation Decks, the Archive of verified intelligence, the Company Quarters where the major firms keep permanent presence, Intelligence Row, and the Discretion Levels for business that must not be seen. Everything public is recorded and everything private is protected—surveillance and counter-surveillance in the same hull.

## The trades

Mercenary contracts are the original business: negotiation in the Decks, registration with the station, arbitration when deals sour, and the informal industry standards the major companies set here.

Information is the other half—raw intelligence, processed analysis, verification services, and access to the four-century Archive—bought by corporations, governments deniably, companies and the wealthy, and sold by operatives, analysts and informants.

The Discretion Levels sell privacy: no questions, no record, and no concern for what is decided there so long as it does not threaten the station itself.

## The Compact

The Veritas Compact binds the signatory companies into a workable order—neutrality among themselves, contract registration and arbitration, shared intelligence on non-payers, no poaching of active personnel—and it works because everyone benefits from its working. Defy a Veritas ruling and future business dries up. Iron Vanguard's permanent presence sets the tone, and its director, Kaela Sarn, is the most powerful individual routinely on the station.

The Cartel's security subsidiary, Aegis, is headquartered here too, renting quarters and registering contracts among the independent companies. From that seat the line between an independent frontier company and a Cartel instrument is as blurry as the Cartel cares to make it.

## Culture

Business is business, personal matters stay personal, enemies elsewhere trade civilly here, and reputation rests on reliability. The station balances contradictory virtues by design—openness in the contract markets, where transparency makes for efficient matching, and secrecy in the Discretion Levels, where opacity is the product—and maintains an explicit neutrality about ethics. What is traded here runs the full range from the legitimate to the monstrous, and Veritas treats it all alike.

## Figures

Broker Thresh—no full name, identity protected, appearing in person rarely and through intermediaries usually—is among the most successful information brokers on the station. "I sell truth. Sometimes the truth is uncomfortable. That's not my problem, that's the product."

Archive Director Edra Vire, 71, keeps the Archive and its integrity, and has met every attempt at tampering with permanent exclusion. "Everything we have was given to us by someone who thought it mattered. Whether it still matters is someone else's decision."

# The Vega Commercial Throne

A hereditary aristocracy under permanent market discipline, and the largest entertainment industry in human space. Around 1.5 trillion people, centred on the Vega system and the throne world of Lyra.

The celebrated nobility numbers a few thousand families. Everyone else is the workforce that builds the spectacle and the audience it is staged for.

Vega's houses do not hold safe seats. They rise and fall on performance, and a house that fails in the marketplace is stripped of rank and name by a procedure called attainder, which takes archdukes as readily as barons. In the twenty-three years since the Banking War, fourteen houses have been attainted and a handful of new ones raised.

## The Throne

The Commercial Throne is a seat of government and the oldest and largest corporation in Vega space at the same time. The monarch is sovereign and chief executive, and the royal family holds controlling shares in enterprises spanning the system.

Queen Lysandra IV has held it for sixty-seven years. She is 134, presents perhaps forty-five, and has ruled through the Andromedan Banking War, the long reconstruction, and a careful recalibration of Vega's place among the powers. Her reputation is for patience, financial acumen, calculated generosity, and a ruthlessness that has on occasion ended houses.

## The feudal-commercial bargain

Vega's nobility holds territory, and territory is not where the power is. The fiefdoms are commercial franchises as much as landed estates: a house owns brands, studios, distribution networks, broadcast licences and manufacturing patents, and passes them down along with its titles. Commercial failure can trigger attainder, and with it the loss of noble status.

## What the trillion do

For most Vegans the aristocratic drama is something that happens on a screen. They work in the prestige industries and the ordinary ones, in the manufactories that make luxuries they will never own, in the entertainment orbital that films a glamour they will only ever watch, and on the agricultural worlds the court forgets between harvests. There are no registration bands here and a commoner is not degraded. What the culture withholds is visibility: the serials are about nobles, the news covers the court, and the advertisements are set in lives no ordinary person leads.

## Recent history

The Andromedan Banking War (3834–3839 C) began in finance, over contested assets on the Andromedan frontier and clever instruments that turned out to be bombs, and escalated through Sable Cartel proxy forces into real fighting. Several houses were destroyed outright and the royal treasury was gutted. The reconstruction has run twenty-three years and is not finished.

Recovery was financed from outside, by a syndicate of hundreds no single member could have carried alone, on terms the aristocracy resents and cannot do without. Some reconstruction credit came from elsewhere on reasonable terms that have since matured into leverage, and several houses now service debts to creditors they cannot prove are Cartel-held and cannot comfortably repay.

## `powers/vega-throne/culture.md`

# The Vega Commercial Throne: Culture

A Vega noble is trained from childhood to appear never to have been trained at all. The word for it is *sprezzatura*, the art of effortless excellence, and it is the most laboured-over thing in human space.

## The performance

The aristocratic ideal is to look perfect without visible effort, to speak elegantly without seeming rehearsed, to know everything relevant without having studied, and to succeed in business without appearing to try. Noble children spend years learning to seem unstudied.

The body is maintained to exacting standards: enhancement in the EX 1.5–2.0 range, with visible modification considered vulgar; immaculate grooming, with staff to maintain it; bearing trained by deportment coaches from childhood. The standard is attainable only with wealth, and the cost of the performance—staff, wardrobe, treatments, time—excludes commoners as efficiently as any law could.

Every interaction is assessed. Other nobles observe and judge, servants observe and gossip, the media reports the public appearances, and the quarterly commercial rankings reduce the whole thing to a number. Nobody escapes it: the Queen's fashion and her family's behaviour are watched as closely as anyone's.

## The audience

For most Vegans, aristocratic culture is consumed rather than lived. The court serials are the closest thing the Throne's worlds have to shared weather, and in the cities of Lyra a person who cannot converse about this season's drama is at a real social disadvantage.

The audience is not one audience. The agricultural worlds run their own serials, with their own stars and their own gossip, that the court has never heard of and would not recognise as Vegan; the outer-system traders follow the freight-drama circuits; whole districts of the Manufactories watch nothing noble at all and follow the works leagues instead.

Commoners relate to the ideal above them by aspiration—pursuing aristocratic style within reach, buying prestige brands at accessible price points, following the noble fashion cycles a season late; by mockery—parodying the pretension and enjoying the great houses' scandals as sport; or by indifference, attending to work and family and the local rather than the Radiant Court.

Vega does not degrade its commoners. There is no forelock-tugging, no caste mark, no Bureau, and the aristocracy maintains distance rather than demanding submission. What the culture withholds is attention: common experience is unremarkable and therefore unremarked. Some find that a relief, since nobody scrutinises their dress or their speech and no quarterly ranking decides their worth. Others find it a slow diminishment.

## Language

The prestige tongue is Vegan Standard, evolved from Old Earth Romance languages with heavy Trade Tongue influence: the language of court, formal entertainment, business and educated conversation. Beneath it the older languages persist without stigma among commoners and with faint condescension from the nobility—Provençal derivatives in Lyra's equatorial regions, Germanian dialects on the northern agricultural worlds, Sinic-influenced creoles among the outer-system traders. Trade Tongue runs through all of it, increasingly so in entertainment, which serves galactic audiences and cannot afford to be parochial.

## Religion

Religious observance is minimal, as across most of the corridor cultures Vega belongs to. The Throne does not suppress belief but regards organised religion with the same wariness it regards any independent power centre, and congregations exist, stay quiet, and stay out of politics. The nearest thing Vega has to a faith is its cult of excellence: quality, achievement and competitive success treated as something close to sacred.

## Art and aesthetics

The Vega style is recognisable across human space: clean lines over rich materials, restraint that implies hidden opulence, the appearance of simplicity achieved by expensive means; sophisticated orchestration and technically demanding performance made to sound effortless; character-driven drama in long arcs with delayed gratification. Bodies are treated as art and rooms as stages.

The line between high culture and popular entertainment is unusually soft. A prestige serial may run for decades, win critical acclaim and command a mass audience at once; a popular song may be genuinely sophisticated; nobles consume popular work and commoners attend the high cultural events. What separates the tiers is production value and subtlety rather than the audience.

## Social customs

Hospitality is taken seriously and predates the Throne. A guest is owed welcome, refreshment, protection from other guests' rudeness, and a gracious farewell, and hosts who fail are quietly avoided.

Gift-giving is a language: the practical gift signals friendship or obligation, the luxury gift a wish to impress, the handmade gift real intimacy, and nothing at all a statement of independence. Misjudging the register damages relationships.

The social calendar is anchored by the great court occasions—the Ascension Ball at the new year, the Commercial Review where the houses present their numbers and the rankings are published, the Founder's Festival, the Harvest Gala—with smaller functions filling every week of the peak season. The Gap days are treated as rest: no formal obligations, reduced business, time returned briefly to private life.

## Scandal and reputation

Reputation is directly convertible into revenue, since consumers buy a house's goods partly for the status the brand confers, and a tarnished house taints its own products. Houses invest heavily in managing it: controlling appearances, cultivating and where necessary pressuring the media, rehearsing crisis responses, constructing long narratives of excellence. When scandal breaks anyway the playbook is to deny if plausible, contextualise if not, distract with positive stories, and rehabilitate over time through demonstrated success.

The media reports noble scandal with delicacy, by mutual interest—today's target is tomorrow's partner—so coverage tends toward innuendo rather than accusation. Genuine exposés happen, and when they do it means someone has made an enemy powerful enough to spend the usual courtesies.

## Outsiders

Visitors find Vega exhausting, impressive, superficial, refined and alienating in proportions that say as much about the visitor as the place. An Arrhene fresh from a society with no hereditary status often trips over the basic premise, though the Arrhene clerks who have worked the reconstruction for twenty years navigate the houses better than some barons. A first-time Mandate visitor recognises hierarchy but not its fusion with the market. A Drift performer used to intimate fleets may find the scale and formality overwhelming, or take to an audience of a trillion like a fish returned to water.

## `powers/vega-throne/economy.md`

# The Vega Commercial Throne: Economy

Vega's economy runs on the manufacture of meaning. A Vega luxury good is worth what it costs because it costs what it is worth: the price is not a measure of the product, it is part of it.

## Currency

Vega uses the standard quid, and keeps its internal books in the crown, a unit pegged to the quid and psychologically detached from it. Prices in crowns feel like Vega prices, untethered from the galactic markets that humbled the Throne in the Banking War.

## The brand economy

Production is held deliberately below demand, the limit being the luxury. Price signals status, so within bounds a higher price raises desire rather than dampening it. Counterfeiting is pursued with a ferocity reserved for heresy, because a fake that passes does not steal a sale, it dissolves the meaning the whole sector is built on. And brands invest fortunes in stories of heritage, craft and distinguished clientele, because the story is the thing the customer actually wears.

The luxury sector floats on Vega's collective prestige, which is why houses that compete viciously for market share will cooperate instantly to defend "Vega-made" as a mark. The same logic governs the entertainment and news houses: compete on everything except the shared illusion that keeps the customers paying.

## The sectors

Entertainment media is the largest by revenue and employment. Luxury goods—fashion, jewellery, furnishings, vehicles, fine consumables—overlap heavily with it, since a fashion house sells the garment and the programme about the garment in the same breath. News is a sector of its own, trusted abroad precisely because it is commercial, and careful at home for the same reason.

## The Banking War and the debt

The Andromedan Banking War bankrupted and dissolved several great houses, wrecked infrastructure and trade routes, and cut output by perhaps fifteen to twenty per cent in the worst year. Recovery has been uneven. News recovered first, bad times raising the price of reliable information; entertainment and luxury have mostly rebuilt; finance never came back at all. Before the war, House Kraeven's instruments were a first-rank name in cross-power lending, and its collapse scattered that business to the regional houses and neutral firms that had always worked the edges of it.

Peak reconstruction debt ran to some 3.8 trillion quid, of which around 2.4 trillion is still outstanding. No single creditor could have carried it: the syndicate ran to hundreds of names, led by the Khivan trade families, the Veskarn Combine's underwriting arm, half the regional banks of the Sol–Vega neighbourhood, and a tail of smaller neutral houses taking a slice each. Some of the credit did not come from any of them but from the Sable Cartel, advanced on reasonable terms that have since matured into leverage.

## Labour and class

Retainers—the personal staff, administrators and specialists attached directly to a noble house—are not noble, but their nearness to nobility confers status and security. Families serve the same house for generations, and the positions are coveted.

Professionals in the studios and manufactories, the skilled trades and the better service roles live decently where talent or scarcity gives them leverage, and adequately where it does not.

Common labour—agriculture on the rural worlds and orbital farms, basic manufacture, services, extraction in the outer system—is the bulk of one and a half trillion people. They are not pressed down so much as left out of frame.

Mobility exists and is narrow. Talent discovery can transform a life, skill can win a retainer's place, and commercial brilliance may, very rarely, attract a grant of rank.

## Pressures

The debt constrains every ambitious move. Competition improved abroad while Vega rebuilt, in Mandate studios and Freehold workshops that no longer simply lose. And labour pressure builds slowly in the entertainment trades, where the discarded occasionally organise; the Throne permits just enough collective action to release the pressure, and watches it for the political content it is careful never quite to develop.

## `powers/vega-throne/government.md`

# The Vega Commercial Throne: Government

Vega is not a democracy and does not pretend to be one. Its argument for itself is that a market votes every day, with money, and disciplines more ruthlessly than any electorate could: a house that fails its customers loses its sales, then its revenue, then its rank, and at last its name.

The market adjudicates the few thousand families that are in it. The other one and a half trillion Vegans get neither a vote nor a verdict.

## The monarch

The monarch holds sovereign authority over all Vega territory, a controlling interest in the Throne Corporation, final arbitration in disputes between houses, and command of the Royal Fleet. These are not ceremonial powers, but the monarch rules through institutions, precedent and the management of competing noble interests far more than by decree.

Lysandra IV has reigned since 3795 C. The Banking War was the test of her reign, and the reconstruction has been, in her hands, partly an opportunity to reshape the field while rivals were weak.

Her children hold posts across the royal enterprises: Prince Caspian, the heir, over the Entertainment Division; Princess Aurelie over External Relations; Prince Hadrian liaising with the Royal Fleet; Princess Seraphine over Luxury Brands; and Prince Octavian over the News Division, a delicate posting given what Vega's press is meant to be trusted as separate from. Family members are expected to perform, and those who cannot are quietly sidelined.

## The houses

| Royal House      | 1      | the monarch's family                                  |
| Archducal houses | 7      | vast commercial empires; one seat attainted and empty |
| Ducal houses     | ~40    | major players with significant market presence        |
| Comital houses   | ~400   | mid-tier, in specialised niches                       |
| Baronial houses  | ~3,000 | minor nobility; single enterprises or localities      |

Noble status confers territorial jurisdiction, commercial licences, court access, trial by peers, and the right to raise a retinue. It requires taxes, court attendance, military contribution, and commercial performance above a minimum threshold.

## Attainder

A house that fails its obligations can be stripped of everything: rank gone, licences revoked and returned to the Throne for redistribution, jurisdiction lost, court access ended, and often personal assets seized. Former nobles become commoners, and their descendants carry the stigma down.

Of the fourteen houses attainted since the Banking War, seven fell for bankruptcy, four for criminal conduct, two for rebellion, and one for the catch-all of conduct unbecoming. The first archducal attainder in two centuries fell in this period, on House Kraeven.

The door opens inward too. New houses are created by royal grant for extraordinary service, by purchase at baronial level only, by marriage, or by commercial triumph too spectacular for the Throne to ignore. Most new creations are baronial, and climbing higher takes generations.

## Valdorian

House Valdorian has won entertainment so thoroughly—roughly a third of all Vega production, the largest private military in Vega space, chokepoints over the infrastructure its rivals depend on—that it approaches the power to defy the Throne. It has done so legitimately. It has not failed, so it cannot be attainted; it has broken no law, so it cannot be charged; and it has performed correct loyalty for thirty-one years under Duke Caelum.

Queen Lysandra has declined every opportunity to clip Valdorian, calculating that moving against a house that has done nothing wrong would damage the legitimacy the whole system runs on.

## The succession

Prince Caspian is the accepted heir and his eventual accession is expected to be smooth. He is also eighty-nine, childless, and has shown no interest in becoming otherwise.

The Throne can designate an heir, but the choice must in practice be acceptable to the great houses, whose withdrawal of cooperation would make rule impossible. Every archducal house has begun, quietly, to game the generation after next.

## Administration

Day-to-day governance runs through professional ministries: the Exchequer for taxation, royal finances and commercial regulation; Justice; Commerce for licensing and market oversight; External Affairs; Defence; Culture for broadcasting regulation and patronage; and a Reconstruction ministry that is theoretically temporary and showing every sign of permanence. These posts are not noble preserves and competent commoners rise to senior roles, though the very top tends to go to minor nobility or royal appointees.

Within their fiefdoms, houses govern as they like, subject to royal law, commercial regulation and custom, and the Throne intervenes only at the extremes. Noble disputes go to the Court of Peers, where nobles are judged by nobles; commoner cases go to ordinary magistrates' courts that function adequately and arbitrarily by turns.

## Politics

Factions form around shared industries, adjacent territories, ideology—traditionalists against modernisers, expansionists against consolidators—and the old currents of marriage, friendship and inherited enmity. The Queen plays these against one another, ensuring no coalition grows strong enough to threaten the Throne.

The reconstruction has left the court less comfortable than it was a generation ago. Houses that weathered the war have grown fat on the assets of houses that did not, the survivors resent the profiteers, the reconstruction debt is a standing humiliation, and a rising cohort of younger nobles who never knew the pre-war prosperity has begun to wonder aloud whether the recovery will ever reach them.

# The Vega Commercial Throne: Media

Vega has no Bureau of Internal Harmony, no listeners and no disappearances. The Throne governs one and a half trillion people with content.

## Entertainment

Some 800 billion quid a year, employing around 40 million directly and perhaps 200 million indirectly across thousands of active productions, reaching most of human space.

The flagship format is the serialised drama, with narratives running for years or decades. *The Amber Throne* is in its sixty-seventh season, has outlived its original creators, and has put a phrase into Trade Tongue: to do an Amber Throne is to navigate a tangled situation with strategic patience. A prestige season costs fifty to a hundred million quid across forty episodes, and earns it back several times over in licensing and merchandise.

Around the serials orbit music built for live performance and relentless touring—the Core, the Mandate circuit, the Freehold run, the Drift stops—the growing trade in immersive formats, and the great spectacles like the Crown Tournament and the Artistic Olympiad, where noble houses sponsor competitors and bask in their wins.

## News

Vega journalism is among the most trusted in human space, and the trust is earned. The reason is structural: the news houses are commercial rather than political, selling information to audiences who want it accurate, so competition rewards truth-telling, because a house caught lying loses the only thing it sells. House Meridian carries around forty per cent of Vega news consumption, keeps bureaux in every major system, and has four and a half centuries invested in its credibility.

The trust is also carefully bounded.

| Covered well      | external politics, business and commerce, foreign scandal |
| Handled carefully | the royal family, inter-house disputes, domestic noble scandal |
| Not seen at all   | common life, structural critique, labour conditions       |

The seam shows most at the Throne itself, where Prince Octavian runs Stellar Networks: professional, competent, and never quite willing to embarrass the family that owns it. Queen Lysandra is scrupulous never to be seen directing the news, tolerating Meridian's independence being cheaper than controlling it, and buying more.

## The foreign hand

Blackwell Media, a subsidiary of the Sable Cartel, has held a presence in the ecosystem since before the Banking War. Its outlets are modest and its content unremarkable. A media operation is a listening post, a channel and a lever, and the Cartel has never owned one for the advertising.

## Regulation

The Ministry of Culture regulates broadcasting and disburses patronage, keeping the apparatus aligned with the Throne without an order crude enough to notice. There is no censor's office worth the name.

## `powers/vega-throne/military.md`

# The Vega Commercial Throne: Military

Vega's forces are defensive by design. The Throne has no wish to conquer anything, garrisons subtracting from commercial competitiveness and conquest paying worse than a hit serial.

## The Royal Fleet

The monarch's own force, around three million strong: a Navy of roughly 2.2 million crewing some two hundred combat vessels for system defence, force projection and convoy escort; half a million Marines for boarding and ground capability; a quarter-million in support; and the fifty-thousand-strong Royal Guard. Squadrons are configured by task—patrol, escort, the rarely deployed strike, and the purely ceremonial, which is not a joke in a culture where being seen to be powerful is most of being powerful.

Naval service is professional and competitive, an avenue of advancement for ambitious commoners and a posting for minor nobility working the military and court hierarchies at once. The enlisted ranks draw from the commoner population and offer steady employment, technical training and modest status. The Royal Guard is a thing apart: rigorous selection, service for life, and loyalty to the Throne personally rather than to any house or institution.

## The house retinues

Noble houses keep private forces as privilege and obligation both. A house can protect its interests and parade its status, and must answer the royal summons when the Throne calls. Scale tracks rank and wealth, from an archducal house's combined-arms hundreds of thousands down to a baron's symbolic guard. They are not standardised—each house equips and trains to its own doctrine and budget—so that the Vega military is really the Royal Fleet plus several thousand private armies of wildly varying seriousness. Combined retinue strength runs to perhaps 15 million.

House Valdorian fields the largest of them: forty vessels of various classes, some 300,000 ground troops, 100,000 in security services, and 150,000 support. This long ago passed the size any entertainment business could need. The house says the forces protect production facilities, distribution routes and talent. Duke Caelum has been scrupulous, and the retinue has never been turned against royal interests in three decades, but the capability exists.

## Doctrine

Hold the home system, secure the trade routes, deter aggression by making it unprofitable, and stay out of foreign wars. The deterrence works because the sums work against an attacker: Vega's system defences are strong, war would wreck the attacker's own access to the entertainment and goods everyone consumes, and the Banking War proved Vega can absorb a blow and recover. In peacetime the Fleet's real work is escorting valuable cargo and chasing pirates.

## The Banking War

The Andromedan Banking War of 3834–3839 C was Vega's largest military experience in centuries. It began as a financial quarrel over contested Andromedan-frontier assets before escalating through Sable Cartel proxy forces into asset seizures, commerce raiding and at last fleet actions in disputed systems. It was never existential—neither side sought the other's destruction, only advantage, concessions and the demonstration of resolve—and ended in a negotiated settlement before a neutral tribunal seated at Khiva, with both sides exhausted and both claiming a victory neither had quite won.

The Fleet performed adequately rather than brilliantly, and the house retinues ranged from sharp to ornamental. Reforms since—fleet modernisation, better Fleet-retinue coordination, expanded logistics, enhanced external intelligence—continue within the limits the reconstruction debt allows.

## The military as performance

Parades and ceremonies project power, uniforms and vessels are designed to look as good as they fight, warships appear in entertainment productions, and the Guard provides a constant photogenic royal presence. Soldiers are expected to look impressive as well as be effective, and the officer corps carries heavy noble representation: younger sons seeking careers, houses demonstrating loyalty through service, and ambitious commoners climbing alongside them.

## Current posture

No significant combat is ongoing, and the Fleet trains, patrols, escorts and maintains readiness. The external threat board is benign—the Cartel a commercial rival of low military menace, the Mandate no direct threat and a vast customer, Freehold raiders a nuisance the patrols absorb—and might not stay so if the Yansieve troubles spill outward. The Throne's standing military priority is the balance among its own houses.

The aristocratic and the commercial are not two faces of the house. Status is bought with revenue and spent on the display that secures more revenue.

## `powers/vega-throne/houses/house-cassiline.md`

# House Cassiline

Archducal, specialising in luxury fashion and textiles. Around 60,000 employees and some 35 billion quid a year. For centuries Cassiline simply was Vega fashion, having invented the aesthetic vocabulary the whole culture still calls Vega style.

## The wound that will not close

The Banking War struck Cassiline disproportionately: facilities damaged in contested systems, distribution severed, inventory lost, and—worst, because hardest to rebuild—its best designers gone, some to rivals offering stability and some of them to Valdorian. Luxury consumption collapsed in wartime, the post-war austerity prolonged the fall, and brand prestige, once visibly weakened, proved far harder to restore than any factory.

Twenty-three years on, Cassiline has recovered its production capacity and not its position. The haute division, Cassiline Couture, runs at half its former output: two seasonal collections where there were four, two thousand bespoke clients where there were five thousand, two hundred designers where there were five hundred. The premium line, Silken Thread, still earns the house's bread, and now does it against Valdorian Lifestyle, which entered premium fashion during the war with scale Cassiline cannot match.

## Duchess Isadora Cassiline

Sixty-two, presenting late thirties, head of the house since 3847 C. She inherited a struggling house from a father who died without resolving the war's aftermath, and has held the line by betting everything on traditional quality: rejecting the compromises that might raise volume, defending the brand against dilution, refusing Valdorian's repeated approaches, and waiting for excellence to be rewarded as she believes it must be.

Her critics call her rigid, in love with a past the market has left behind. Her supporters call brand identity the one asset worth protecting.

Her brother and heir, Lord Lucan Cassiline, who runs the house's fashion-content arm at Phoenix Studios, wants the opposite: volume, accessibility, branded content. The siblings' disagreement about whether to chase the market or outlast it is the house's real unresolved argument, conducted in private and known to the whole industry.

## The vice

Cassiline carries roughly 200 billion quid in syndicated reconstruction loans, at fifteen billion a year to service, with payoff projected for 3890 C if nothing goes wrong. The burden constrains everything that might reverse the decline: new capacity, talent, marketing, the acquisitions a stronger house would make. The house is solvent and unable to move.

Valdorian has approached about acquisition more than once and will approach again. Cassiline's prestige would complete Valdorian Lifestyle, and Cassiline's weakness is exactly the kind of opportunity Valdorian was built to take. Acquisition would mean the end of the family's independence and the absorption or quiet discarding of an identity centuries old. Isadora has refused each time.

## Holdings and relations

The house seats at Silken Reach on the Aurelian continent, arguably the most beautiful of the great estates and an expense the house can barely meet. It keeps the Cassiline Atelier on the Manufactories, where its couture is still made by hand.

Relations are hostile with Valdorian, cooperative with Therenne, since jewellery complements fashion, and quietly sympathetic with Aurelian, the other house declining from former heights. They are strained now with the royal house itself, which after generations of dressing exclusively in Cassiline has begun to wear Valdorian designs alongside it.

## `powers/vega-throne/houses/house-kraeven.md`

# House Kraeven

Formerly archducal, specialising in finance, investment and complex instruments. Attainted in 3840 C and dissolved. Its seat, Shadowmere, stands empty on the Aurelian continent, maintained just enough not to fall down.

## The rise, and the instruments

Kraeven climbed to archducal status on financial innovation, and by 3800 C it was woven into the foundations of Vega commerce: every major house used its instruments and the royal treasury employed its managers.

Cascade Notes were layered obligations in which the returns from one investment funded the next. Temporal Arbitrage Bonds exploited the communication lag between systems. Risk Tranches sliced commercial risk into tradeable segments. They returned more than conventional investments and concentrated risk in ways their buyers never fully understood.

## The collapse

The Banking War broke the assumptions underneath them. Trade routes that were supposed to function stopped, counterparties that were supposed to pay could not, and assets that were supposed to hold value evaporated. Because Kraeven's instruments were everywhere, the failure cascaded: the most leveraged products failed, the institutions holding them sold other Kraeven products to cover the losses, the selling drove prices down, and more products failed, faster.

Within months the house's debts exceeded its assets not by percentages but by multiples. Lord Varen Kraeven—not a malicious man, only a confident one who believed his own models—tried to manage it and failed.

## The attainder

The Throne's response, one year after the war, was the first attainder of an archducal house in two centuries: rank stripped, licences revoked, assets seized for the creditors, and the family reduced to common status.

Varen Kraeven kept his life and lost everything else, tried to rebuild as a commoner, and died in 3855 C. His wife, Lady Serene, retreated to her birth house among the minor nobility. His daughter Lady Vera married into minor Mandate nobility. His son Lord Caspar is rumoured somewhere in the Freeholds; the family is not sure. Lord Dorian, the heir who would have led the house, works as a financial analyst in Aurelius City, distinguished from any other only by his name, which he explains reluctantly.

Dorian has spoken of the collapse rarely, and with a flatness that may be acceptance. "We believed our models. The models were wrong. Everything else follows from that." Asked whether he resents the Throne's response: "The attainder was correct. We failed. What else should have happened?"

## What was left behind

The post-Kraeven rules constrain financial innovation: disclosure requirements for complex instruments, monitored leverage, and enhanced Exchequer oversight. They make another such collapse less likely, and they throttle the financial sector that might otherwise have recovered. Vega once wrote paper the whole corridor traded; after Kraeven it does not.

The Throne seized Shadowmere and has pointedly never redistributed it, declining every suggestion that it be granted to a rising house. It stands empty, decaying by slow degrees, maintained at the minimum that keeps it standing.

## `powers/vega-throne/houses/house-meridian.md`

# House Meridian

Archducal, specialising in news, journalism and documentary. Around 80,000 employees and some 40 billion quid a year. Neither the largest house nor the richest; its significance is that its journalism is trusted across human space.

## The credibility investment

Founded in 3412 C—the same year as Valdorian—when the Throne ennobled a consortium of news producers who had reported reliably through a succession crisis. The house has run on one principle since: it succeeds by being trustworthy, so it protects its trustworthiness above any single story. It shields its journalists from pressure, publishes work that damages powerful interests including its own, accepts short-term losses for long-term reputation, and recruits the best regardless of origin.

The Banking War was the proof. Meridian reported Vega's defeats and casualties accurately while friendlier sources spun, and came out of the war trusted more than before, because audiences had learned they could believe it even when the news was bad.

## Lord Castor Meridian

Seventy-eight, presenting early fifties, head of the house for thirty years. He is rarely seen and rules through written instruction and trusted deputies, known through what Meridian produces rather than any public presence. "The house sells trust. If we sell it short, we have nothing."

He has never married. His heir is his niece, Lady Theodora Meridian, 52, who runs daily operations and handles the public and political work he avoids, and who plays the court game more willingly than her uncle.

## The operation

Meridian Broadcasting is the largest news operation in human space: five thousand correspondents, bureaux in every major system, and continuous coverage carried to audiences across the Mandate, the Freeholds, the Chandrasar Weave and the Drift.

Revenue comes chiefly from subscriptions—institutional, professional and individual—which frees the house from advertisers it would otherwise have to please. It answers to its subscribers, and its subscribers want only that it be right.

The Verity House Academy on the Lyra estate trains its journalists and takes outside students whose fees fund the work. A Meridian credential carries weight across the industry. Verity House itself is unusual among the great estates: gardens open to the public, archives available for research, and the whole thing less ostentatious than its peers.

## The limit

Meridian's independence is exactly bounded. It does not criticise the royal family directly, and it covers internal noble affairs with the delicacy of a house that must live among its subjects. Its clarity is reserved for the external world, where it has no stake. The Queen values that clarity even when it inconveniences her.

The harder problem is Valdorian. Meridian's news must occasionally cover the largest house in Vega, and the larger Valdorian grows, the more delicate each line of that coverage becomes: how much scrutiny is appropriate, at what point reporting becomes provocation, and what an entertainment giant with the largest private army in Vega might do to a news house that pressed too hard. Meridian has not yet been leaned on.

## `powers/vega-throne/houses/house-valdorian.md`

# House Valdorian

The dominant entertainment house, ascendant since the Banking War. Archducal rank, around 500,000 direct employees, some 150 billion quid a year in revenue, and roughly a third of all Vega entertainment production—a concentration without precedent in living memory.

## The rise

Elevated to nobility in 3412 C for early success in entertainment, the house climbed on a single repeated move: quality production, strategic acquisition, relentless expansion. Baronial to comital within a century, ducal by 3600 C, archducal after the consolidation of 3702 C.

The Banking War was the masterstroke. It damaged Valdorian but devastated its rivals, and when the shooting stopped Valdorian had carefully preserved capital while its competitors had only debt. The house bought struggling studios, the distribution networks of failing rivals, and the talent contracts bankrupt employers could no longer honour.

## Duke Caelum Valdorian

Ninety-four, presenting mid-forties, head of the house for thirty-one years since 3831 C. He is the architect of its wartime survival and post-war dominance: the man who preserved capital while rivals spent, and bought the distressed assets at the bottom.

He is not liked. Rivals find him predatory and his own people find him distant, and he has never seemed to mind either judgement. In thirty-one years he has never once moved against the Throne, and the loyalty is performed with the same cold precision as everything else he does.

The Duke has no children. His designated heir is his nephew, Lord Evander Valdorian, 67, who runs the music division: competent, not brilliant, and untested at the scale his uncle operates. Factions within the house already favour other candidates the Duke declines to discuss. His sister, Lady Virellia Valdorian, has handled the delicate work of absorbing acquired studios and the resentments that come with them, and is the more approachable of the two and the more underestimated. "Caelum sees numbers. I see people. Someone has to." Whether she wants the chair, nobody knows.

## Holdings

Radiant Productions, the flagship studio, defines prestige drama; *The Amber Throne* has filmed there for sixty-seven seasons. Cascade Entertainment runs music from development to touring under Lord Evander. Eventide chases the growing immersive sector. Valdorian Lifestyle turns entertainment into fashion-adjacent merchandise and, lately, into the premium-fashion market, where it grinds against a wounded Cassiline.

The house seats at Starhold on Lyra's Aurelian continent, an estate half residence and half showcase, built like a small fortress. It holds substantial portions of Stellavista, where its control of production facilities and the distribution hub becomes leverage over every rival who must use them.

## Relations

Meridian cooperates, news serving entertainment marketing. Cassiline is openly hostile: Valdorian took its talent during the war and now circles what is left. Therenne stays neutral, Corvane commercial, and Aurelian is dismissed as a fading thing.

When the royal succession after Caspian finally comes due, the house best placed to exploit an uncertain Throne is also the house the rest of the aristocracy would most like to see stumble.

The throne world of the Vega Commercial Throne: around 4 billion people at 0.94 gravity, in the Vega system.

The equatorial continent, where the work happens: urban centres, commercial districts, and the professional and labouring classes who serve aristocratic enterprise without belonging to it. Lyra's orbital space carries the Court's ceremonial annex, commercial and industrial platforms, defence installations, and the great production orbitals of Stellavista and the Manufactories. The rings are visible from the surface.

## `powers/vega-throne/locations/manufactories.md`

# The Manufactories

An orbital complex trailing Stellavista by forty degrees around Lyra, some 8 million strong at a standardised 0.85 G, where Vega produces what "Vega-made" signifies.

## The four zones

The Design Ring is where concepts become products and a successful designer can achieve near-celebrity: house studios, independent designers, materials libraries, prototyping.

The Artisan Quarter is where traditional craft persists, in workshops producing bespoke work by hand, because scarcity is the luxury and the labour itself is what the customer pays for.

The Production Halls run scalable manufacturing—automated and semi-automated lines turning out licensed goods that are still better than the alternatives, just not unique.

The Showrooms are where products meet buyers in curated, flattering spaces, the selling begun before a price is named.

## Authenticity

Authenticity is policed by embedded markers, database registration and a certification regime pursued with near-religious ferocity. A counterfeit that passes does not steal a sale, it dissolves the meaning the entire sector floats on, which is why the houses cooperate instantly and absolutely on brand protection.

## The two kinds of worker

The artisan is trained for years, narrowly specialised, and produces limited work that he signs, so that individual identity attaches to the product and genuine prestige to the person. A master craftsman may not be rich by aristocratic standards, and he can refuse a commission, which is how everyone knows he is somebody.

The production worker is adequately paid, regularly scheduled and largely interchangeable, with little prospect of advancement, making luxuries he cannot afford for customers he will never meet. The work is not harsh and it is not fulfilling either.

The guilds—Tailors', Jewellers', Furniture, Leatherworkers'—set training standards, certify credentials, and preserve craft knowledge individual houses might let lapse. Their authority stops where the houses' control of production begins.

## Places

The Cassiline Atelier is where Vega fashion still originates, collections debuting here before they reach the markets that will imitate them. The Gilded Vault holds House Therenne's reserves of gold, platinum and gemstones. The Certification Hall is the gate between production and market. The Archive preserves centuries of design for the designers who mine the past to make the present.

## People

Master Artisan Kell Soren, a jeweller of sixty years' reputation, has settings collectors seek by name, and has declined noble commissions that failed to interest him. "The hand learns what the mind cannot teach. You must do the work ten thousand times before you understand it."

Designer Lyric Vane, five years out of the academy and signed to Cassiline, is a nostalgic modernist by the critics' label she dislikes and accepts for its marketing value. "They love me now. They'll love someone else later. What matters is the work while I can do it."

Quality Director Tam Essien, senior official of the Certification Hall, has rejected products from every great house, and his judgement is final: a commoner with an absolute veto over the work of nobles, tolerated because the system's honesty depends on someone who cannot be charmed. "If I approved substandard work, the system collapses. Everyone knows this. They still resent me for it." He buys little, owns less, and lives in a small apartment far from the luxury he guards.

# The Radiant Court

The royal palace complex on Lyra's Aurelian continent: some fifty square kilometres of buildings inside five hundred of grounds, holding around 200,000 staff, residents and permanent visitors.

## The complex

At its heart is the Central Palace—the Throne Hall for formal audiences, the Great Gallery, the Council Chamber, the royal apartments—old at its core and layered with centuries of additions, each expressing its era's taste while pretending continuity with the last.

Around it sit the administrative districts holding the Ministries, the Courts, the Archives and the Exchequers; the Noble Quarters, where visiting aristocrats are housed in accommodation graded precisely to rank, an archduke's townhouse a small palace and a baron's suite merely comfortable; and the Commercial Exchanges, where business meets government because proximity to the Throne is itself a tradeable asset.

## Precedence

Attendance at the great court occasions is not optional. Each rank owes a calibrated minimum of presence, and absence without an excuse accepted by the Chamberlain's office reads as disloyalty.

What governs every interaction is precedence—who stands where, enters when, speaks in what order—determined by rank, then seniority, then current commercial standing, then royal favour. The precedence lists are updated quarterly, so a house rising in the market is placed visibly ahead of an older house in decline, and the court reads the seating like a stock ticker.

## The two hundred thousand

| Commercial services | ~80,000 | exchanges, banking, retail |
| Civil servants      | ~50,000 | government functions       |
| Maintenance         | ~30,000 | physical plant and grounds |
| Security            | ~20,000 | guard presence             |
| Ceremonial staff    | ~10,000 | event production, protocol |
| Household servants  | ~5,000  | direct royal service       |

Court service carries status—"served at the Court" opens doors elsewhere—and it is service. Petitioners who come seeking royal attention wait in designated areas, submit through proper channels, and may wait weeks or receive no answer at all.

## Reaching the Queen

Queen Lysandra IV is not constantly visible. She appears at the functions that require her and the occasions she chooses, and her absence is noted as sharply as her presence is prized. Reaching her means passing through layers, each with its gatekeeper: court presence through the Chamberlain's office, noble audience through the Lord Chamberlain, private audience through the Queen's Secretary, informal contact through the royal family itself.

Duke Vasilis Therenne has been Lord Chamberlain for nineteen years: meticulous, discreet, feared, loyal to the Throne rather than to any house, and careful to keep his own house's benefit from his position within tolerable bounds.

Lady Celeste Aurum, the Queen's Secretary, controls the royal schedule. She rose from modest minor nobility on civil-service competence and is loyal to Lysandra personally. What reaches the Queen's attention, Aurum decides.

Sir Marcus Varn, Captain of the Guard, and Countess Elara Silven, Mistress of the Robes, complete the inner ring, controlling the body and the image of the sovereign.

## Notable spaces

The Throne Hall is built to overwhelm—vaulted, vast, the throne raised so that supplicants must look up—and stages the receptions, ennoblings and major announcements. The Mirror Gallery runs four hundred metres of accumulated masterworks. The Star Gardens, planted with species that glow after dark, host the prestige evening functions. And the Commercial Floor of the Exchanges hums on through all of it: when the Ascension Ball turns in the Central Palace, the trades still clear next door.

## `powers/vega-throne/locations/stellavista.md`

# Stellavista

The entertainment orbital above Lyra: some 12 million people across a complex of studios, residences and distribution infrastructure, at gravities from 0.3 to 1.0 G depending on the section.

## The machine

Concentric Production Rings house the major studios at varying gravities—the Outer Ring for Radiant's prestige drama and Eventide's immersive work, the Middle for Cascade's music and the smaller houses, the Inner for royal Starfire and the technical facilities.

The Talent Quarter is where performers live, in residences graded from a star's luxury to an aspirant's shared dormitory. Living there is privilege and constraint at once, close to the work and close to the observation.

The Processing Centres finish nearly all Vega content—editing, effects, sound, quality control—and the Distribution Hub is where it leaves for human space. The Hub is the chokepoint, and House Valdorian's grip on it, along with its studios, is what makes the house's dominance more than a matter of market share.

## The hierarchy

| Stars                 | luxury residences, personal staff, every comfort |
| Established talent    | good housing; choice of work                     |
| Working professionals | adequate housing; steady employment              |
| Aspirants             | shared quarters; ceaseless competition           |
| Support staff         | variable; the crews who build it all             |

The hierarchy is not abstract here. Different levels eat in different cafeterias, residential areas are separated, and social mixing is limited and noticed.

Young hopefuls arrive constantly, from across Vega space and beyond, carrying training and talent and savings into a competition almost all of them will lose. Housing is dear, work is scarce, the savings drain, and the years pass without a breakthrough. The predictable costs—substance use, mental-health collapse, the exploitation desperate people accept—are known and not prioritised.

## People

Vex Calder is a former star two decades past his fame, taking supporting parts and teaching master classes, managing a smaller apartment with care and no visible bitterness. "The industry takes what it needs and discards the rest. I gave good years; I received good payment. Complaining would be unseemly."

Liora Thrace, an aspirant from Deneb Reach three years in, has nearly exhausted her savings, shares a dormitory with three others, and is still auditioning. "Ask me again next year. Either I'll have broken through, or I'll have gone home. Either way, I'll know."

Director Drusa Verro is one of Radiant's senior directors, with thirty years of prestige productions, a recognisable style, no interest in celebrity, and modest habits by Stellavista standards. She reinvests her earnings into work she believes in. "The work is the point. The rest is noise."

## Where they meet

The Promenade is the public entertainment district and the station's real marketplace of careers, where opportunities surface in conversation as often as through any formal process. Studio 7, where *The Amber Throne* has filmed for sixty-seven seasons, is an unremarkable room made sacred by association and included reverently on the tours. The Audition Halls process thousands of hopefuls a day through a liturgy of waiting, brief performance and vague response. The Observation Deck, with its view of Lyra turning below, is where industry people go to talk without being overheard.
